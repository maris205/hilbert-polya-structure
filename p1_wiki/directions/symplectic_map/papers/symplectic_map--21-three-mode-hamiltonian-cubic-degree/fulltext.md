# Introduction {#sec:introduction}

Polynomial automorphisms can hide their degree-growth mechanisms inside a composition word. In a product-like setting, the degree of a composition may reflect only the visible degrees of the factors. In a genuinely coupled setting, however, the selected monomials in one step can alter the winner in the next step, and the complete degree recurrence may only appear after two phases. This paper isolates one such coupled but still fully explicit family.

We work over an algebraically closed field $K$ of characteristic zero and study the map $F_g=T\circ S$ on $\mathbb{A}^6$, where $q=(q_1,q_2,q_3)$ and $p=(p_1,p_2,p_3)$. The displayed potentials are $$V(q)=q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2+q_1^g,\qquad
W(p)=p_1^2p_2^2p_3^2+p_3^g.$$ They are asymmetric in exactly one pure-power row each. The asymmetry is deliberate: the first shear should push weight into the $p$-coordinates, and the second shear should pull that weight back into the $q$-coordinates.

The exact theorem is the following.

[\[thm:main\]]{#thm:main label="thm:main"} Let $K$ be algebraically closed of characteristic zero and let $g\geq 8$ be an integer. Define $$S(q,p)=(q,p+\nabla V(q)),\qquad
T(q,p)=(q+\nabla W(p),p),\qquad
F_g=T\circ S.$$ Then:

1.  $S$ and $T$ are polynomial automorphisms with subtraction inverses, and $F_g$ is symplectic for the standard form on $\mathbb{A}^6$.

2.  The selected phase matrices are $$A_g=\begin{pmatrix}g-1&0&0\\2&1&2\\2&2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
      B_g=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\2&1&2\\0&0&g-1\end{pmatrix},$$ and the complete-step matrix is $C_g=B_gA_g$.

3.  On the strict cone $$\mathcal{K}_g=\{u\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3:\;x=u_2/u_1\geq 1,\ y=u_3/u_1\geq 1,\ x+y<(g-3)/2\},$$ there are exactly two selector gaps, $$M_S=(g-2)-2x-2y,\qquad
      M_T=(2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6,$$ and no third face occurs.

4.  The cone is $C_g$-invariant, the phase/carry induction gives $v_{n+1}=A_gu_n$ and $u_{n+1}=C_gu_n$, and positive coefficients in characteristic zero prevent cancellation of the selected support.

5.  The third $q$-coordinate is visible and maximal, so $$\operatorname{deg}(F_g^n)=e_3^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\mathbf{1} \quad (n\geq 0).$$ Consequently $\lambda_1(F_g)=\rho(C_g)$, and $\rho(C_g)<(g-1)^2$.

6.  The characteristic polynomial is $$\chi_{C_g}(t)=t^3-(2g+11)t^2+(g^2-16g+19)t-9(g-1)^2,$$ and for $g\equiv 2,3,4 \pmod 5$ the mod-five reduction is irreducible, yielding infinite cubic Perron subfamilies.

7.  The case $g=7$ is a strict-cone boundary with no selector tie. No genericity, entropy, universal non-conjugacy, priority, or CAS-based proof certificate is claimed.

The theorem is intentionally linear. It does not branch into an alternative support analysis, a half-step degree observable, or a numerical side computation. The proof first certifies that the two displayed shears are canonical polynomial automorphisms, then freezes the complete support ledger, then proves that exactly two selector margins govern every iterate, and only after those steps turns to the exact degree row and the arithmetic corollary. This order is not stylistic. If the support rows were allowed to drift before the selector analysis, or if the degree observable were guessed before the visibility proof, the statement would no longer be auditable from the displayed family alone.

What makes the family genuinely coupled is that neither phase carries the final degree observable by itself. The first shear creates a $p$-degree vector whose dominant row is only an intermediate support signal, and the second shear then reads that signal through a different support ledger before returning to the $q$-coordinates. If one stopped after the first phase, the matrix $A_g$ would describe only a transient support transfer. If one jumped directly to a guessed scalar recurrence, the proof would lose the mechanism that certifies which support row actually survives the iteration. The chain $$u_n \xrightarrow{\;S\;} v_{n+1} \xrightarrow{\;T\;} u_{n+1}$$ is therefore not a stylistic decomposition; it is the minimal bookkeeping that keeps the support selection, the old-term dominance, and the final degree observable logically separate.

This also explains why the full-step matrix $C_g=B_gA_g$ enters only after the selector proof. The entries of $A_g$ and $B_g$ are read row by row from the displayed supports, whereas the cone invariance, the visibility argument, and the Perron root belong to the composed matrix $C_g$. The proof would be circular if it invoked Perron positivity before establishing that the same two selector faces are chosen at every iterate. The manuscript therefore first proves that the selected support is stable and only then uses the positivity of $C_g$ to turn that stable support into an exact recurrence.

# Bounded related work and scope boundary {#sec:related}

#### Affine-triangular terminology.

The larger affine-triangular comparison class is a convenient language for degree-growth problems, but it is not the class studied here. Our maps are canonical gradient shears with symmetric Hessian blocks, and the distinction matters because the symplectic proof is tied to that exact structure [@BlancVanSanten2019]. More concretely, the present argument needs the Jacobian blocks to be lower- and upper-unitriangular with Hessian entries in the off-diagonal blocks. That feature is automatic for gradient shears and is what makes the six-by-six symplectic identity a formal calculation. We cite this source only as a terminology boundary; the theorem does not transfer from it, and no result from that paper is imported as a substitute for the support analysis below.

The distinction also matters at the level of proof architecture. In a general affine-triangular setting, one often proves degree growth by tracking a family of possible leading terms and then showing that one family eventually dominates the rest. Here the argument is sharper: the support family is fixed in advance, the cone is two-dimensional after normalization, and the only degree choices are the two selector gaps $M_S$ and $M_T$. That is why the paper does not need the broader machinery usually associated with affine-triangular dynamics. The proof is smaller in scope but more explicit in structure.

#### Four-dimensional neighboring context.

The recent dimension-four affine-triangular algebraic-degree context does not address this specific degree problem [@ShaoSun2025]. It is useful here only as a dimension boundary for terminology: in the present paper the degree transfer is governed by a $3\times 3$ matrix, the cone has a two-parameter normalization $(x,y)$, and the characteristic polynomial is genuinely cubic. Those three features separate the current family from that four-dimensional context. The citation is therefore contextual rather than foundational; it supplies no symplectic, selector, recurrence, or proof input for the theorem below.

There is also a conceptual difference in what the polynomial data are doing. The neighboring four-dimensional context studies a different algebraic-degree pattern in a different ambient space. Here the three-mode family is chosen so that a single pure-power row is balanced against a mixed sextic row in each phase. That balance is what creates the exact $A_g$, $B_g$, and $C_g$ chain, and it is what makes the cubic Perron corollary possible after the mod-five audit. The paper is therefore not a dimension lift of the neighboring result; it is a different exact family with a different proof spine.

#### Bounded novelty wording.

The novelty wording is intentionally restrained. The present manuscript makes no global priority claim and no exhaustive classification claim. What matters for the proof is the exact support pattern written in the displayed family: three $q$-modes, three $p$-modes, one mixed sextic monomial in each potential, and one pure-power row in opposite phases. If a future comparison refined the surrounding novelty language, the theorem below would remain the same because it depends only on the explicit gradients, matrices, cone inequalities, and residue checks written in the main body.

This bounded wording is not evasive; it is part of the proof discipline. It prevents several false enlargements at once. The present paper is not a residue-theoretic paper, not a moduli or ramification paper, not a torus-escape paper, and not a generic dimension-lift theorem. It is one exact six-variable Hamiltonian family with one exact recurrence.

The same restraint governs the shape of the related-work section itself. A context citation is useful here only if it marks a boundary of the claim: what comparison class is being excluded, or what neighboring lower-dimensional phenomenon serves merely as orientation. Once those two tasks are done, additional citation accumulation would not prove a single selector inequality, would not change the support ledger, and would not strengthen the Perron corollary. In a proof-first manuscript, the relevant question is therefore not how broad a survey can be attached to the theorem, but which exact enlargements of the theorem must be ruled out before the symbolic argument begins.

The hard anti-claims can therefore be stated before the proof begins:

-   no genericity statement for arbitrary Hamiltonian shears, arbitrary dimensions, arbitrary support words, or positive characteristic;

-   no entropy, periodic-point, trace, torus, conjugacy, or classification theorem;

-   no absolute priority, firstness, or optimality claim; and

-   no empirical, numerical, or CAS-produced certificate.

# Exact family, gradients, inverses, symplectic blocks, and support ledger {#sec:setup}

## The maps and their inverses {#sec:maps}

We write the phase variables as $(q,p)\in K^3\times K^3$. The two canonical shears are $$S(q,p)=(q,p+\nabla V(q)),\qquad
T(q,p)=(q+\nabla W(p),p).$$ The family is deliberately asymmetric. The pure power $q_1^g$ appears only in the first potential, while $p_3^g$ appears only in the second. This placement means that the potentially dominant pure row of the first shear is in the $q_1$-slot, whereas the potentially dominant pure row of the second shear is in the $p_3$-slot. The degree mechanism is therefore not visible inside a single triangular map; it only appears after the support created by the first shear is fed into the second. Their explicit gradients are $$\begin{aligned}
\nabla V
&=
\bigl(2q_1q_2^2q_3^2+gq_1^{g-1},\;2q_1^2q_2q_3^2,\;2q_1^2q_2^2q_3\bigr),\\
\nabla W
&=
\bigl(2p_1p_2^2p_3^2,\;2p_1^2p_2p_3^2,\;2p_1^2p_2^2p_3+gp_3^{g-1}\bigr).\end{aligned}$$ The corresponding Hessian matrices, which will be used only through their symmetry, are $$H_V=
\begin{pmatrix}
2q_2^2q_3^2+g(g-1)q_1^{g-2} & 4q_1q_2q_3^2 & 4q_1q_2^2q_3\\
4q_1q_2q_3^2 & 2q_1^2q_3^2 & 4q_1^2q_2q_3\\
4q_1q_2^2q_3 & 4q_1^2q_2q_3 & 2q_1^2q_2^2
\end{pmatrix},$$ $$H_W=
\begin{pmatrix}
2P_2^2P_3^2 & 4P_1P_2P_3^2 & 4P_1P_2^2P_3\\
4P_1P_2P_3^2 & 2P_1^2P_3^2 & 4P_1^2P_2P_3\\
4P_1P_2^2P_3 & 4P_1^2P_2P_3 & 2P_1^2P_2^2+g(g-1)P_3^{g-2}
\end{pmatrix},$$ where $P=(P_1,P_2,P_3)=p+\nabla V(q)$ is the intermediate $p$-vector. Displaying these Hessians makes the block computation completely transparent: the only reason the symplectic identity works is that the off-diagonal blocks come from symmetric second-derivative matrices. The displayed formulas also explain why the later degree proof has exactly two competitive rows and not more. In $H_V$, every off-diagonal entry comes from the mixed sextic $q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2$, while only the $(1,1)$-entry carries the additional pure-power contribution $g(g-1)q_1^{g-2}$. In $H_W$, the same asymmetry is mirrored in the opposite corner: the mixed sextic fills the matrix, and only the $(3,3)$-entry receives the extra term $g(g-1)P_3^{g-2}$. Thus the first-derivative competition between one mixed row and one pure row is already encoded at the second-derivative level. The block symplectic calculation and the later selector calculation are therefore different views of the same structural asymmetry, not unrelated steps that happen to use the same polynomials. The inverses are the subtraction inverses $$S^{-1}(q,p)=(q,p-\nabla V(q)),\qquad
T^{-1}(q,p)=(q-\nabla W(p),p).$$ No denominator, birational correction, or analytic continuation appears. Thus every later degree calculation takes place in the polynomial category from start to finish. In particular, there is no hidden birational simplification that could alter the support rows or insert exceptional cancellation phenomena.

[\[lem:canonical\]]{#lem:canonical label="lem:canonical"} The maps $S$ and $T$ are polynomial automorphisms of $\mathbb{A}^6$, and $F_g=T\circ S$ preserves the standard symplectic form.

The triangular inverses follow immediately from the explicit formulas above. Write the Jacobians in block form using the Hessians $H_V=\nabla^2V(q)$ and $H_W=\nabla^2W(P)$, where $P=p+\nabla V(q)$ is the intermediate $p$-coordinate after applying $S$: $$J_S=\begin{pmatrix}I_3&0\\H_V&I_3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
J_T=\begin{pmatrix}I_3&H_W\\0&I_3\end{pmatrix}.$$ Both Hessians are symmetric because they are Hessians of scalar polynomials. This symmetry is the only structural input needed for the symplectic check. With the standard symplectic matrix $\Omega=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I_3\\-I_3&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, one first computes $$\Omega J_S=
\begin{pmatrix}
H_V&I_3\\
-I_3&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Omega J_T=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&I_3\\
-I_3&-H_W
\end{pmatrix}.$$ Multiplying on the left by the transposes gives $$J_S^{\mathsf T}\Omega J_S
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&H_V^{\mathsf T}\\
0&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
H_V&I_3\\
-I_3&0
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
H_V-H_V^{\mathsf T}&I_3\\
-I_3&0
\end{pmatrix}
=\Omega$$ and similarly $$J_T^{\mathsf T}\Omega J_T
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&0\\
H_W^{\mathsf T}&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
0&I_3\\
-I_3&-H_W
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&I_3\\
-I_3&H_W^{\mathsf T}-H_W
\end{pmatrix}
=\Omega.$$ Hence $J_{F_g}=J_TJ_S$ is symplectic. The proof is independent of the later cone and selector arguments: canonicality is already forced by the gradient form of the two shears. $$J_{F_g}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&H_W\\
0&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&0\\
H_V&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3+H_WH_V&H_W\\
H_V&I_3
\end{pmatrix}.$$ The product shows at a glance that the full map remains polynomial and that the upper-right block is exactly the Hessian block governing the second shear. It also gives $\det J_{F_g}=1$, the determinant shadow of symplecticity.

Reading Table [5](#tab:scores){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:scores"} row by row clarifies why the mixed rows are not merely lower-order decoration. In the second and third $S$-rows the mixed monomials are the only monomials available, so they define the transfer matrix just as decisively as the pure $q_1^{g-1}$ row. The same is true for the first two $T$-rows. The selector analysis is therefore not saying that mixed terms disappear; it is saying something narrower and more informative, namely that among the six displayed rows only two rows contain a genuine choice, and those two choices can be audited by explicit inequalities.

The full-step matrix $C_g$ is not inserted by fiat. It is the literal product of the two phase matrices: $$B_gA_g=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&2\\
2&1&2\\
0&0&g-1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
g-1&0&0\\
2&1&2\\

\end{pmatrix}
=

2g+4&5&4\\

\end{pmatrix}.$$ Thus the first row of $C_g$ is the sum of the first row of $A_g$ plus twice the second and third rows of $A_g$; the second and third rows are obtained in the same way. This explicit multiplication matters because later inequalities are proved for $C_g$ itself, not for an abstract matrix with the same characteristic polynomial. Table [5](#tab:scores){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:scores"} makes one bookkeeping point explicit. The $S$-phase is evaluated against the $u$-weights, while the $T$-phase is evaluated against the intermediate weights $v=A_gu$. The notation prevents a common error in two-phase degree arguments, namely using the same weight vector on both sides of the phase boundary. Here the boundary is real: the second phase sees the support created by the first, not the original $u$-weights directly.

[\[lem:matrix-read\]]{#lem:matrix-read label="lem:matrix-read"} The six support rows in Table [4](#tab:support){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:support"} determine the displayed matrices $A_g$ and $B_g$ uniquely.

The $S$-phase reads the support rows of $\nabla V$. In the $\partial_{q_1}V$ row the candidates are the mixed exponent $(1,2,2)$ and the pure exponent $(g-1,0,0)$; the selector analysis in the next section decides which one dominates. In the $\partial_{q_2}V$ and $\partial_{q_3}V$ rows there is no choice at all, because each row has a single mixed monomial. This yields the three rows of $A_g$: $$(g-1,0,0),\qquad (2,1,2),\qquad (2,2,1).$$ The $T$-phase reads the support rows of $\nabla W$. The $\partial_{p_1}W$ and $\partial_{p_2}W$ rows again have single mixed monomials, while the $\partial_{p_3}W$ row has a mixed candidate $(2,2,1)$ and a pure candidate $(0,0,g-1)$. These are exactly the rows of $B_g$. No additional support row exists, so no other matrix can arise from the displayed family.

-   The first $S$-row is the only row with a genuine pure-vs-mixed choice. It is the only place where a selector gap can appear before the cone is imposed.

-   The second and third $S$-rows are rigid: their mixed monomials fix the second and third rows of $A_g$ with no ambiguity.

-   The first and second $T$-rows are equally rigid and therefore do not contribute selector faces.

-   The third $T$-row is the only second-phase row that can compete with a pure term; that competition produces $M_T$.

-   Because every row is accounted for explicitly, the support ledger is exhaustive rather than schematic.

This row-by-row reading is what makes the later degree induction honest: the matrices are not guessed, and no hidden monomial family is being suppressed.

[\[lem:matrix-positive\]]{#lem:matrix-positive label="lem:matrix-positive"} For $g\geq 8$, the matrix $C_g$ is entrywise positive, and both $A_g$ and $B_g$ have nonnegative entries with positive row sums.

The entries of $A_g$ and $B_g$ are visibly nonnegative from their definitions. Their row sums are $(g-1), 5, 5$ and $5, 5, g-1$ respectively, so every row is nonzero. The product matrix $C_g$ has entries $g+7,6,6,2g+4,5,4,2(g-1),2(g-1),g-1$, all of which are strictly positive for $g\geq 8$. This is the positivity input used later in the cone and Perron arguments.

[\[def:weight\]]{#def:weight label="def:weight"} For a positive vector $u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3$ and an exponent vector $\alpha=(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)\in\mathbb N^3$, define the weighted score $$\operatorname{wt}_u(\alpha)=\alpha_1u_1+\alpha_2u_2+\alpha_3u_3.$$ Within any support row, the selected row is the monomial whose exponent vector has maximal weighted score.

This definition is the bridge from the support ledger to the matrix recurrence. Because multiplying $u$ by a positive scalar multiplies every score by the same scalar, only the ratios $u_2/u_1$ and $u_3/u_1$ matter for selection. That scale-invariance is exactly why the selector problem can be reduced to the two normalized variables $x$ and $y$. In the competitive $S$-row one obtains $$\operatorname{wt}_u(g-1,0,0)-\operatorname{wt}_u(1,2,2)=u_1\bigl((g-2)-2x-2y\bigr)=u_1M_S$$ whereas the competitive $T$-row must be evaluated at the intermediate weight $v=A_gu$, giving $$\operatorname{wt}_v(0,0,g-1)-\operatorname{wt}_v(2,2,1)
=u_1\bigl((2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6\bigr)=u_1M_T.$$ Thus $M_S$ and $M_T$ are not ad hoc inequalities; they are precisely the normalized score gaps of the only two competitive support rows, with the phase change made explicit in the second comparison.

[\[lem:explicit-gaps\]]{#lem:explicit-gaps label="lem:explicit-gaps"} Let $u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3$ and write $x=u_2/u_1$, $y=u_3/u_1$. Then the $S$- and $T$-selector gaps arise by direct score subtraction from the two competitive rows and from no others.

In the competitive $S$-row one compares the mixed exponent $(1,2,2)$ with the pure exponent $(g-1,0,0)$. Their scores are $$\operatorname{wt}_u(1,2,2)=u_1+2u_2+2u_3=u_1(1+2x+2y)$$ and $$\operatorname{wt}_u(g-1,0,0)=(g-1)u_1.$$ Subtracting gives $$\operatorname{wt}_u(g-1,0,0)-\operatorname{wt}_u(1,2,2)=u_1\bigl((g-2)-2x-2y\bigr)=u_1M_S.$$ For the competitive $T$-row one must first pass through the selected $S$-phase vector $v=A_gu$, so $$v_1=(g-1)u_1,\qquad v_2=(2+x+2y)u_1,\qquad v_3=(2+2x+y)u_1.$$ The mixed $T$-candidate $(2,2,1)$ has score $$2v_1+2v_2+v_3=u_1\bigl(2g+4+4x+5y\bigr),$$ while the pure $T$-candidate $(0,0,g-1)$ has score $$(g-1)v_3=u_1\bigl((2g-2)+(2g-2)x+(g-1)y\bigr).$$ Subtracting yields $$(g-1)v_3-(2v_1+2v_2+v_3)
=u_1\bigl((2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6\bigr)
=u_1M_T.$$ All other support rows are singletons, so they admit no nontrivial selector gap.

The second-phase computation is worth isolating conceptually. The formula for $M_T$ is not read directly from the $p$-support row in isolation; it is read from that row after the first-phase selection has already replaced the original weights $u$ by the intermediate weights $v=A_gu$. This is the first place where the two-phase character of the family becomes unavoidable. The asymmetry between $M_S$ and $M_T$ is therefore not an accident of notation. It records the exact point at which the first selector influences the second.

# Selector gaps and cone invariance {#sec:selectors}

## Two and only two selector gaps {#sec:two-gaps}

Let $u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)^{\mathsf T}$ be a positive weight vector and set $x=u_2/u_1$, $y=u_3/u_1$. The comparison of the pure row $q_1^g$ with the mixed row $q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2$ gives $$M_S=(g-2)-2x-2y.$$ The comparison of the pure row $p_3^g$ with the mixed row $p_1^2p_2^2p_3$ gives $$M_T=(2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6.$$ The normalization by $u_1$ is chosen so that the selector problem depends on ratios rather than on the absolute scale of the degree vector. Once $u_1>0$ is factored out, the mixed support comparison becomes a planar question in the $(x,y)$-variables. The lower bounds $x,y\geq 1$ encode the fact that the second and third coordinates should not fall below the first, while the upper bound $x+y<(g-3)/2$ is exactly the margin needed for the pure $q_1^g$ term to keep beating the mixed $q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2$ term. This is why the cone is written in normalized coordinates rather than as a homogeneous region in $\mathbb R_{>0}^3$.

The normalized cone also keeps the selector problem separate from the growth problem. The signs of $M_S$ and $M_T$ decide which support rows win inside the individual shears. By contrast, the positivity of $C_g$ and the later Perron argument concern what happens only after those winners have already been frozen. Working in $(x,y)$-coordinates prevents a logical shortcut: a face is selected because its normalized score is larger, not because the eventual full-step matrix turns out to have positive entries.

[\[lem:seed\]]{#lem:seed label="lem:seed"} The seed $u_0=\mathbf{1}$ belongs to $\mathcal{K}_g$ for every $g\geq 8$. For $g=7$, the same seed lies on the boundary $x+y=(g-3)/2$.

For $u_0=\mathbf{1}$ one has $x=y=1$. Thus $x,y\geq 1$ always. If $g\geq 8$, then $$x+y=2<\frac{g-3}{2},$$ so $u_0\in\mathcal{K}_g$. If $g=7$, then $x+y=2=(g-3)/2$, so the seed lies on the boundary rather than in the open cone.

The ledger in Table [6](#tab:selector){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:selector"} separates two different tasks. The quantities $M_S$ and $M_T$ decide which support row wins inside a fixed shear. The quantities $X'-1$, $Y'-1$, and $H_2$ belong to the full-step matrix and decide whether the normalized cone is preserved from one iterate to the next. Keeping those tasks separate prevents a common source of drift, namely using a full-step estimate to justify a half-step selector choice.

This separation also explains why the proof needs exactly two selector gaps and no more. A third gap would require a third row with two competing monomials, but the support ledger has only two such rows. All other rows are rigid, so they can change the value of a selected degree vector only after the selected support has already been decided. In other words, the phase structure is not an artifact of the proof; it is already present in the support ledger itself.

[\[lem:twofaces\]]{#lem:twofaces label="lem:twofaces"} For the displayed family there are exactly two nontrivial selector comparisons: $M_S$ in the $S$-phase and $M_T$ in the $T$-phase. No third selector face occurs.

By Table [4](#tab:support){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:support"}, the $S$-phase has three rows to inspect. The $\partial_{q_2}V$ and $\partial_{q_3}V$ rows each contain a single monomial, so they cannot produce a selector boundary. Only the $\partial_{q_1}V$ row contains both a mixed and a pure candidate, and their weight difference is $M_S$. The same reasoning applies to the $T$-phase: the $\partial_{p_1}W$ and $\partial_{p_2}W$ rows are single-support rows, while $\partial_{p_3}W$ contains the unique pure-versus-mixed choice with gap $M_T$. Since there are no further support rows, there is no third selector face.

[\[lem:selector\]]{#lem:selector label="lem:selector"} For $g\geq 8$ and $(x,y)$ in the cone $\mathcal{K}_g$, both $M_S$ and $M_T$ are strictly positive.

On $\mathcal{K}_g$, the height condition $x+y<(g-3)/2$ implies $$M_S=(g-2)-2x-2y>(g-2)-2\cdot \frac{g-3}{2}=1.$$ Since $x,y\geq 1$, $$M_T=(2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6\geq (2g-6)+(g-6)-6=3g-18\geq 6.$$ Hence both gaps are strict. The case $g=7$ is not inside the open cone and is recorded separately as a boundary note.

## Cone invariance {#sec:cone}

Let $U'=C_gU$. With $x=U_2/U_1$, $y=U_3/U_1$, and $D=g+7+6x+6y$, direct substitution yields $$U'_1=DU_1,\qquad
U'_2=\bigl(2g+4+5x+4y\bigr)U_1,\qquad
U'_3=\bigl(2(g-1)+2(g-1)x+(g-1)y\bigr)U_1.$$ Hence the normalized coordinates $X'=U'_2/U'_1$ and $Y'=U'_3/U'_1$ satisfy $$X'-1=\frac{g-3-x-2y}{D},\qquad
Y'-1=\frac{g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y}{D}.$$

[\[lem:slack\]]{#lem:slack label="lem:slack"} Put $$h=g-3-2x-2y.$$ On $\mathcal{K}_g$, one has $h>0$, and the first selector margin and the first normalized cone margin admit the exact rewritings $$M_S=1+h,
\qquad
X'-1=\frac{x+h}{D}.$$ Moreover, $$M_T\geq 3g-18\geq 6,
\qquad
g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y\geq 4g-24\geq 8.$$ Thus both selector decisions and both normalized lower faces are strict before the upper-height face is considered.

The definition of the open cone is precisely $h>0$ together with $x,y\geq1$. Substitution gives $$M_S=(g-2)-2x-2y=1+h$$ and $$g-3-x-2y=x+h,$$ which proves the two identities. For the second selector, the coefficients of $x$ and $y$ are positive when $g\geq8$, so their minimum on the lower faces occurs at $x=y=1$: $$M_T\geq(2g-6)+(g-6)-6=3g-18.$$ The numerator of $Y'-1$ is treated in the same way: $$g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y
\geq g-9+(2g-8)+(g-7)=4g-24.$$ The final numerical lower bounds follow from $g\geq8$. Notice that this is an exact facial calculation, not a compactness argument: the strictness of the first two target faces is visible directly in the four displayed margins.

The first two cone inequalities are therefore reduced to sign checks on two explicit numerators. The third cone inequality is encoded by the height functional $H=R U'_1-U'_2-U'_3$ with $R=(g-3)/2$, or equivalently by $H_2=2H=(g-3)U'_1-2U'_2-2U'_3$. These three tests are sufficient because the cone has exactly three defining inequalities after normalization: $x\geq 1$, $y\geq 1$, and $x+y<R$. Thus $X'-1$ certifies the first lower bound, $Y'-1$ certifies the second, and $H_2>0$ certifies the upper-height bound. No extra hidden face remains to be checked once these three signs are positive. To expose every coefficient in the remaining test, substitute the three rows of $C_g$ before collecting terms: $$\begin{aligned}
\frac{H_2}{U_1}
&=(g-3)(g+7+6x+6y)\\
&\quad-2(2g+4+5x+4y)
      -2\bigl(2(g-1)+2(g-1)x+(g-1)y\bigr)\\
&=g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)x+4(g-6)y.\end{aligned}$$ Consequently $$H_2=(g-3)U'_1-2U'_2-2U'_3=U_1Q,$$ where $$Q=g^2+2gx+4gy-4g-24x-24y-25,
\qquad
H=\frac{H_2}{2}=R U'_1-U'_2-U'_3.$$ The lower-height parameter is $R=(g-3)/2$. The split $8\leq g\leq 11$ versus $g\geq 12$ is the only place where the inequality needs a case distinction. For the smaller range the coefficient of $x$ in $Q$ is negative, so one uses the cone inequality $x<(g-3)/2-y$ to eliminate $x$: $$\begin{aligned}
Q
&=g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)x+4(g-6)y\\
&>g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)\Bigl(\frac{g-3}{2}-y\Bigr)+4(g-6)y\\
&=2g^2-19g+11+2gy\\
&\geq 2g^2-17g+11.\end{aligned}$$ This yields $$Q>2g^2-17g+11\geq 3 \qquad (8\leq g\leq 11),$$ because the last comparison has the exact factorization $$2g^2-17g+11=3+(g-8)(2g-1).$$ For the larger range all coefficients of $x$ and $y$ are nonnegative, so the trivial lower bounds $x,y\geq 1$ are enough: $$\begin{aligned}
Q
&=g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)x+4(g-6)y\\
&\geq g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)+4(g-6)\\
&=g^2+2g-73.\end{aligned}$$ Thus $$Q\geq g^2+2g-73>0 \qquad (g\geq 12).$$ Here the lower endpoint is also explicit: $$g^2+2g-73=95+(g-12)(g+14),$$ so the large-$g$ estimate has a positive margin already at $g=12$ and only increases afterwards. No numerical trial is hidden here; the split is forced only by the sign change of the $x$-coefficient in $Q$. Therefore $H_2>0$, hence $H>0$, and so $x'+y'<(g-3)/2$.

The geometry behind the split is simple but essential. For $8\leq g\leq 11$, the coefficient $2(g-12)$ is negative, so increasing $x$ makes the height test worse rather than better. In that range the sharp move is to push $x$ to the boundary value $(g-3)/2-y$ supplied by the cone and then use only the lower bound $y\geq 1$. For $g\geq 12$, the sign reverses and both $x$ and $y$ become helpful directions, so the coarse lower bounds $x,y\geq 1$ already suffice. The proof therefore uses the weakest valid estimate in each range and never appeals to a sampled picture of the cone.

[\[prop:cone\]]{#prop:cone label="prop:cone"} If $U\in \mathcal{K}_g$, then $U'=C_gU\in \mathcal{K}_g$.

Because $D>0$, the sign of $X'-1$ is the sign of $g-3-x-2y$. On the cone one has $x+2y<(g-3)/2+y\leq g-4<g-3$, so $X'-1>0$. Likewise $Y'-1>0$ because $g-9\geq -1$ and the coefficients of $x$ and $y$ are nonnegative, with at least one strictly positive contribution once $x,y\geq
1$. The height inequality follows from the already verified positivity of $H_2=U_1Q$. Hence $X',Y'\geq 1$ and $X'+Y'<(g-3)/2$, which is exactly the condition $U'\in \mathcal{K}_g$.

The force of Proposition [\[prop:cone\]](#prop:cone){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:cone"} is that it returns the iterate to the *open* cone and not merely to its closure. That distinction is what lets the selector proof restart with strict inequalities at every step. A closed cone argument would leave open the possibility of landing on a boundary where a future selector tie could occur. The present proof needs the stronger conclusion: once inside the cone, every full iterate comes back with room to spare, so the same two selector winners remain available indefinitely.

[\[rem:g7\]]{#rem:g7 label="rem:g7"} At $g=7$, the seed $(x,y)=(1,1)$ lies on the boundary $x+y=(g-3)/2$, and the first gap is $M_S=1$. The seed is therefore a strict-cone boundary example, not a selector tie.

# Phase/carry induction and characteristic-zero no-cancellation {#sec:induction}

## Phase vectors {#sec:phase}

Let $u_0=\mathbf{1}=(1,1,1)^{\mathsf T}$, and let $v_{n+1}$ and $u_{n+1}$ be the selected $p$- and $q$-degree vectors after the first and second shears at time $n$. The phase convention is $$v_{n+1}=A_gu_n,\qquad u_{n+1}=C_gu_n.$$ The first equality reads the first selected face, and the second equality reads the complete step. The distinction matters throughout the section. The intermediate vector $v_{n+1}$ is the selected support after applying only $S$, while $u_{n+1}$ is the selected support after the full map $T\circ S$. The proof keeps those two phases separate so that the half-step matrix $A_g$ is never confused with the full-step matrix $C_g$.

There are two kinds of bookkeeping to maintain at once. One is geometric: which face wins inside a given support row. The other is temporal: whether the newly created term at time $n$ is still larger than the term carried from time $n-1$. The selector gaps $M_S$ and $M_T$ resolve the first issue, while Lemma [\[lem:monotone\]](#lem:monotone){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:monotone"} resolves the second by showing that the full-step degree vector grows strictly in every coordinate.

The iterate structure is easiest to read as a chain: $$u_0 \xrightarrow{S} v_1 \xrightarrow{T} u_1 \xrightarrow{S} v_2
\xrightarrow{T} u_2 \xrightarrow{S} \cdots$$ At each $S$-arrow the first matrix $A_g$ acts, and at each $T$-arrow the second matrix $B_g$ acts on the selected support to produce the full-step matrix $C_g$. The point of the chain is not decorative; it prevents the common mistake of comparing a carried term from time $n-1$ directly against a full-step term at time $n+1$ without accounting for the intermediate phase.

The first symbolic iterate already exhibits the later mechanism in miniature: $$v_1=A_g\mathbf{1}=(g-1,5,5)^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
u_1=C_g\mathbf{1}=(g+19,2g+13,5(g-1))^{\mathsf T}.$$ The first vector shows the immediate $S$-phase transfer, while the second already contains the three row sums that later reappear in the Perron bound. It also shows that the full-step third coordinate is largest at the seed itself, since $5(g-1)>2g+13>g+19$ for $g\geq 8$. The induction below should therefore be read as a persistence argument for a pattern that is visible from the first full iterate and not as a wholly different asymptotic phenomenon.

One more exact step already displays the quadratic growth pattern hidden behind the linear recurrence: $$v_2=A_gu_1=
\bigl((g-1)(g+19),\;14g+41,\;11g+59\bigr)^{\mathsf T},$$ $$u_2=C_gu_1=
\bigl(g^2+68g+181,\;2g^2+72g+121,\;11g^2+48g-59\bigr)^{\mathsf T}.$$ These formulas are not needed for the abstract induction, but they provide a useful audit of what the induction is claiming. The full-step third coordinate already remains visible at the second iterate because $$u_{2,3}-u_{2,2}=3(g-6)(3g+10)>0,
\qquad
u_{2,3}-u_{2,1}=10(g-6)(g+4)>0$$ for every $g\geq 8$. In other words, the visible-row phenomenon is not only eventually true; it is already visible in the first two exact full iterates, and the later cone argument explains why this symbolic dominance persists for all subsequent iterates.

## The inductive state in all six coordinates {#sec:six-state}

Write the $n$-th iterate in coordinates as $$F_g^n(q,p)=\bigl(Q^{(n)}(q,p),P^{(n)}(q,p)\bigr),$$ where $Q^{(n)}=(Q^{(n)}_1,Q^{(n)}_2,Q^{(n)}_3)$ and similarly for $P^{(n)}$. Applying one more complete step first forms $$\widetilde P^{(n+1)}
=P^{(n)}+\nabla V\bigl(Q^{(n)}\bigr)$$ and then forms $$Q^{(n+1)}
=Q^{(n)}+\nabla W\bigl(\widetilde P^{(n+1)}\bigr),
\qquad
P^{(n+1)}=\widetilde P^{(n+1)}.$$ These identities show exactly where the two carried vectors enter. The old $P^{(n)}$ competes with the fresh $V$-gradient during the first phase; the old $Q^{(n)}$ competes with the fresh $W$-gradient during the second. Thus a proof that checks only the internal pure-versus-mixed selectors is not yet an induction: the carried coordinate in each displayed sum must also lose strictly.

Let $$\mathbf d_q^{(n)}
=\bigl(\deg Q^{(n)}_1,\deg Q^{(n)}_2,\deg Q^{(n)}_3\bigr)^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
\mathbf d_p^{(n)}
=\bigl(\deg P^{(n)}_1,\deg P^{(n)}_2,\deg P^{(n)}_3\bigr)^{\mathsf T}.$$ The induction will certify the six-coordinate state $$\mathbf d_q^{(n)}=u_n,
\qquad
\mathbf d_p^{(n)}=v_n\quad(n\geq1),$$ with $\mathbf d_q^{(0)}=\mathbf d_p^{(0)}=\mathbf{1}$. This statement is not assumed in defining $u_n,v_n$: it is the conclusion obtained after strict selection, carried-term dominance, and coefficient survival have all been checked. The two phases can be summarized as follows.

The vector $v_{n+1}$ is both the output of the new $S$-phase and the $p$-degree vector retained at the end of the complete step. The vector $u_{n+1}$ is created only after the $T$-phase. In particular, $B_gv_{n+1}$ may be replaced by $C_gu_n$ only after the first equality in the table has been proved. This is the phase-carry dependency that the remaining lemmas close.

[\[lem:monotone\]]{#lem:monotone label="lem:monotone"} For $g\geq 8$, the matrix $$C_g-I=
\begin{pmatrix}
g+6&6&6\\
2g+4&4&4\\
2g-2&2g-2&g-2
\end{pmatrix}$$ is entrywise positive. Consequently, whenever $u_{n+1}=C_gu_n$ with $u_n\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3$, one has $u_{n+1}>u_n$ coordinatewise.

Every entry of $C_g-I$ is strictly positive for $g\geq 8$. Therefore $(C_g-I)u_n>0$ for every positive vector $u_n$, which is equivalent to $u_{n+1}-u_n>0$.

[\[lem:T-carry\]]{#lem:T-carry label="lem:T-carry"} Suppose that the $S$-phase has selected $v_{n+1}=A_gu_n$. Then the fresh $T$-gradient degree vector strictly dominates the carried $q$-degree vector, including at the base step: $$B_gv_{n+1}-u_n
 =C_gu_n-u_n
 =(C_g-I)u_n>0,
 \qquad n\geq 0.$$ Consequently the carried coordinates $q_1,q_2,q_3$ cannot alter the selected full-step vector $u_{n+1}=C_gu_n$.

The identity $B_gv_{n+1}=B_gA_gu_n=C_gu_n$ is the phase product. Because every $u_n$ is positive and $C_g-I$ is entrywise positive by Lemma [\[lem:monotone\]](#lem:monotone){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:monotone"}, the displayed difference is positive in all three coordinates. For $n=0$ this reads $(C_g-I)\mathbf{1}>0$, so the argument includes the initially carried coordinate functions of degree one rather than starting only after the first iterate. The selector gap $M_T$ has a separate role: it chooses the pure term over the mixed term inside the competitive third $W$-row. The present lemma is what compares those freshly selected $W$-gradient rows with the three carried $q$-coordinates.

[\[prop:base\]]{#prop:base label="prop:base"} At $n=0$, the selected $S$-face dominates the carried term, and $v_1=A_gu_0>\mathbf{1}$.

Lemma [\[lem:seed\]](#lem:seed){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:seed"} places the seed in the cone when $g\geq 8$. Lemma [\[lem:selector\]](#lem:selector){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:selector"} then gives $M_S>0$, so the pure-versus-mixed comparison on the $S$-phase is strict. Since $$A_g\mathbf{1}=(g-1,5,5)^{\mathsf T},$$ the selected gradient terms strictly dominate the carried $p$-terms. At the base step the carried $p$-coordinates are simply the original coordinate functions $p_1,p_2,p_3$, all of degree one, so there is no hidden competitor with the same weight.

[\[prop:induction\]]{#prop:induction label="prop:induction"} For every $n\geq 1$, if $p_n=A_gu_{n-1}$, then the $S$-selector remains strict, the intermediate vector satisfies $A_gu_n>p_n$, and the $T$-phase selects $u_{n+1}=C_gu_n$.

Assume $p_n=A_gu_{n-1}$. By Proposition [\[prop:cone\]](#prop:cone){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:cone"}, every $u_n$ lies in $\mathcal{K}_g$, and by Lemma [\[lem:monotone\]](#lem:monotone){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:monotone"} one has $u_n>u_{n-1}$ coordinatewise. Since $A_g$ has nonnegative entries and each row has at least one strictly positive entry, it follows that $$A_gu_n>A_gu_{n-1}=p_n.$$ Writing $\Delta_n=u_n-u_{n-1}$, the strict difference is $$A_g\Delta_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
(g-1)\Delta_{n,1}\\
2\Delta_{n,1}+\Delta_{n,2}+2\Delta_{n,3}\\
2\Delta_{n,1}+2\Delta_{n,2}+\Delta_{n,3}
\end{pmatrix}
>0.$$ This is the old-term dominance statement: the newly selected $S$-term is strictly larger than the carried $p$-term from the previous iterate. The $T$-selector is then controlled by the second explicit gap $M_T$, whose strict positivity was proved in Lemma [\[lem:selector\]](#lem:selector){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:selector"}. After that internal face comparison, Lemma [\[lem:T-carry\]](#lem:T-carry){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:T-carry"} proves separately that $C_gu_n-u_n>0$, so all three freshly selected $T$-gradient rows beat the carried $q$-coordinates. Thus the same two faces are selected again and the full step is exactly $u_{n+1}=C_gu_n$. At no stage do we invoke a preservation of arbitrary face hyperplanes; we use the two explicit gaps $M_S$ and $M_T$, the $S$-carry margins, and the explicit $T$-carry margin $(C_g-I)u_n$.

[\[lem:unique-selection\]]{#lem:unique-selection label="lem:unique-selection"} For every $n\geq 0$, each support row contributing to the recurrence has a unique selected exponent vector.

The rigid rows in Table [4](#tab:support){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:support"} are singletons, so uniqueness is automatic there. In the $S$-competitive row, Lemma [\[lem:selector\]](#lem:selector){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:selector"} gives $M_S>0$ on $\mathcal{K}_g$, so the pure exponent $(g-1,0,0)$ strictly beats the mixed exponent $(1,2,2)$. In the $T$-competitive row, the same lemma gives $M_T>0$, so the pure exponent $(0,0,g-1)$ strictly beats the mixed exponent $(2,2,1)$. Because the cone is invariant, these strict inequalities are available at every iterate. Hence each selected row in the recurrence has a unique winner before any coefficient bookkeeping is considered.

## No-cancellation in characteristic zero {#sec:nocancel}

[\[lem:nocancel\]]{#lem:nocancel label="lem:nocancel"} Let $R=\mathbb N[z_1,\dots,z_6]$. If a polynomial is written as a finite sum of distinct monomials with positive coefficients, then substituting polynomials from $R$ preserves positivity of every selected Minkowski-sum coefficient. Over a field of characteristic zero, the resulting selected coefficient is nonzero.

Distributivity expands the substituted polynomial as a finite sum of products of positive integers. No sign changes occur, and characteristic zero prevents the positive integer coefficient from vanishing. In the present application the selected monomial support is obtained by Minkowski-summing already selected supports from the previous phase. Every contribution to the selected coefficient is therefore a positive integer, so the coefficient after substitution is a positive integer as well. The lemma is exactly the support-level version of the carry induction above: once the selector gaps are strict, the only remaining obstruction would be cancellation, and that obstruction is absent in characteristic zero.

The role of Lemma [\[lem:unique-selection\]](#lem:unique-selection){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:unique-selection"} is precisely to make this coefficient statement meaningful. Strict selector gaps identify a unique exponent vector in each competitive row, and the semiring argument then shows that the coefficient attached to that unique exponent remains positive after substitution. The proof does not rely on a general theorem about all substitutions of positive polynomials; it uses the much narrower fact that the selected exponent is unique and every contributing coefficient path is a positive integer path.

A representative example is the pure $T$-row. Once the selected $S$-phase support for $p_3$ is fixed, the pure monomial $p_3^{g-1}$ expands into a product of $g-1$ identical positive-support polynomials. Every path contributing to the selected exponent adds a positive integer coefficient, and no negative sign is ever introduced. The same mechanism applies to the mixed rows, where the selected exponent is obtained by a Minkowski sum of already selected supports. Thus the no-cancellation lemma is not an abstract semiring tagline; it is the coefficient-level reason the exact selected row survives the entire iteration.

[\[lem:coefficient-witness\]]{#lem:coefficient-witness label="lem:coefficient-witness"} Assume the selected monomial in each $Q_i^{(n)}$ has a positive integer coefficient $a_i$. Then the three selected $S$-gradient monomials receive positive contributions with coefficients $$g a_1^{g-1},
\qquad
2a_1^2a_2a_3^2,
\qquad
2a_1^2a_2^2a_3.$$ If the corresponding selected monomials of $\widetilde P^{(n+1)}$ have positive coefficients $b_1,b_2,b_3$, then the three selected $T$-gradient monomials receive positive contributions with coefficients $$2b_1b_2^2b_3^2,
\qquad
2b_1^2b_2b_3^2,
\qquad
g b_3^{g-1}.$$ Every one of these witnesses is nonzero in characteristic zero.

The first list is read directly from $$\partial_{q_1}V
=2q_1q_2^2q_3^2+gq_1^{g-1},\quad
\partial_{q_2}V=2q_1^2q_2q_3^2,\quad
\partial_{q_3}V=2q_1^2q_2^2q_3.$$ The strict $S$-selector chooses the pure term in the first row, while the other two rows are singletons. Choosing one already selected monomial from each factor in the indicated products gives exactly the three displayed positive contributions. Likewise, $$\partial_{p_1}W=2p_1p_2^2p_3^2,\quad
\partial_{p_2}W=2p_1^2p_2p_3^2,\quad
\partial_{p_3}W=2p_1^2p_2^2p_3+gp_3^{g-1}.$$ The first two rows are singletons and the strict $T$-selector chooses the pure term in the third row, producing the second list.

Other products may contribute to the same exponent, so the displayed numbers are coefficient witnesses rather than claims that no other path exists. That distinction is harmless and useful: all forward coordinate polynomials lie in the positive semiring $\mathbb N[q_1,q_2,q_3,p_1,p_2,p_3]$, so any additional path only adds another nonnegative integer. Since $2$ and the positive integer $g$ remain nonzero in characteristic zero, each selected exponent survives with a nonzero coefficient. The lemma therefore turns the abstract semiring statement into six explicit recursive witnesses, one for each fresh gradient row.

[\[thm:recurrence\]]{#thm:recurrence label="thm:recurrence"} The complete selected recurrence is $$v_{n+1}=A_gu_n,\qquad u_{n+1}=C_gu_n\qquad (n\geq 0),$$ and the selected support coefficient never cancels.

Combine Proposition [\[prop:base\]](#prop:base){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:base"}, Lemma [\[lem:T-carry\]](#lem:T-carry){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:T-carry"}, Proposition [\[prop:induction\]](#prop:induction){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:induction"}, Lemma [\[lem:unique-selection\]](#lem:unique-selection){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:unique-selection"}, and Lemmas [\[lem:nocancel\]](#lem:nocancel){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:nocancel"}--[\[lem:coefficient-witness\]](#lem:coefficient-witness){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:coefficient-witness"}. The recurrence is phase-labelled: the first matrix is the first shear, the second matrix is the complete step, and the support coefficient remains positive at every iteration. Both carried-coordinate comparisons are explicit: $A_gu_n$ beats the carried $p$-vector, while $C_gu_n$ beats the carried $q$-vector. In particular, the induction does not merely identify the maximal degree numerically; it identifies a specific support row whose coefficient remains nonzero through the entire iteration process.

This is the point at which characteristic zero is genuinely used. If the field had positive characteristic, a positive integer coefficient could vanish after substitution, and the support-level argument would no longer imply that the selected row survives. The paper therefore keeps the field hypothesis in the main theorem rather than hiding it in a remark, because it is part of the mechanism that makes the exact recurrence possible.

The conclusion of the theorem is stronger than a bare degree recurrence. It records a persistent support mechanism. At each stage one can point to the same selector rows, the same carried-coordinate comparisons, and the same positivity argument on coefficients. This is why the later visibility proof can safely read the degree from one coordinate functional: the recurrence is not an accidental equality of numbers but the shadow of a support pattern that has been preserved through every phase of the iteration.

# Visibility, exact degree, and Perron bound {#sec:visibility}

## Visibility of the third coordinate {#sec:visible}

The matrix difference is $$C_g-A_g=
\begin{pmatrix}
8&6&6\\
2g+2&4&2\\
2g-4&2g-4&g-2
\end{pmatrix},$$ which is entrywise positive for $g\geq 8$. Furthermore, $$U_3-U_2=[-6+(2g-7)x+(g-5)y]U_1>0,
\qquad
U_3-U_1=[g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y]U_1>0.$$ The entrywise positivity of $C_g-A_g$ controls the $q$- versus $p$-phase comparison, while the two displayed differences control the internal ordering of the $q$-coordinates. Together they show that the visible coordinate is not merely one of several maximal rows: it is the unique row that dominates the other five coordinate degrees after each full iterate. Hence $q_3$ is the maximal coordinate degree among all six coordinates.

[\[lem:six-dominance\]]{#lem:six-dominance label="lem:six-dominance"} For every $n\geq1$, the full-step degree vectors satisfy $$u_n>v_n$$ coordinatewise, and the third component of $u_n$ is strictly larger than the first two components. Consequently the third $q$-coordinate has degree strictly larger than each of the other five coordinate functions of $F_g^n$.

Write the input to the $n$-th full step as $w=u_{n-1}=(W_1,W_2,W_3)^{\mathsf T}$, with $x=W_2/W_1$ and $y=W_3/W_1$. The exact recurrence gives $$u_n-v_n=(C_g-A_g)w>0$$ because $C_g-A_g$ is entrywise positive. This compares corresponding $q_i$- and $p_i$-rows.

For the internal $q$-ordering, put $u_n=(U_1,U_2,U_3)^{\mathsf T}$. Subtracting rows of $C_g$ gives $$\begin{aligned}
U_3-U_2
&=\bigl[-6+(2g-7)x+(g-5)y\bigr]W_1,\\
U_3-U_1
&=\bigl[g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y\bigr]W_1.\end{aligned}$$ Since $x,y\geq1$, their bracketed coefficients have the explicit lower bounds $$-6+(2g-7)+(g-5)=3g-18\geq6,
\qquad
g-9+(2g-8)+(g-7)=4g-24\geq8.$$ Thus $U_3>U_2,U_1$. Finally, the dominance chains $$U_3>U_1>\deg P^{(n)}_1,
\qquad
U_3>U_2>\deg P^{(n)}_2,
\qquad
U_3>\deg P^{(n)}_3$$ compare the visible row with all three $p$-coordinates. Hence it is the unique maximal degree among all six coordinate functions.

The third row then dominates the first two rows because the two displayed inequalities show $U_3-U_2>0$ and $U_3-U_1>0$. Only after that reduction does it compare the three $q$-rows among themselves. This order matters because the functional $e_3^{\mathsf T}$ is an output of the visibility proof and not an assumption built into the recurrence from the beginning.

[\[prop:degree\]]{#prop:degree label="prop:degree"} For every $n\geq 0$, $$\operatorname{deg}(F_g^n)=e_3^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\mathbf{1}.$$

For $n\geq 1$, the phase recurrence gives the selected $q$-degree vector $u_n=C_g^n\mathbf{1}$ and the intermediate $p$-degree vector $v_n=A_gu_{n-1}$. Because $$u_n-v_n=(C_g-A_g)u_{n-1},$$ and $C_g-A_g$ is entrywise positive for $g\geq 8$, every $q$-coordinate degree after the full step exceeds the corresponding intermediate $p$-coordinate degree. Among the $q$-coordinates, the displayed inequalities give $U_3>U_2$ and $U_3>U_1$. Since the visible coefficient never cancels, the degree of $F_g^n$ is read off by the third-coordinate functional and equals $e_3^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\mathbf{1}$.

Already at the first full iterate, every $q$-coordinate beats the corresponding $p$-coordinate and the third $q$-coordinate beats the other two $q$-coordinates by explicit positive amounts. One can read the first values of the exact degree sequence directly from this audit: $$\operatorname{deg}(F_g^0)=1,\qquad
\operatorname{deg}(F_g^1)=5(g-1),\qquad
\operatorname{deg}(F_g^2)=11g^2+48g-59.$$ These values are not obtained by guessing a scalar recurrence first and then verifying it afterward. They are obtained by following the visible row of the matrix recurrence that has already been certified symbolically. This point becomes important in the next section: once the characteristic polynomial of $C_g$ is known, it governs the *exact* degree sequence because the degree has already been tied to one fixed row of $C_g^n$, not merely bounded above by it.

[\[cor:perron\]]{#cor:perron label="cor:perron"} The matrix $C_g$ is primitive, hence $$\lambda_1(F_g)=\rho(C_g).$$ Its row sums are $g+19$, $2g+13$, and $5(g-1)$, all strictly below $(g-1)^2$ for $g\geq 8$. Therefore $$\rho(C_g)<(g-1)^2.$$

$C_g$ itself is entrywise positive for $g\geq 8$, hence primitive. Perron--Frobenius therefore provides a positive right eigenvector $r\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3$ for the simple eigenvalue $\rho(C_g)$. Choose positive constants $a,b$ such that $$a r\leq\mathbf{1}\leq b r$$ coordinatewise; for example, take $a=\min_i r_i^{-1}$ and $b=\max_i r_i^{-1}$. Positivity of $C_g$ preserves these inequalities, so $$a\rho(C_g)^n r_3
\leq e_3^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\mathbf{1}
\leq b\rho(C_g)^n r_3.$$ Taking $n$-th roots proves directly that $$\lim_{n\to\infty}
\bigl(e_3^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\mathbf{1}\bigr)^{1/n}=\rho(C_g).$$ By Proposition [\[prop:degree\]](#prop:degree){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:degree"}, $$\operatorname{deg}(F_g^n)=e_3^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\mathbf{1},$$ so the dynamical degree $\lambda_1(F_g)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{deg}(F_g^n)^{1/n}$ is exactly the Perron root of $C_g$. The row sums are $$(g+7)+6+6=g+19,\qquad (2g+4)+5+4=2g+13,\qquad 2(g-1)+2(g-1)+(g-1)=5(g-1).$$ Their maximum bounds the spectral radius from above, and the differences $$\begin{aligned}
(g-1)^2-(g+19)&=(g-6)(g+3),\\
(g-1)^2-(2g+13)&=(g-6)(g+2),\\
(g-1)^2-5(g-1)&=(g-1)(g-6)\end{aligned}$$ are all positive for $g\geq 8$. Hence $\rho(C_g)<(g-1)^2$.

The estimate is intentionally elementary. The exact Perron root has already been identified as the dynamical degree, so the remaining task is only to place that root below a simple closed expression. The row-sum bound does this with visible factorizations and no need to solve the cubic or estimate eigenvectors. What matters is not sharpness for its own sake, but the fact that the upper bound can be checked directly from the displayed matrix entries.

# Characteristic polynomial and mod-five cubic Perron subfamilies {#sec:cubic}

[\[lem:charpoly\]]{#lem:charpoly label="lem:charpoly"} The characteristic polynomial of $C_g$ is $$\chi_{C_g}(t)=t^3-(2g+11)t^2+(g^2-16g+19)t-9(g-1)^2.$$

For a $3\times 3$ matrix, the characteristic polynomial is determined by the trace, the sum of principal $2\times 2$ minors, and the determinant. Here $$\operatorname{tr}(C_g)=(g+7)+5+(g-1)=2g+11.$$ The principal $2\times 2$ minors are $$\det\!\begin{pmatrix}g+7&6\\2g+4&5\end{pmatrix}=11-7g,
\qquad
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}g+7&6\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}=(g-1)(g-5),$$ and $$\det\!\begin{pmatrix}5&4\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}=-3(g-1).$$ Their sum is $$(11-7g)+(g-1)(g-5)-3(g-1)=g^2-16g+19.$$ Finally, $$\det(C_g)=\det(B_g)\det(A_g)=\bigl(-3(g-1)\bigr)^2=9(g-1)^2.$$ Substituting these three quantities into the standard $3\times 3$ characteristic-polynomial formula yields the stated expression. The coefficient pattern is rigid and uses no hidden CAS identity.

This trace--minor--determinant route is the shortest exact path from the displayed matrix to the cubic polynomial. A direct expansion of $\det(tI-C_g)$ would certainly be possible, but it would obscure the meaning of the coefficients. Here each coefficient is attached to a standard matrix invariant, so a reader can audit the cubic one invariant at a time.

[\[prop:mod5\]]{#prop:mod5 label="prop:mod5"} If $g\equiv 2,3,4 \pmod 5$, then $\chi_{C_g}(t)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

Reduce the characteristic polynomial modulo $5$. The three residue classes $g\equiv 2,3,4\pmod 5$ produce exactly the three cubics listed in Table [13](#tab:mod5){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:mod5"}. A cubic polynomial over $\mathbb F_5$ is reducible if and only if it has a root in $\mathbb F_5$. The table records the values at all five field elements and shows that none of the three cubics vanishes. Each reduced polynomial is therefore irreducible in $\mathbb F_5[t]$. Gauss's lemma then gives irreducibility of $\chi_{C_g}(t)$ in $\mathbb Q[t]$.

The arithmetic input is intentionally minimal. For $g\equiv 2\pmod 5$ the reduced cubic is $t^3+t+1$; for $g\equiv 3\pmod 5$ it is $t^3-2t^2-1$; and for $g\equiv 4\pmod 5$ it is $t^3+t^2+t-1$. In each case the table is already a complete no-root certificate because it records the value at every element of $\mathbb F_5$. No hidden finite search or external algebra system is being appealed to: the proof is exactly the five-point audit visible in the main text.

[\[cor:cubic\]]{#cor:cubic label="cor:cubic"} For $g\equiv 2,3,4 \pmod 5$, the Perron root $\rho(C_g)$ is a degree-three algebraic integer. These are infinite cubic Perron subfamilies of the family $F_g$.

Combine Proposition [\[prop:mod5\]](#prop:mod5){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:mod5"} with Corollary [\[cor:perron\]](#cor:perron){reference-type="ref" reference="cor:perron"}. Since $\rho(C_g)$ is the dominant eigenvalue of an integral $3\times 3$ matrix with irreducible characteristic polynomial over $\mathbb Q$, it has degree three over $\mathbb Q$ and is a Perron algebraic integer. The word "cubic" therefore refers only to the algebraic degree of the Perron root in these residue classes; it is not a claim about a broader classification of the family.

[\[rem:mod5\]]{#rem:mod5 label="rem:mod5"} The mod-five test is intentionally one-sided. It proves irreducibility for the listed residue classes and stays silent for $g\equiv 0,1\pmod 5$. The paper does not claim that the omitted classes are reducible, nor that mod five is the only useful modulus. The corollary is exactly the arithmetic statement that the displayed table certifies and nothing broader.

# Boundary audit, limitations, anti-claims, and conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

The borderline case $g=7$ is a helpful anti-example. The seed $\mathbf{1}$ lies on the strict-cone boundary and the $S$-gap is positive, so the obstruction is geometric rather than a selector tie. This is why the theorem is stated for $g\geq 8$: the proof uses the open cone and the $g=8,\dots,11$ versus $g\geq 12$ split.

The limitations are not cosmetic. We do not claim a generic finite Newton-fan theorem, a classification of all symplectic or affine-triangular automorphisms, an entropy theorem, a periodic-point theorem, a torus theorem, a conjugacy theorem, or a priority claim. We also do not claim any CAS output as proof, nor any numerical sampling as evidence. The theorem is about one displayed family, one displayed cone, and one exact recurrence.

The bounded novelty wording should be read in the same restrained way. It is not an absolute novelty certificate. If a later comparison changed the surrounding context language, the proof of the present theorem would remain the same because it depends only on the explicit gradients, matrices, inequalities, and residue checks already displayed in the main text.

This is why the conclusion keeps returning to exactness rather than breadth. The paper succeeds by freezing one family tightly enough that every theorem-critical step fits in the main body: two gradients, six support rows, two selector gaps, one invariant cone, one visible degree row, and one finite residue audit. Any broader theorem would first need to replace at least one of those exact components with a genuinely new argument. The present manuscript does not attempt that broader project.

There is also a methodological lesson in the proof order itself. The paper never uses spectral information to discover the recurrence, never uses the final degree formula to justify visibility, and never uses the mod-five corollary to motivate the support ledger retroactively. Each stage is proved with the data available at that stage and then handed forward. In a short paper one might compress these steps into a single narrative paragraph, but for an exact theorem the separation is part of the rigor: it tells the reader which calculation depends on which earlier certification and prevents later algebra from hiding an earlier selector gap.

From the proof one should remember three facts. First, the selected matrices $A_g$, $B_g$, and $C_g$ are read from a six-row support ledger and not from an abstract template. Second, the correct degree observable is the third $q$-coordinate, not a half-step intermediate. Third, the algebraic consequence of the mod-five audit is a genuine family of cubic Perron numbers, not a casual irreducibility remark.

The paper therefore closes where it began: with one exact family, one exact degree recurrence, and one exact algebraic corollary. The rest is bookkeeping around those identities. Nothing theorem-critical is hidden offstage: a reader who wants to verify the result can replay Lemmas [\[lem:canonical\]](#lem:canonical){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:canonical"} through Proposition [\[prop:mod5\]](#prop:mod5){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:mod5"} in order and recover the whole theorem from the main body alone.

For that reason the manuscript is self-contained at the proof level: a reader only needs the fixed proof order L1 through L9, the two contextual citations, and the displayed support ledger. There is no missing appendix dependency, no figure dependency, and no computational certificate to request before the theorem can be checked line by line.
