# Owned geometry, totalized map, and theorem {#sec:setup}

Kocik and Solecki explicitly describe the four vertices of an orthocentric system, each point being the orthocenter of the triangle formed by the other three [@KocikSolecki2009]. Wildberger develops affine metrical triangle geometry and orthocenters over finite fields [@Wildberger2008]. Guy also treats a triangle as an orthocentric quadrangle, but his iterative "trisequence" is generated by Steiner-line reflections and circumcircle intersections, not by the sliding window below [@Guy2019]. Accordingly, the orthocentric identity, its four-window recurrence, and the finite-field setting are background only.

Let $p\equiv3\pmod4$ be prime. On $V=\mathbb F_p^2$ use $$(x,y)\mathbin\cdot(u,v)=xu+yv.$$ An ordered triple $(A,B,C)$ is a triangle when its points are noncollinear. Its orthocenter $H(A,B,C)$ is the unique solution of $$\label{eq:altitudes}
 (H-A)\cdot(B-C)=0,\qquad (H-B)\cdot(A-C)=0.$$ Let $\mathcal X_p$ contain every ordered triangle and one sink $\dagger$. Define $$\label{eq:map}
 F(A,B,C)=
 \begin{cases}
 (B,C,H(A,B,C)),&B,C,H(A,B,C)\text{ are noncollinear},\\
 \dagger,&\text{otherwise},
 \end{cases}
 \qquad F(\dagger)=\dagger.$$ A triangle is *right at coordinate $i$* when its two side vectors from the $i$th listed vertex are perpendicular. Put $$\label{eq:counts}
 T=p^2(p^2-1)(p^2-p),\quad
 R=p^2(p^2-1)(p-1),\quad
 Q=T-3R=p^2(p^2-1)(p-1)(p-3).$$ For a state $x$, let $d(x)$ be its distance to the recurrent set.

[\[thm:atlas\]]{#thm:atlas label="thm:atlas"} The map $F$ has the following complete description.

(i) There are $T$ ordered triangles and exactly $R$ right triangles at each specified coordinate. The three right-angle classes are disjoint. The $Q$ nonright triangles have exact period four; the sink is fixed.

(ii) A triangle right at coordinate one has depth two. A triangle right at coordinate two or three has depth one. Hence the sharp height is two, and $$\label{eq:depth-cdf}
      \#\{x:d(x)\leq0\}=1+Q,\quad
      \#\{x:d(x)\leq1\}=1+T-R,\quad
      \#\{x:d(x)\leq2\}=1+T.$$ The dynamical zeta function is $$\label{eq:zeta}
      \zeta_F(z)=(1-z)^{-1}(1-z^4)^{-Q/4}.$$

(iii) The one-step target fibres are $$\label{eq:fibres}
       \#F^{-1}(y)=
       \begin{cases}
       1+2R,&y=\dagger,\\
       0,&y\text{ is right at coordinate one or two},\\
       1,&y\text{ is right at coordinate three or is nonright}.
       \end{cases}$$ Consequently $$\begin{aligned}
       \operatorname{im}F&=\{\dagger\}\cup\{\text{nonright triangles}\}
             \cup\{\text{triangles right at coordinate three}\},
             \label{eq:image-one}\\
       \#\operatorname{im}F&=1+T-2R,\qquad
       F^2(\mathcal X_p)=\{\dagger\}\cup\{\text{nonright triangles}\},
       \label{eq:stable-image}\\
       \#F^2(\mathcal X_p)&=1+Q,\qquad F^t(\mathcal X_p)=F^2(\mathcal X_p)\quad(t\geq2).\end{aligned}$$

(iv) At $p=3$, $T=432$, $R=144$, and $Q=0$. Thus the sink is the only recurrent state, while the depth-two shell still contains $144$ triangles. The one-step image has size $145$, the stable image has size one, and the sink fibre has size $289$.

# Anisotropy and the right-angle strata {#sec:counts}

Because $p\equiv3\pmod4$, $-1$ is not a square. Therefore $$\label{eq:anisotropy}
 x^2+y^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x,y)=(0,0).$$ Indeed, if $y\neq0$ then $(x/y)^2=-1$, and if $y=0$ then $x=0$. Thus the dot product is anisotropic. A nonzero vector $(x,y)$ has perpendicular line spanned by $(-y,x)$, and no nonzero perpendicular vector is parallel to it.

There are $p^2$ choices for $A$, then $p^2-1$ choices for $B-A$, and $p^2-p$ choices for $C$ off the affine line $AB$. This proves the formula for $T$. To count triangles right at $A$, choose $A$, a nonzero vector $u=B-A$, and one of the $p-1$ nonzero vectors $v=C-A$ on $u^\perp$. Anisotropy makes $u,v$ independent, giving $R$.

The three right classes cannot overlap. For example, if $u=B-A$ and $v=C-A$ made the triangle right at both $A$ and $B$, then $$u\cdot v=0,\qquad (-u)\cdot(v-u)=0$$ would imply $u\cdot u=0$, contradicting [\[eq:anisotropy\]](#eq:anisotropy){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:anisotropy"}. The other pairs follow by relabeling. Subtracting the three disjoint classes proves the formula for $Q$ in [\[eq:counts\]](#eq:counts){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:counts"}. The altitude equations [\[eq:altitudes\]](#eq:altitudes){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:altitudes"} have a unique solution because the two opposite-side vectors are independent and the dot pairing is nondegenerate.

# Four windows and oriented singular depths {#sec:forward}

We include an algebraic verification of the owned orthocentric identity only to close the field-specific proof. From [\[eq:altitudes\]](#eq:altitudes){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:altitudes"}, subtracting the two zero scalar products gives $(H-C)\cdot(A-B)=0$. Thus $H$ lies on all three altitudes. Conversely, the same three perpendicularities show that $A$ lies on the altitudes of $(B,C,H)$. Hence $$\label{eq:quartet}
 H(B,C,H(A,B,C))=A$$ whenever that next triangle is noncollinear; cyclic variants hold as well.

If the original triangle is nonright, $H$ is distinct from $A,B,C$. No three of the four points are collinear: otherwise the altitude equations and anisotropy force the alleged third point to coincide with an endpoint. Equation [\[eq:quartet\]](#eq:quartet){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:quartet"} therefore produces the valid windows $$\label{eq:four-windows}
 (A,B,C),\ (B,C,H),\ (C,H,A),\ (H,A,B),\ (A,B,C).$$ No return at time one, two, or three is compatible with the three distinct ordered vertices. The period is exactly four. This classical core receives no contribution credit.

For a right triangle the orthocenter is its right-angle vertex. If it is right at $A$, the first successor is the cyclic rotation $(B,C,A)$, which is right at its third coordinate; its next successor is degenerate and hence $\dagger$. If the original triangle is right at $B$ or $C$, the first next window already repeats that coordinate and goes to $\dagger$. This proves the oriented depths, sharp height, stable recurrent set, and [\[eq:depth-cdf\]](#eq:depth-cdf){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:depth-cdf"}. There is one fixed cycle and $Q/4$ four-cycles, which gives [\[eq:zeta\]](#eq:zeta){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:zeta"} by the standard finite-map product formula.

# The unique reverse window and all fibres {#sec:inverse}

The fibre law is not obtained by dividing orbit counts. Let $(A,B,C)$ be a target triangle. Any nonsink predecessor has the form $(D,A,B)$ and must satisfy $H(D,A,B)=C$. Orthocentric symmetry then forces the unique candidate $$\label{eq:reverse}
 (D,A,B)=(H(A,B,C),A,B).$$ If the target is right at $A$, its orthocenter equals $A$, so [\[eq:reverse\]](#eq:reverse){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:reverse"} repeats $A$ and is invalid. If it is right at $B$, the candidate repeats $B$ and is again invalid. For a target right at $C$, the candidate is the cyclic rotation $(C,A,B)$ and maps to the target. For a nonright target, the four points in [\[eq:four-windows\]](#eq:four-windows){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:four-windows"} are distinct with no collinear triple, so the candidate is valid and maps to the target. This proves the triangle cases of [\[eq:fibres\]](#eq:fibres){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:fibres"} directly.

The immediate predecessors of $\dagger$ are $\dagger$ itself and precisely the triangles right at coordinate two or three. These two disjoint classes have total size $2R$, proving the sink fibre. Reading the positive fibres gives [\[eq:image-one\]](#eq:image-one){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:image-one"}; applying the oriented depth result once more gives [\[eq:stable-image\]](#eq:stable-image){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:stable-image"}. This completes Theorem [\[thm:atlas\]](#thm:atlas){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:atlas"}.
