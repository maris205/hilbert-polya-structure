L28: ###### Abstract.
L29: The dynamics of a linear dynamical system over a finite field can be described by using the elementary divisors of the corresponding matrix. It is natural to extend the investigation to a general finite commutative ring. In a previous publication, the last two authors developed an efficient algorithm to determine whether a linear dynamical system over a finite commutative ring is a fixed point system or not.
L30: The algorithm can also be used to reduce the problem of finding the cycles of such a system to the case where the system is given by an automorphism. Here, we further analyze the cycle structure of such a system and develop a method to determine its cycles.

L33: Finite rings, Modules, Linear systems, Cycle lengths

L38: ## 1. Introduction
L39: 
L40: Let $R$ be a commutative ring with $1$, let $M$ be an $R$-module, and let $f:M\longrightarrow M$ be an $R$-module endomorphism. We may consider $f$ as a dynamical system via iteration:
L41: 
L42:  | $\displaystyle f,\;\;f^{2}=f\circ f,\;\;\cdots,\;\;f^{m}=\underbrace{f\circ f\circ\cdots\circ f}_{\mbox{$m$ copies}},\;\;\cdots,$  |
L43: and investigate the behaviors of the system. For our convenience, we set $f^{0}=id$. By studying the dynamics of the system, we mean to investigate its possible cycles (including fixed points) generated by the elements of $M$. It is clear that if the cardinality of $M$ is infinite, then there may not be any cycle except the obvious fixed point $0$. But if $M$ is finite, then for any initial input $a\in M$, the sequence $f^{m}(a)$, $m\geq 0$, will eventually either stabilize or enter a cycle.
L44: We should restrict our attention to the case where both $R$ and $M$ are finite in this paper, and we call such a system a linear dynamical system over the finite ring $R$.
L45: If $R=F$ is a finite field, then $M$ is a finite dimensional vector space over $F$, and the dynamics of $f$ can be described by using the elementary divisors of the $F$-linear map $f$. Suppose the elementary divisors of $f$ are (see [cite12†7 ], pp. 356-357):
L46: 
L47:  | $\displaystyle p_{i}^{m_{ij}},\;\;1\leq i\leq s,\;\;1\leq j\leq k_{i},$  |
L48: 
L49: where each $p_{i}$ is an irreducible polynomial in $F[x]$, and the $m_{ij}$ are integers such that for each $i$,
L50:  | $\displaystyle m_{i1}\geq m_{i2}\geq\cdots\geq m_{ik_{i}}>0.$  |
L51: 
L52: Then there are $f$-cyclic subspaces $M_{ij}$ (i.e. there exists a vector $v\in M_{ij}$ and an integer $k\geq 0$ such that $(v,f(v),\ldots,f^{k}(v))$ is a basis of $M_{ij}$) with minimal polynomial $p_{i}^{m_{ij}}$, $1\leq i\leq s,\;1\leq j\leq k_{i}$, such that $M$ is a direct sum of the $M_{ij}$’s.
L53: It is easy to see (over any ring $R$) that if $M=M_{1}\oplus M_{2}$ is a direct sum decomposition of $f$-invariant submodules (we will just call them $f$-submodules), and $C_{i}$ is a cycle in $M_{i}$ of length $c_{i}$ ($i=1,2$), then the direct product
L54: 
L55:  | $\displaystyle C_{1}\times C_{2}:=\{(a,b)\;|\;a\in M_{1},\;b\in M_{2}\}$  |
L56: 
L57: offers $GCD(c_{1},c_{2})$ cycles of length $LCM(c_{1},c_{2})$ for the module $M$. So we can just work with each direct summand independently.
L58: Therefore, the problem of finding the cycles (including fixed points) of $f$ (over a field $F$) reduces to the problem of finding the cycles of the restricted linear maps
L59: 
L60:  | $\displaystyle f_{ij}=f|_{M_{ij}}\;:\;M_{ij}\longrightarrow M_{ij},\;\;1\leq i\leq s,\;\;1\leq j\leq k_{i}.$  |
L61: If $p_{i}=x$, the corresponding linear maps $f_{ij},\;1\leq j\leq k_{i}$, are nilpotent and thus all have just one fixed point $0$ and have no cycle of length $>1$. So the question is to find the cycle structures of those $f_{ij}$ such that the corresponding $p_{i}$ are not $x$ (these $f_{ij}$ are automorphisms of the $M_{ij}$).
L62: For these cases, since the characteristic polynomial of each $f_{ij}$ is equal to its minimal polynomial, Elspas’ formula describes its cycle structure [cite13†4 ][cite14†6 ] (we use $g_{ij}^{k}$ instead of $p_{i}^{m_{ij}}$ to simplify the notation in the formula):
L63:  | $\displaystyle G_{ij}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\frac{q^{mk}-q^{m(k-1)}}{c_{k}}\mathcal{C}_{k},$  |
L64: where $q=|F|$, $1$ corresponds to the fixed point $0$, $\mathcal{C}_{k}$ is a cycle of length $c_{k}$, and $c_{k}=\mbox{ord}(g_{ij}^{k})$ (i.e. $c_{k}$ is the least positive integer $t$ such that $g_{ij}^{k}$ divides $x^{t}-1$).
L65: This formula gives a nice description of the cycle structure, though the actual computation is more involved: one first computes the normal form of the matrix $xI-A$, where $A$ is the matrix of $f$ with respect to a basis of $M$, or computes its minimum polynomial $m(f)$, then factors the polynomials that appear in the normal form of $xI-A$ (or $m(f)$) to get the $g_{ij}^{k}$, and then computes the order of each $g_{ij}^{k}$.
L66: Note that if $g_{ij}^{k}$ divides $x^{t}-1$, then all $g_{ij}^{s},\;0\leq s\leq k$, divide $x^{t}-1$. So by the comment about the cycles of a direct sum of $f$-modules, we have:
L67: ###### Lemma 1.1.
L68: 
L69: The $F$-module endomorphism $f$ possesses a cycle of maximum length, say $c$, such that all other cycle lengths are divisors of $c$.
L70: For a general finite commutative ring $R$, since the factorization in $R[x]$ may not be unique, different approaches are needed. The study of linear dynamical systems over rings of the form $\mathbb{Z}_{q}$ was suggested in [cite15†2 ] due to its relation with monomial dynamical systems over finite fields. In [cite16†1 ] an approach by using Fitting’s lemma [cite12†7 ]: there is a positive integer $k$ such that $M=\mbox{Ker}f^{k}\oplus\mbox{Im}f^{k}$ was suggested.
L71: However, the key to the success of the proposed approach, which is to find a proper $k$ and compute $f^{k}$ efficiently, was not treated. In [cite17†9 ], an upper bound for $k$ was determined, and an algorithm that runs in time $O(n^{3}\log(n\log(q)))$, where $n$ is given by $M=R^{n}$ and $q=|R|$, to compute $f^{k}$ was developed.
L72: The results of [cite17†9 ] provide a solution to the problem of determining whether or not such a system is a fixed point system, and also reduce the problem of finding the cycle structure of such a system to that of an automorphism (since $f:\mbox{Im}f^{k}\longrightarrow\mbox{Im}f^{k}$ is bijective).
L73: The main idea of [cite17†9 ] is, to determine the cycles of $f$, we can just consider the $f$-module $M$ as an abelian group ($\mathbb{Z}$-module) and determine the lengths of the cycles. After obtaining the information on the lengths of the cycles, we can find the cycles by solving linear systems of the form $(f^{c}-I)X=0$, where $I$ is the identity map and $c$ is a cycle length. By the structure theorem of finitely generated abelian groups [cite12†7 ], we can assume that
L74: (1.1)  |  | $\displaystyle\mbox{Im}f^{k}=\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}^{a_{1}}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p_{2}^{a_{2}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p_{m}^{a_{m}}},$  |
L75: 
L76: where $p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}$ are (not necessarily distinct) primes, and $a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}$ are (not necessarily distinct) positive integers.
L77: We further note that an $f$-module as in (cite18†1.1 ) can be written as a direct sum of $f$-submodules such that each of these submodules is formed by grouping those $\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}$’s with the same prime $p_{i}$ together. As discussed before, whenever we have a direct sum of $f$-submodules, we can just work with each direct summand independently. Therefore, our goal here is to analyze the cycle structure of an automorphism of an abelian group of the form ($p$ is a prime):
L78: (1.2)  |  | $\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182ptM=\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a_{1}}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a_{2}}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a_{m}}},\;1\leq a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{m}.$  |
L79: Since abelian groups are $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, an endomorphism of $M$ can be represented by an integer matrix $A$; and if all $a_{i}=1$, i.e. $M=\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{p}$, it is an endomorphism of the vector space $M$ over the field $\mathbb{Z}_{p}$. In the case where all $a_{i}$’s are the same, i.e. $M=\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{p^{a}}$, an approach was developed in [cite19†3 ] for an arbitrary endomorphism of $M$ using number theory techniques based on congruence of integers.
L80: In this paper, we consider the general case where $M$ is defined by (cite20†1.2 ) such that the $a_{i}$’s are not necessary the same. Our emphasis is on the computation of the cycles; and for that, we need to be able to compute the possible cycle lengths efficiently. In Section 2, we give an upper bound for the cycle lengths such that all possible cycle lengths are factors of the given upper bound.
L81: The factors of the given upper bound are obtained from the induced linear systems over the finite field $\mathbb{Z}_{p}$, and thus there is no extra work needed to factor the upper bound. The results of this paper and the results in [cite17†9 ] together provide an algorithm to determine the cycles structure of a linear dynamical system over a finite commutative ring. The algorithm is given in Section 3. We consider three examples in Section 4.
L82: The first example shows that the given upper bound for the cycle lengths is sharp, and the third example is a linear system derived from a real world cancer regulatory network.
L83: ## 2. Result
L84: 
L85: We begin by collecting some general facts. The following lemma holds for any ring $R$.
L86: 
L87: ###### Lemma 2.1.
L88: 
L89: Let $M,N$ be $R$-modules, let $\varphi:M\rightarrow N$ be an onto $R$-module homomorphism, and let $K=\ker\varphi$. If $f:M\rightarrow M$ is an $R$-module endomorphism such that $f(K)\subset K$, then $f$ induces an $R$-module endomorphism $\bar{f}$ of $N$ by $\bar{f}(\varphi(m))=\varphi(f(m)),\;m\in M$.
L90: ###### Proof.
L91: 
L92: The proof is a straightforward verification of the definition. ∎
L93: Recall that we work with a finite commutative ring $R$ and a finite $R$-module, so it is possible to use a reduction approach to find the cycles of an $R$-module endomorphism $f$. For this purpose, we investigate the relationships among the cycles of the $f$-modules $M$, $N$, and $K$ (notation as in Lemma 2). For any $m\in M$, we denote by $\ell(m)$ the length of the cycle generated by $m$ (if $m$ lies on a cycle). We have the following simple fact about fixed points:
L94: ###### Proposition 2.1.
L95: 
L96: Notation as in Lemma cite21†2.1 . Let $v$ be a fixed point of $N$ and let $u$ be a fixed point of $M$ such that $\varphi(u)=v$. Then $u_{1}$ is a fixed point of $M$ such that $\varphi(u_{1})=v$ if and only if $u_{1}=u+w$ for a fixed point $w\in K$. In particular, the number of fixed points $u$ of $M$ such that $\varphi(u)=v$ is $0$ or equal to the number of fixed points of $K$.

L98:
