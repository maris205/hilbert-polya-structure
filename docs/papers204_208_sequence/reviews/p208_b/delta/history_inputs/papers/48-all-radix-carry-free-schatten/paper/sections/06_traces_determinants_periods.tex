\section{Traces, determinants, and temporal support}
\label{sec:dynamics}

\subsection{The diagonal trace}

A diagonal entry is present precisely when a positive integer is
carry-free with itself.  Define
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D_b
  =
  \{
    m\ge1:
    \text{every base-\(b\) digit of \(m\) is at most }
    \lfloor\frac{b-1}{2}\rfloor
  \}.
  \label{eq:loop-digit-set}
\end{equation}
The all-zero digit word would satisfy the digit restriction, but it is
deleted because the source alphabet is \(\N\).

\begin{proposition}[Trace and its domain]
\label{prop:diagonal-trace}
If \(\sigma>\alpha_b\), then
\begin{equation}
  \Tr(B_{b,s})
  =
  \sum_{m\in\mathcal D_b}m^{-s},
  \label{eq:trace-dirichlet}
\end{equation}
and the series is absolutely convergent.  In this trace-class domain, the
trace is zero for \(b=2\).  For \(b>2\), it is strictly positive on the
real half-line \(s=\sigma>\alpha_b\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{cor:operator-classes}, \(B_{b,s}\) is trace class in the stated
domain, so its trace is the absolutely convergent sum of its diagonal
entries in the standard basis.  The condition \(m\carryfree m\) is exactly
\(2m_j<b\) at every digit, which is
\cref{eq:loop-digit-set}; the diagonal weight is \(m^{-s}\).
For \(b=2\), the only allowed digit is zero, so no positive vertex remains
and \(\mathcal D_2=\varnothing\).  For \(b>2\), the vertex \(1\) is in
\(\mathcal D_b\), and every term is positive when \(s=\sigma\) is real.
\end{proof}

The statement that the binary trace is zero in
\cref{prop:diagonal-trace} is an operator-trace statement only for
\(\sigma>\alpha_2\).  Outside that domain, the diagonal or loop support is
still empty, but we do not thereby define an operator trace.  Likewise,
positivity of the real Dirichlet series gives no zero-free statement for
nonreal \(s\).

\subsection{Trace powers as absolutely convergent walk sums}

Let a \emph{based closed \(r\)-walk} be a tuple
\((n_1,\ldots,n_r)\in\N^r\) satisfying
\[
  n_i\carryfree n_{i+1},
  \qquad i\pmod r,
\]
where \(n_{r+1}=n_1\).  The base point matters, so cyclic rotations are
not identified.

\begin{proposition}[Trace-power expansion]
\label{prop:trace-powers}
If \(\sigma>1\) and \(r\ge2\), then \(B_{b,s}^r\in S_1\) and
\begin{equation}
  \Tr(B_{b,s}^r)
  =
  \sum_{\substack{n_1,\ldots,n_r\ge1\\
                  n_i\carryfree n_{i+1}\ (i\bmod r)}}
  \prod_{i=1}^{r}n_i^{-s}.
  \label{eq:closed-walk-trace}
\end{equation}
The series on the right is absolutely convergent.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{cor:operator-classes}, \(B_{b,s}\in S_2\) and is bounded.  Hence
\(B_{b,s}^2\in S_1\), and multiplying by bounded powers shows
\(B_{b,s}^r\in S_1\) for every \(r\ge2\).

Let \(Q_K=\sum_{k=0}^{K}P_k\) and
\(T_K=Q_KB_{b,s}Q_K\).  Because \(B_{b,s}\) is Hilbert--Schmidt and
\(Q_K\) increases strongly to the identity,
\[
  \|T_K-B_{b,s}\|_{S_2}\longrightarrow0.
\]
For \(r=2\), the ideal inequality gives
\[
  \|T_K^2-B_{b,s}^2\|_{S_1}
  \le
  (\|T_K\|_{S_2}+\|B_{b,s}\|_{S_2})
  \|T_K-B_{b,s}\|_{S_2}.
\]
For \(r>2\), a telescoping expansion followed by bounded multiplication
gives the same convergence in \(S_1\):
\begin{equation}
  T_K^r\longrightarrow B_{b,s}^r
  \quad\text{in }S_1.
  \label{eq:power-trace-norm-limit}
\end{equation}
Thus the finite traces converge to the operator trace.

For each \(K\), direct finite matrix multiplication yields
\[
  \Tr(T_K^r)
  =
  \sum_{\substack{n_1,\ldots,n_r<b^{K+1}\\
                  n_i\carryfree n_{i+1}\ (i\bmod r)}}
  \prod_{i=1}^{r}n_i^{-s}.
\]
To justify passage through the infinite sum, repeat the construction with
the real matrix \(B_{b,\sigma}\).  Every term is nonnegative, the finite
walk sums increase with \(K\), and
\cref{eq:power-trace-norm-limit} shows that their traces converge to the
finite number \(\Tr(B_{b,\sigma}^r)\).  Monotone convergence therefore
gives
\[
  \sum_{\text{based closed \(r\)-walks}}
  \prod_{i=1}^{r}n_i^{-\sigma}<\infty.
\]
This is exactly the sum of the absolute values of the complex-\(s\) terms.
The complex finite sums may consequently pass term by term to the
absolutely convergent limit, and their trace-norm limit identifies that
limit with \(\Tr(B_{b,s}^r)\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Regularized and ordinary determinants}

\begin{corollary}[Determinant domains]
\label{cor:determinant-domains}
For \(\sigma>1\), the Hilbert--Schmidt regularized determinant
\[
  \Dettwo(I-zB_{b,s})
\]
is an entire function of \(z\).  On a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\(z=0\), with the logarithm normalized to vanish at zero,
\begin{equation}
  \log\Dettwo(I-zB_{b,s})
  =
  -\sum_{r=2}^{\infty}
  \frac{z^r}{r}\Tr(B_{b,s}^r),
  \label{eq:det2-local-log}
\end{equation}
and each coefficient has the absolutely convergent walk expansion in
\cref{eq:closed-walk-trace}.  The ordinary trace and Fredholm determinant
\(\det(I-zB_{b,s})\) are asserted only for
\(\sigma>\alpha_b\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For an \(S_2\) operator, the regularized determinant
\(\Dettwo(I-zB)=\det((I-zB)\exp(zB))\) is entire in \(z\); this is the
standard trace-ideal construction \cite{simon2005trace}.  For, say,
\(|z|\|B_{b,s}\|<1\), the norm-convergent logarithm and
\cref{prop:trace-powers} give \cref{eq:det2-local-log}.  The ordinary
Fredholm determinant requires \(B_{b,s}\in S_1\), whose exact domain is
\(\sigma>\alpha_b\) by \cref{cor:operator-classes}.
\end{proof}

We make no claim that \cref{eq:det2-local-log} converges for all \(z\), even
though the determinant itself is entire in \(z\).  Nor do we construct a
completed function of \(s\), a functional equation, or a prescribed
divisor.

\subsection{Least periods of the graph shift}

Let
\[
  \Sigma_b
  =
  \{(x_j)_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\in\N^{\mathbb N_0}:
    x_j\carryfree x_{j+1}\text{ for every }j\in\mathbb N_0\}
\]
with the one-sided left shift.  Repeating a cyclically admissible
period-\(r\) word to the right gives a periodic point, and every periodic
point arises this way; its least period is its least shift period.  We
discuss support only, independently of the complex weights.

\begin{proposition}[Least-period support]
\label{prop:least-periods}
The least-period sets are
\begin{equation}
  \LPS(\Sigma_2)=\{2,3,\ldots\},
  \qquad
  \LPS(\Sigma_b)=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\quad(b>2).
  \label{eq:least-period-sets}
\end{equation}
For every allowed length, there are infinitely many periodic points.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Distinct powers of \(b\) are pairwise carry-free because their nonzero
digits occupy distinct positions.  At \(b=2\), no positive vertex has a
loop: any nonzero binary digit produces a carry when added to itself.
Two distinct powers give a cyclic word of least period two.  For each
\(r\ge3\), a word of \(r\) distinct powers is cyclically admissible and
cannot have a smaller period because all of its symbols are distinct.

For \(b>2\), the vertex \(1\) has a loop, providing least period one, and
the same distinct-power construction gives every \(r\ge2\).  Finally,
shifting every chosen digit position upward by an arbitrary common amount
produces infinitely many distinct witnesses of the same least period.
\end{proof}

It follows that the unweighted fixed-point count is infinite at every
allowed length (for \(b=2\), at every length at least two).  Therefore the
usual Artin--Mazur coefficients are not finite and no unweighted
Artin--Mazur zeta function is claimed.  The period statement is proved from
support witnesses, not inferred from traces that could cancel for complex
\(s\).
