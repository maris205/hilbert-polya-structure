\section{Exponent rectangles and boundary laws}\label{sec:rectangles}

Arithmetic prefixes intersect many root components irregularly.  An exponent
rectangle instead stays inside one component and retains a Cartesian set of
valuation coordinates.  Fix $r\in\cR_{a,b}$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rectangle}
 Q_r(M,N)=\{r a^i b^j:0\leq i<M,\ 0\leq j<N\},
 \qquad M,N\geq1.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Exact exponent-box law]\label{prop:rectangle}
For every $M,N\geq1$,
\begin{align}
 \dim\proj_{Q_r(M,N)}(X_{a,b})&=M+N-1,
 \label{eq:rectangle-dimension}\\
 |\proj_{Q_r(M,N)}(X_{a,b})|&=q^{M+N-1},
 \label{eq:rectangle-count}\\
 \beta(Q_r(M,N))&=(M-1)(N-1).
 \label{eq:rectangle-beta}
\end{align}
Under normalized Haar measure,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rectangle-haar}
 \Ent_\mu(Z_{Q_r(M,N)})=(M+N-1)\log q,
 \qquad
 \TC_\mu(Z_{Q_r(M,N)})=(M-1)(N-1)\log q.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The graph $G_r(Q_r(M,N))$ contains all edges between its $M$ row vertices and
$N$ column vertices.  It is the connected complete bipartite graph
$K_{M,N}$.  Its graphic rank is $M+N-1$, while its cycle rank is
\[
 MN-(M+N)+1=(M-1)(N-1).
\]
Apply \Cref{cor:graphic-matroid,prop:haar}.
\end{proof}

The pattern exponent has boundary rather than area order:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rectangle-area-rate}
 \frac{1}{MN}\log|\proj_{Q_r(M,N)}(X_{a,b})|
 =\frac{M+N-1}{MN}\log q.
\end{equation}
In particular, \eqref{eq:rectangle-area-rate} tends to zero whenever both
$M$ and $N$ diverge.  This vanishing reflects the factorization into row and
column potentials.  It does not contradict the positive arithmetic-prefix
rate in \eqref{eq:prefix-rate}; the two sequences sample different portions
of the product coordinates.

\begin{corollary}[Rectangles on distinct roots]\label{cor:root-rectangles}
Let $r_1,\ldots,r_s$ be distinct roots and let
\[
 F=\bigsqcup_{t=1}^s Q_{r_t}(M_t,N_t).
\]
Then
\begin{align*}
 \dim\proj_F(X_{a,b})
 &=\sum_{t=1}^s(M_t+N_t-1),\
 \beta(F)&=\sum_{t=1}^s(M_t-1)(N_t-1).
\end{align*}
The corresponding Haar random vectors are independent across the roots.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Distinct roots belong to distinct factors in the product
\eqref{eq:global-product}.  Their incidence matrices and finite Haar images
form direct products, so dimensions, cycle ranks, and entropies add.
\end{proof}

The same calculation applies to nonrectangular shapes without new extension
arguments.  We make the one-edge law explicit.

\begin{corollary}[One-edge rank update]\label{cor:edge-update}
Let $F\subset\N$ be finite.  If $n\in F$ and its edge lies on a cycle of its
root graph, then
\[
 d(F\setminus\{n\})=d(F),\qquad
 \beta(F\setminus\{n\})=\beta(F)-1.
\]
If that edge is a bridge, then
\[
 d(F\setminus\{n\})=d(F)-1,\qquad
 \beta(F\setminus\{n\})=\beta(F).
\]
Conversely, let $n=r a^i b^j\notin F$.  If its two endpoints already lie in
the same connected component of $G_r(F)$, then adjoining $n$ preserves $d$
and increases $\beta$ by one.  In every other case, adjoining $n$ increases
$d$ by one and preserves $\beta$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
An edge on a cycle is dependent on the remaining cycle edges, so its deletion
preserves rank; a bridge belongs to every maximal spanning forest, so its
deletion lowers rank by one.
The deletion formulas for $\beta=|F|-d$ follow.  An added edge is dependent
exactly when its endpoints were already connected.  This proves the two
addition formulas.
\end{proof}

Thus the arbitrary-shape theorem records more than the number of rows and
columns: it tracks precisely which missing cells destroy which dependencies.
