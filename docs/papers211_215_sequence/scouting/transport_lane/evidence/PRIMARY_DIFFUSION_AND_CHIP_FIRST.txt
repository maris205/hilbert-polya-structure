L7@P0: We introduce a natural variant of the parallel chip-firing game, called the diffusion game. Chips are initially assigned
L8@P0: to vertices of a graph. At every step, all vertices simultaneously send one chip to each neighbour with fewer chips.
L9@P0: As the dynamics of the parallel chip-firing game occur on a finite set the process is inherently periodic. However the
L10@P0: diffusion game is not obviously periodic: even if 2|E(G)| chips are assigned to vertices of graph G, there may exist
L11@P0: time steps where some vertices have a negative number of chips. We investigate the process, prove periodicity for a
L12@P0: number of graph classes, and pose some questions for future research.
L13@P0: Keywords: chip-firing, discrete-time graph processes
L14@P0: 1 Introduction
L15@P0: Each vertex of a graph is assigned a finite integral number of chips. The chips are then redistributed via a
L16@P0: stepwise parallel process where at each step all vertices simultaneously send one chip to each neighbour
L17@P0: with fewer chips. If the chips are thought of as molecules, then this process models diffusion: the movement of molecules from regions of high concentration to regions of low concentration. As a result, the
L18@P0: process described above is termed the diffusion game(i). A natural first question is whether the process is
L19@P0: necessarily periodic.
L20@P0: The diffusion game is akin to the Discharging Method, wherein a set of charges, rather than chips,
L21@P0: are assigned to vertices and then moved around the graph according to a set of rules. The Discharging
L22@P0: Method is a technique best known for its use in the proof of the famous Four Colour Theorem, but has
L23@P0: been used in graph theoretic proofs for over a century. An overview of this technique is given by Cranston
L24@P0: and West (2016). The diffusion game is also a natural variation of the well-studied Chip-Firing Game.
L25@P0: Chip-firing games on graphs provide a curious area of study for both their mathematical connections

L37@P1: the Tutte polynomial, critical groups of graphs, stochastic processes and graph cleaning (see Gaspers
L38@P1: et al. (2009, 2010); Kayll and Perkins (2013) and Messinger et al. (2008)). In the (sequential) chip-firing
L39@P1: game (see Bjorner et al. (1991)), every vertex in a graph is initially assigned a non-negative integral ¨
L40@P1: number of chips. At each step, one vertex with at least degree-many chips is “fired”; whereupon it
L41@P1: sends one chip to each of its neighbours. This firing of vertices continues as long as there is a vertex
L42@P1: with at least degree-many chips. The parallel chip-firing game has been studied by Bitar and Goles
L43@P1: (1992); Kiwi et al. (1994) and Levine (2011): at each step, every vertex with at least degree-many
L44@P1: chips is fired simultaneously. It is easy to see that in both the parallel chip-firing game and the diffusion
L45@P1: game, the process is completely determined by the initial distribution of chips. Hence, the term “game”
L46@P1: is a misnomer; however we refer to this new variation as the diffusion “game” because of this close
L47@P1: relationship to the parallel chip-firing game. Since the total number of chips C remains constant and it
L48@P1: is not possible for a vertex to have a negative number of chips, the dynamics for parallel chip-firing on
L49@P1: a graph G occur in the finite set {0, 1, . . . , C}|V (G)|and so parallel chip-firing games eventually exhibit
L50@P1: periodic behaviour. A number of papers have studied the periodicity in parallel chip-firing and results
L51@P1: are known about the periods of trees, cycles, complete graphs, and complete bipartite graphs (see Bitar
L52@P1: and Goles (1992); Dall’Asta (2006); Kiwi et al. (1994); Kominers and Kominers (2010); Jiang (2010) and
L53@P1: Levine (2011)). It is important to note that although Bitar and Goles (1992) conjectured that on a graph G
L54@P1: any chip configuration is eventually periodic with period at most |V (G)|, Kiwi et al. (1994) provided an
L55@P1: example of a graph with period exponential in |V (G)|.

L167@P3: Given any finite graph and fixed number C of chips, we can consider an auxiliary directed graph A(G, C)
L168@P3: that encodes the chip configurations. Each vertex of A(G, C) is a chip configuration where the total
L169@P3: number of chips is C. For a pair of vertices u, v ∈ V (A(G, C)), there is an arc from u to v if applying the
L170@P3: dynamics of the chip configuration corresponding to u yields the configuration at v. Observe that since the
L171@P3: process is deterministic, the out-degree of any vertex is 1, but the in-degree can be arbitrary. Consider P3,
L172@P3: for example, and let ct = (ct(x), ct(y), ct(z)) where deg(y) = 2. If ct = (1, 1, 2) then ct−1 could
L173@P3: have been (0, 1, 3), (0, 2, 2), or (0, 3, 1); thus in the auxiliary graph A(P3, 4), the vertex corresponding
L174@P3: to configuration (1, 1, 2) has three parent vertices. Note that Question 1 is equivalent to asking if every
L175@P3: directed path in A(G, C) is finite. Conjecture 1 is equivalent to saying that each strongly connected
L176@P3: component of A(G, C) has cardinality 1 or 2.
L177@P3: 2 General Periodicity Results
L178@P3: In this section, we affirm Question 1 for some well-known classes of graphs. To do this, we show that
L179@P3: the values for ct = (ct(v1), ct(v2), . . . , ct(vn)) are in a finite set. In other words, we show for every
L180@P3: integer t ≥ 0, the number of chips on any vertex in such graphs is always in a finite interval. As there
L181@P3: will be a finite number of distinct configurations in the process and ct+1 is determined by ct, and as the
L182@P3: diffusion game continues indefinitely, the configurations must eventually exhibit periodic behaviour. This
L183@P3: approach will be used for paths, cycles, wheels, complete graphs, and complete bipartite graphs. We begin
L184@P3: with a few definitions and preliminary results in Section 2.1.
