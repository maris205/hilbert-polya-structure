\title{\textbf{Three-Mode Hamiltonian Shears in \(\A^6\):}\\
Exact Degree Growth and Cubic Perron Subfamilies}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We prove an exact degree formula for a fixed family of canonical polynomial
automorphisms of six-dimensional affine space.  Let \(\K\) be algebraically
closed of characteristic zero, let \(g\geq 8\), and compose the two
triangular Hamiltonian shears generated by
\[
\Vpoly(q)=q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2+q_1^g,
\qquad
\Wpoly(p)=p_1^2p_2^2p_3^2+p_3^g.
\]
The mixed supports force a genuinely coupled two-phase degree calculation.
We show that the first shear selects the degree-transfer matrix
\(\Ag\), the second selects \(\Bg\), and the complete step is governed by
\(\Cg=\Bg\Ag\), not by either half-step matrix alone.  A strict cone in the
ratio variables \(x=u_2/u_1\), \(y=u_3/u_1\) is invariant under \(\Cg\);
strict old-term gaps and positive coefficients in characteristic zero prevent
cancellation at every iterate.  The third \(q\)-coordinate sees the Perron
class, so
\[
\degmap(\Fg^n)=\ethree^{\mathsf T}\Cg^n\1,\qquad
\lambda_1(\Fg)=\rhoPF(\Cg).
\]
The characteristic polynomial of \(\Cg\) is cubic, and a mod-five residue
audit yields infinite cubic Perron subfamilies for \(g\equiv 2,3,4 \pmod 5\).
The theorem is exact, proof-first, and bounded to this displayed family; no
genericity, priority, or classification claim is made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Polynomial automorphisms can hide their degree-growth mechanisms inside a
composition word.  In a product-like setting, the degree of a composition may
reflect only the visible degrees of the factors.  In a genuinely coupled
setting, however, the selected monomials in one step can alter the winner in the
next step, and the complete degree recurrence may only appear after two phases.
This paper isolates one such coupled but still fully explicit family.

We work over an algebraically closed field \(\K\) of characteristic zero and
study the map \(\Fg=T\circ S\) on \(\A^6\), where \(q=(q_1,q_2,q_3)\) and
\(p=(p_1,p_2,p_3)\).  The displayed potentials are
\[
\Vpoly(q)=q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2+q_1^g,\qquad
\Wpoly(p)=p_1^2p_2^2p_3^2+p_3^g.
\]
They are asymmetric in exactly one pure-power row each.  The asymmetry is
deliberate: the first shear should push weight into the \(p\)-coordinates, and
the second shear should pull that weight back into the \(q\)-coordinates.

The exact theorem is the following.

\begin{theorem}[Main theorem]\label{thm:main}
Let \(\K\) be algebraically closed of characteristic zero and let
\(g\geq 8\) be an integer.  Define
\[
S(q,p)=(q,p+\grad \Vpoly(q)),\qquad
T(q,p)=(q+\grad \Wpoly(p),p),\qquad
\Fg=T\circ S.
\]
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(S\) and \(T\) are polynomial automorphisms with subtraction inverses,
  and \(\Fg\) is symplectic for the standard form on \(\A^6\).
\item The selected phase matrices are
  \[
  \Ag=\begin{pmatrix}g-1&0&0\\2&1&2\\2&2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \Bg=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\2&1&2\\0&0&g-1\end{pmatrix},
  \]
  and the complete-step matrix is \(\Cg=\Bg\Ag\).
\item On the strict cone
  \[
  \cK_g=\{u\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3:\;x=u_2/u_1\geq 1,\ y=u_3/u_1\geq 1,\ x+y<(g-3)/2\},
  \]
  there are exactly two selector gaps,
  \[
  \MS=(g-2)-2x-2y,\qquad
  \MT=(2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6,
  \]
  and no third face occurs.
\item The cone is \(\Cg\)-invariant, the phase/carry induction gives
  \(v_{n+1}=\Ag u_n\) and \(u_{n+1}=\Cg u_n\), and positive coefficients in
  characteristic zero prevent cancellation of the selected support.
\item The third \(q\)-coordinate is visible and maximal, so
  \[
  \degmap(\Fg^n)=\ethree^{\mathsf T}\Cg^n\1 \quad (n\geq 0).
  \]
  Consequently \(\lambda_1(\Fg)=\rhoPF(\Cg)\), and \(\rhoPF(\Cg)<(g-1)^2\).
\item The characteristic polynomial is
  \[
  \chi_{\Cg}(t)=t^3-(2g+11)t^2+(g^2-16g+19)t-9(g-1)^2,
  \]
  and for \(g\equiv 2,3,4 \pmod 5\) the mod-five reduction is irreducible,
  yielding infinite cubic Perron subfamilies.
\item The case \(g=7\) is a strict-cone boundary with no selector tie.  No
  genericity, entropy, universal non-conjugacy, priority, or CAS-based proof
  certificate is claimed.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The theorem is intentionally linear.  It does not branch into an alternative
support analysis, a half-step degree observable, or a numerical side
computation.  The proof first certifies that the two displayed shears are
canonical polynomial automorphisms, then freezes the complete support ledger,
then proves that exactly two selector margins govern every iterate, and only
after those steps turns to the exact degree row and the arithmetic corollary.
This order is not stylistic.  If the support rows were allowed to drift before
the selector analysis, or if the degree observable were guessed before the
visibility proof, the statement would no longer be auditable from the displayed
family alone.

What makes the family genuinely coupled is that neither phase carries the final
degree observable by itself.  The first shear creates a \(p\)-degree vector
whose dominant row is only an intermediate support signal, and the second shear
then reads that signal through a different support ledger before returning to
the \(q\)-coordinates.  If one stopped after the first phase, the matrix
\(\Ag\) would describe only a transient support transfer.  If one jumped
directly to a guessed scalar recurrence, the proof would lose the mechanism
that certifies which support row actually survives the iteration.  The chain
\[
u_n \xrightarrow{\;S\;} v_{n+1} \xrightarrow{\;T\;} u_{n+1}
\]
is therefore not a stylistic decomposition; it is the minimal bookkeeping that
keeps the support selection, the old-term dominance, and the final degree
observable logically separate.

This also explains why the full-step matrix \(\Cg=\Bg\Ag\) enters only after
the selector proof.  The entries of \(\Ag\) and \(\Bg\) are read row by row
from the displayed supports, whereas the cone invariance, the visibility
argument, and the Perron root belong to the composed matrix \(\Cg\).  The proof
would be circular if it invoked Perron positivity before establishing that the
same two selector faces are chosen at every iterate.  The manuscript therefore
first proves that the selected support is stable and only then uses the
positivity of \(\Cg\) to turn that stable support into an exact recurrence.

The paper contributes four things, all internal to the fixed family:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an explicit six-dimensional canonical gradient shear pair with a complete
  support ledger and symplectic block check;
\item an exact two-face selector proof, including the corrected \(M_T\) and the
  \(g=8,\dots,11\) versus \(g\geq 12\) cone split;
\item a phase/carry induction that turns selected support into the exact degree
  row; and
\item a cubic characteristic polynomial whose mod-five reduction yields
  infinite degree-three Perron subfamilies.
\end{enumerate}

The proof itself moves through four phases.  First, Section~\ref{sec:setup}
freezes the maps, their inverses, the block Jacobians, and the support rows.
Second, Section~\ref{sec:selectors} proves that only two selector inequalities
matter and that the normalized cone is invariant under the full-step matrix.
Third, Sections~\ref{sec:induction} and~\ref{sec:visibility} turn those strict
inequalities into a phase-labeled recurrence and then into the exact degree
row.  Fourth, Section~\ref{sec:cubic} extracts the cubic characteristic
polynomial and checks the mod-five residue classes one by one.  No theorem
critical step is moved to an appendix.

This exactness is the central point of the paper.  The theorem is not an
asymptotic growth statement, because the recurrence is not estimated from above
or below in the large-\(n\) limit.  Instead, every iterate is described by the
same matrix recurrence once the cone is entered.  Likewise, the Perron root
corollary is not a generic spectral bound; it is the spectral radius of one
explicit \(3\times 3\) matrix with a fully written characteristic polynomial.
That is why the theorem is proof-first rather than heuristic: the two selected
faces and the one visible coordinate are enough to determine all later iterates.

Two exclusions should be explicit before the paper proceeds.  The first is a
class exclusion: the theorem concerns one exact Hamiltonian gradient-shear
family, not the whole affine-triangular world.  The second is a proof-mode
exclusion: nothing below appeals to CAS output, numerical iteration, or a
finite search certificate.  Every formula used in the proof is written in the
main body and can be checked directly from the displayed supports.

\section{Bounded related work and scope boundary}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Affine-triangular terminology.}
The larger affine-triangular comparison class is a convenient language for
degree-growth problems, but it is not the class studied here.  Our maps are
canonical gradient shears with symmetric Hessian blocks, and the distinction
matters because the symplectic proof is tied to that exact structure
\citep{BlancVanSanten2019}.  More concretely, the present argument needs the
Jacobian blocks to be lower- and upper-unitriangular with Hessian entries in
the off-diagonal blocks.  That feature is automatic for gradient shears and is
what makes the six-by-six symplectic identity a formal calculation.  We cite
this source only as a terminology boundary; the theorem does not transfer from
it, and no result from that paper is imported as a substitute for the support
analysis below.

The distinction also matters at the level of proof architecture.  In a general
affine-triangular setting, one often proves degree growth by tracking a family
of possible leading terms and then showing that one family eventually dominates
the rest.  Here the argument is sharper: the support family is fixed in advance,
the cone is two-dimensional after normalization, and the only degree choices are
the two selector gaps \(\MS\) and \(\MT\).  That is why the paper does not need
the broader machinery usually associated with affine-triangular dynamics.  The
proof is smaller in scope but more explicit in structure.

\paragraph{Four-dimensional neighboring context.}
The recent dimension-four affine-triangular algebraic-degree context does not
address this specific degree problem \citep{ShaoSun2025}.  It is useful here
only as a dimension boundary for terminology: in the present paper the degree
transfer is governed by a \(3\times 3\) matrix, the cone has a two-parameter
normalization \((x,y)\), and the characteristic polynomial is genuinely
cubic.  Those three features separate the current family from that
four-dimensional context.  The citation is therefore contextual rather than
foundational; it supplies no symplectic, selector, recurrence, or proof input
for the theorem below.

There is also a conceptual difference in what the polynomial data are doing.
The neighboring four-dimensional context studies a different algebraic-degree
pattern in a different ambient space.  Here the three-mode family is chosen so
that a single pure-power row is balanced against a mixed sextic row in each
phase.  That balance is what creates the exact \(\Ag\), \(\Bg\), and \(\Cg\)
chain, and it is what makes the cubic Perron corollary possible after the
mod-five audit.  The paper is therefore not a dimension lift of the neighboring
result; it is a different exact family with a different proof spine.

\paragraph{Bounded novelty wording.}
The novelty wording is intentionally restrained.  The present manuscript makes
no global priority claim and no exhaustive classification claim.  What matters
for the proof is the exact support pattern written in the displayed family:
three \(q\)-modes, three \(p\)-modes, one mixed sextic monomial in each
potential, and one pure-power row in opposite phases.  If a future comparison
refined the surrounding novelty language, the theorem below would remain the
same because it depends only on the explicit gradients, matrices, cone
inequalities, and residue checks written in the main body.

This bounded wording is not evasive; it is part of the proof discipline.  It
prevents several false enlargements at once.  The present paper is not a
residue-theoretic paper, not a moduli or ramification paper, not a torus-escape
paper, and not a generic dimension-lift theorem.  It is one exact six-variable
Hamiltonian family with one exact recurrence.

The same restraint governs the shape of the related-work section itself.  A
context citation is useful here only if it marks a boundary of the claim: what
comparison class is being excluded, or what neighboring lower-dimensional
phenomenon serves merely as orientation.  Once those two tasks are done,
additional citation accumulation would not prove a single selector inequality,
would not change the support ledger, and would not strengthen the Perron
corollary.  In a proof-first manuscript, the relevant question is therefore not
how broad a survey can be attached to the theorem, but which exact enlargements
of the theorem must be ruled out before the symbolic argument begins.

The hard anti-claims can therefore be stated before the proof begins:
\begin{itemize}
\item no genericity statement for arbitrary Hamiltonian shears, arbitrary
  dimensions, arbitrary support words, or positive characteristic;
\item no entropy, periodic-point, trace, torus, conjugacy, or classification
  theorem;
\item no absolute priority, firstness, or optimality claim; and
\item no empirical, numerical, or CAS-produced certificate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exact family, gradients, inverses, symplectic blocks, and support ledger}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The maps and their inverses}\label{sec:maps}

We write the phase variables as \((q,p)\in \K^3\times \K^3\).  The two
canonical shears are
\[
S(q,p)=(q,p+\grad \Vpoly(q)),\qquad
T(q,p)=(q+\grad \Wpoly(p),p).
\]
The family is deliberately asymmetric.  The pure power \(q_1^g\) appears only
in the first potential, while \(p_3^g\) appears only in the second.  This
placement means that the potentially dominant pure row of the first shear is in
the \(q_1\)-slot, whereas the potentially dominant pure row of the second shear
is in the \(p_3\)-slot.  The degree mechanism is therefore not visible inside a
single triangular map; it only appears after the support created by the first
shear is fed into the second.
Their explicit gradients are
\begin{align}
\grad \Vpoly
&=
\bigl(2q_1q_2^2q_3^2+gq_1^{g-1},\;2q_1^2q_2q_3^2,\;2q_1^2q_2^2q_3\bigr),\\
\grad \Wpoly
&=
\bigl(2p_1p_2^2p_3^2,\;2p_1^2p_2p_3^2,\;2p_1^2p_2^2p_3+gp_3^{g-1}\bigr).
\end{align}
The corresponding Hessian matrices, which will be used only through their
symmetry, are
\[
H_V=
\begin{pmatrix}
2q_2^2q_3^2+g(g-1)q_1^{g-2} & 4q_1q_2q_3^2 & 4q_1q_2^2q_3\\
4q_1q_2q_3^2 & 2q_1^2q_3^2 & 4q_1^2q_2q_3\\
4q_1q_2^2q_3 & 4q_1^2q_2q_3 & 2q_1^2q_2^2
\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
H_W=
\begin{pmatrix}
2P_2^2P_3^2 & 4P_1P_2P_3^2 & 4P_1P_2^2P_3\\
4P_1P_2P_3^2 & 2P_1^2P_3^2 & 4P_1^2P_2P_3\\
4P_1P_2^2P_3 & 4P_1^2P_2P_3 & 2P_1^2P_2^2+g(g-1)P_3^{g-2}
\end{pmatrix},
\]
where \(P=(P_1,P_2,P_3)=p+\grad\Vpoly(q)\) is the intermediate \(p\)-vector.
Displaying these Hessians makes the block computation completely transparent:
the only reason the symplectic identity works is that the off-diagonal blocks
come from symmetric second-derivative matrices.
The displayed formulas also explain why the later degree proof has exactly two
competitive rows and not more.  In \(H_V\), every off-diagonal entry comes from
the mixed sextic \(q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2\), while only the \((1,1)\)-entry carries
the additional pure-power contribution \(g(g-1)q_1^{g-2}\).  In \(H_W\), the
same asymmetry is mirrored in the opposite corner: the mixed sextic fills the
matrix, and only the \((3,3)\)-entry receives the extra term
\(g(g-1)P_3^{g-2}\).  Thus the first-derivative competition between one mixed
row and one pure row is already encoded at the second-derivative level.  The
block symplectic calculation and the later selector calculation are therefore
different views of the same structural asymmetry, not unrelated steps that
happen to use the same polynomials.
The inverses are the subtraction inverses
\[
S^{-1}(q,p)=(q,p-\grad \Vpoly(q)),\qquad
T^{-1}(q,p)=(q-\grad \Wpoly(p),p).
\]
No denominator, birational correction, or analytic continuation appears.
Thus every later degree calculation takes place in the polynomial category from
start to finish.  In particular, there is no hidden birational simplification
that could alter the support rows or insert exceptional cancellation
phenomena.

\begin{lemma}[Canonicality]\label{lem:canonical}
The maps \(S\) and \(T\) are polynomial automorphisms of \(\A^6\), and
\(\Fg=T\circ S\) preserves the standard symplectic form.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The triangular inverses follow immediately from the explicit formulas above.
Write the Jacobians in block form using the Hessians \(H_V=\grad^2\Vpoly(q)\)
and \(H_W=\grad^2\Wpoly(P)\), where \(P=p+\grad \Vpoly(q)\) is the
intermediate \(p\)-coordinate after applying \(S\):
\[
J_S=\begin{pmatrix}I_3&0\\H_V&I_3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
J_T=\begin{pmatrix}I_3&H_W\\0&I_3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both Hessians are symmetric because they are Hessians of scalar polynomials.
This symmetry is the only structural input needed for the symplectic check.
With the standard symplectic matrix
\(\Omega=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I_3\\-I_3&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\),
one first computes
\[
\Omega J_S=
\begin{pmatrix}
H_V&I_3\\
-I_3&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Omega J_T=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&I_3\\
-I_3&-H_W
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Multiplying on the left by the transposes gives
\[
J_S^{\mathsf T}\Omega J_S
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&H_V^{\mathsf T}\\
0&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
H_V&I_3\\
-I_3&0
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
H_V-H_V^{\mathsf T}&I_3\\
-I_3&0
\end{pmatrix}
=\Omega
\]
and similarly
\[
J_T^{\mathsf T}\Omega J_T
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&0\\
H_W^{\mathsf T}&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
0&I_3\\
-I_3&-H_W
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&I_3\\
-I_3&H_W^{\mathsf T}-H_W
\end{pmatrix}
=\Omega.
\]
Hence \(J_{\Fg}=J_TJ_S\) is symplectic.  The proof is independent of the later
cone and selector arguments: canonicality is already forced by the gradient
form of the two shears.
\[
J_{\Fg}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&H_W\\
0&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3&0\\
H_V&I_3
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_3+H_WH_V&H_W\\
H_V&I_3
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The product shows at a glance that the full map remains polynomial and that the
upper-right block is exactly the Hessian block governing the second shear.  It
also gives \(\det J_{\Fg}=1\), the determinant shadow of symplecticity.
\end{proof}

\((q_1,q_2,q_3,p_1,p_2,p_3)\).  They are the audit trail for every selected
matrix entry.

rows without a selector competition are what make the final recurrence matrix a
primitive Perron object.  There is also a complementary negative statement: no
hidden support row remains offstage to create a third selector face after
composition.  Every exponent vector used later in the proof is already present
in Table~\ref{tab:support}, and every weighted-degree comparison is a linear
combination of those displayed rows.

Reading Table~\ref{tab:scores} row by row clarifies why the mixed rows are not
merely lower-order decoration.  In the second and third \(S\)-rows the mixed

just as decisively as the pure \(q_1^{g-1}\) row.  The same is true for the
first two \(T\)-rows.  The selector analysis is therefore not saying that mixed
terms disappear; it is saying something narrower and more informative, namely
that among the six displayed rows only two rows contain a genuine choice, and
those two choices can be audited by explicit inequalities.

The full-step matrix \(\Cg\) is not inserted by fiat.  It is the literal
product of the two phase matrices:
\[

\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus the first row of \(\Cg\) is the sum of the first row of \(\Ag\) plus twice
the second and third rows of \(\Ag\); the second and third rows are obtained in
the same way.  This explicit multiplication matters because later inequalities
are proved for \(\Cg\) itself, not for an abstract matrix with the same
characteristic polynomial.
Table~\ref{tab:scores} makes one bookkeeping point explicit.  The \(S\)-phase
is evaluated against the \(u\)-weights, while the \(T\)-phase is evaluated
against the intermediate weights \(v=\Ag u\).  The notation prevents a common
error in two-phase degree arguments, namely using the same weight vector on both
sides of the phase boundary.  Here the boundary is real: the second phase sees
the support created by the first, not the original \(u\)-weights directly.

\begin{lemma}[Reading the transfer matrices from the support ledger]\label{lem:matrix-read}
The six support rows in Table~\ref{tab:support} determine the displayed
matrices \(\Ag\) and \(\Bg\) uniquely.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The \(S\)-phase reads the support rows of \(\grad\Vpoly\).  In the
\(\partial_{q_1}\Vpoly\) row the candidates are the mixed exponent
\((1,2,2)\) and the pure exponent \((g-1,0,0)\); the selector analysis in the
next section decides which one dominates.  In the \(\partial_{q_2}\Vpoly\) and
\(\partial_{q_3}\Vpoly\) rows there is no choice at all, because each row has a
single mixed monomial.  This yields the three rows of \(\Ag\):
\[
(g-1,0,0),\qquad (2,1,2),\qquad (2,2,1).
\]
The \(T\)-phase reads the support rows of \(\grad\Wpoly\).  The
\(\partial_{p_1}\Wpoly\) and \(\partial_{p_2}\Wpoly\) rows again have single
mixed monomials, while the \(\partial_{p_3}\Wpoly\) row has a mixed candidate
\((2,2,1)\) and a pure candidate \((0,0,g-1)\).  These are exactly the rows of
\(\Bg\).  No additional support row exists, so no other matrix can arise from
the displayed family.
\begin{itemize}
\item The first \(S\)-row is the only row with a genuine pure-vs-mixed choice.
  It is the only place where a selector gap can appear before the cone is
  imposed.
\item The second and third \(S\)-rows are rigid: their mixed monomials fix the
  second and third rows of \(\Ag\) with no ambiguity.
\item The first and second \(T\)-rows are equally rigid and therefore do not
  contribute selector faces.
\item The third \(T\)-row is the only second-phase row that can compete with a
  pure term; that competition produces \(\MT\).
\item Because every row is accounted for explicitly, the support ledger is
  exhaustive rather than schematic.
\end{itemize}
\noindent
This row-by-row reading is what makes the later degree induction honest: the
matrices are not guessed, and no hidden monomial family is being suppressed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Entrywise positivity of the selected matrices]\label{lem:matrix-positive}
For \(g\geq 8\), the matrix \(\Cg\) is entrywise positive, and both \(\Ag\)
and \(\Bg\) have nonnegative entries with positive row sums.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The entries of \(\Ag\) and \(\Bg\) are visibly nonnegative from their
definitions.  Their row sums are \((g-1), 5, 5\) and \(5, 5, g-1\)
respectively, so every row is nonzero.  The product matrix \(\Cg\) has entries
\(g+7,6,6,2g+4,5,4,2(g-1),2(g-1),g-1\), all of which are strictly positive for
\(g\geq 8\).  This is the positivity input used later in the cone and Perron
arguments.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Weighted score and selected row]\label{def:weight}
For a positive vector \(u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3\) and an exponent
vector \(\alpha=(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)\in\mathbb N^3\), define the
weighted score
\[
\wt_u(\alpha)=\alpha_1u_1+\alpha_2u_2+\alpha_3u_3.
\]
Within any support row, the selected row is the monomial whose exponent vector
has maximal weighted score.
\end{definition}

This definition is the bridge from the support ledger to the matrix recurrence.
Because multiplying \(u\) by a positive scalar multiplies every score by the
same scalar, only the ratios \(u_2/u_1\) and \(u_3/u_1\) matter for selection.
That scale-invariance is exactly why the selector problem can be reduced to the
two normalized variables \(x\) and \(y\).  In the competitive \(S\)-row one
obtains
\[
\wt_u(g-1,0,0)-\wt_u(1,2,2)=u_1\bigl((g-2)-2x-2y\bigr)=u_1\MS
\]
whereas the competitive \(T\)-row must be evaluated at the intermediate
weight \(v=\Ag u\), giving
\[
\wt_v(0,0,g-1)-\wt_v(2,2,1)
=u_1\bigl((2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6\bigr)=u_1\MT.
\]
Thus \(\MS\) and \(\MT\) are not ad hoc inequalities; they are precisely the
normalized score gaps of the only two competitive support rows, with the
phase change made explicit in the second comparison.

\begin{lemma}[Explicit normalized selector derivation]\label{lem:explicit-gaps}
Let \(u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3\) and write \(x=u_2/u_1\),
\(y=u_3/u_1\).  Then the \(S\)- and \(T\)-selector gaps arise by direct score
subtraction from the two competitive rows and from no others.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In the competitive \(S\)-row one compares the mixed exponent \((1,2,2)\) with
the pure exponent \((g-1,0,0)\).  Their scores are
\[
\wt_u(1,2,2)=u_1+2u_2+2u_3=u_1(1+2x+2y)
\]
and
\[
\wt_u(g-1,0,0)=(g-1)u_1.
\]
Subtracting gives
\[
\wt_u(g-1,0,0)-\wt_u(1,2,2)=u_1\bigl((g-2)-2x-2y\bigr)=u_1\MS.
\]
For the competitive \(T\)-row one must first pass through the selected
\(S\)-phase vector \(v=\Ag u\), so
\[
v_1=(g-1)u_1,\qquad v_2=(2+x+2y)u_1,\qquad v_3=(2+2x+y)u_1.
\]
The mixed \(T\)-candidate \((2,2,1)\) has score
\[
2v_1+2v_2+v_3=u_1\bigl(2g+4+4x+5y\bigr),
\]
while the pure \(T\)-candidate \((0,0,g-1)\) has score
\[
(g-1)v_3=u_1\bigl((2g-2)+(2g-2)x+(g-1)y\bigr).
\]
Subtracting yields
\[
(g-1)v_3-(2v_1+2v_2+v_3)
=u_1\bigl((2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6\bigr)
=u_1\MT.
\]
All other support rows are singletons, so they admit no nontrivial selector
gap.
\end{proof}

The second-phase computation is worth isolating conceptually.  The formula for
\(\MT\) is not read directly from the \(p\)-support row in isolation; it is
read from that row after the first-phase selection has already replaced the
original weights \(u\) by the intermediate weights \(v=\Ag u\).  This is the
first place where the two-phase character of the family becomes unavoidable.
The asymmetry between \(\MS\) and \(\MT\) is therefore not an accident of
notation.  It records the exact point at which the first selector influences
the second.

\section{Selector gaps and cone invariance}\label{sec:selectors}

\subsection{Two and only two selector gaps}\label{sec:two-gaps}

Let \(u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)^{\mathsf T}\) be a positive weight vector and set
\(x=u_2/u_1\), \(y=u_3/u_1\).  The comparison of the pure row \(q_1^g\) with
the mixed row \(q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2\) gives
\[
\MS=(g-2)-2x-2y.
\]
The comparison of the pure row \(p_3^g\) with the mixed row
\(p_1^2p_2^2p_3\) gives
\[
\MT=(2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6.
\]
The normalization by \(u_1\) is chosen so that the selector problem depends on
ratios rather than on the absolute scale of the degree vector.  Once \(u_1>0\)
is factored out, the mixed support comparison becomes a planar question in the
\((x,y)\)-variables.  The lower bounds \(x,y\geq 1\) encode the fact that the
second and third coordinates should not fall below the first, while the upper
bound \(x+y<(g-3)/2\) is exactly the margin needed for the pure \(q_1^g\) term
to keep beating the mixed \(q_1^2q_2^2q_3^2\) term.  This is why the cone is
written in normalized coordinates rather than as a homogeneous region in
\(\mathbb R_{>0}^3\).

The normalized cone also keeps the selector problem separate from the growth
problem.  The signs of \(\MS\) and \(\MT\) decide which support rows win inside
the individual shears.  By contrast, the positivity of \(\Cg\) and the later
Perron argument concern what happens only after those winners have already been
frozen.  Working in \((x,y)\)-coordinates prevents a logical shortcut: a face
is selected because its normalized score is larger, not because the eventual
full-step matrix turns out to have positive entries.

\begin{lemma}[Seed location]\label{lem:seed}
The seed \(u_0=\1\) belongs to \(\cK_g\) for every \(g\geq 8\).  For \(g=7\),
the same seed lies on the boundary \(x+y=(g-3)/2\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For \(u_0=\1\) one has \(x=y=1\).  Thus \(x,y\geq 1\) always.  If \(g\geq 8\),
then
\[
x+y=2<\frac{g-3}{2},
\]
so \(u_0\in\cK_g\).  If \(g=7\), then \(x+y=2=(g-3)/2\), so the seed lies on
the boundary rather than in the open cone.
\end{proof}

The ledger in Table~\ref{tab:selector} separates two different tasks.  The
quantities \(\MS\) and \(\MT\) decide which support row wins inside a fixed
shear.  The quantities \(X'-1\), \(Y'-1\), and \(H_2\) belong to the full-step
matrix and decide whether the normalized cone is preserved from one iterate to
the next.  Keeping those tasks separate prevents a common source of drift,
namely using a full-step estimate to justify a half-step selector choice.

This separation also explains why the proof needs exactly two selector gaps and
no more.  A third gap would require a third row with two competing monomials,
but the support ledger has only two such rows.  All other rows are rigid, so
they can change the value of a selected degree vector only after the selected
support has already been decided.  In other words, the phase structure is not an
artifact of the proof; it is already present in the support ledger itself.

\begin{lemma}[Exactly two selector faces]\label{lem:twofaces}
For the displayed family there are exactly two nontrivial selector comparisons:
\(\MS\) in the \(S\)-phase and \(\MT\) in the \(T\)-phase.  No third selector
face occurs.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Table~\ref{tab:support}, the \(S\)-phase has three rows to inspect.  The
\(\partial_{q_2}\Vpoly\) and \(\partial_{q_3}\Vpoly\) rows each contain a
single monomial, so they cannot produce a selector boundary.  Only the
\(\partial_{q_1}\Vpoly\) row contains both a mixed and a pure candidate, and
their weight difference is \(\MS\).  The same reasoning applies to the
\(T\)-phase: the \(\partial_{p_1}\Wpoly\) and \(\partial_{p_2}\Wpoly\) rows are
single-support rows, while \(\partial_{p_3}\Wpoly\) contains the unique
pure-versus-mixed choice with gap \(\MT\).  Since there are no further support
rows, there is no third selector face.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Selector positivity]\label{lem:selector}
For \(g\geq 8\) and \((x,y)\) in the cone \(\cK_g\), both \(\MS\) and
\(\MT\) are strictly positive.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On \(\cK_g\), the height condition \(x+y<(g-3)/2\) implies
\[
\MS=(g-2)-2x-2y>(g-2)-2\cdot \frac{g-3}{2}=1.
\]
Since \(x,y\geq 1\),
\[
\MT=(2g-6)x+(g-6)y-6\geq (2g-6)+(g-6)-6=3g-18\geq 6.
\]
Hence both gaps are strict.  The case \(g=7\) is not inside the open cone and
is recorded separately as a boundary note.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cone invariance}\label{sec:cone}

Let \(U'=\Cg U\).  With \(x=U_2/U_1\), \(y=U_3/U_1\), and
\(D=g+7+6x+6y\), direct substitution yields
\[
U'_1=DU_1,\qquad
U'_2=\bigl(2g+4+5x+4y\bigr)U_1,\qquad
U'_3=\bigl(2(g-1)+2(g-1)x+(g-1)y\bigr)U_1.
\]
Hence the normalized coordinates \(X'=U'_2/U'_1\) and \(Y'=U'_3/U'_1\) satisfy
\[
X'-1=\frac{g-3-x-2y}{D},\qquad
Y'-1=\frac{g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y}{D}.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Facial slack ledger]\label{lem:slack}
Put
\[
h=g-3-2x-2y.
\]
On \(\cK_g\), one has \(h>0\), and the first selector margin and the first
normalized cone margin admit the exact rewritings
\[
\MS=1+h,
\qquad
X'-1=\frac{x+h}{D}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\MT\geq 3g-18\geq 6,
\qquad
g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y\geq 4g-24\geq 8.
\]
Thus both selector decisions and both normalized lower faces are strict before
the upper-height face is considered.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The definition of the open cone is precisely \(h>0\) together with
\(x,y\geq1\).  Substitution gives
\[
\MS=(g-2)-2x-2y=1+h
\]
and
\[
g-3-x-2y=x+h,
\]
which proves the two identities.  For the second selector, the coefficients of
\(x\) and \(y\) are positive when \(g\geq8\), so their minimum on the lower
faces occurs at \(x=y=1\):
\[
\MT\geq(2g-6)+(g-6)-6=3g-18.
\]
The numerator of \(Y'-1\) is treated in the same way:
\[
g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y
\geq g-9+(2g-8)+(g-7)=4g-24.
\]
The final numerical lower bounds follow from \(g\geq8\).  Notice that this is
an exact facial calculation, not a compactness argument: the strictness of the
first two target faces is visible directly in the four displayed margins.
\end{proof}

The first two cone inequalities are therefore reduced to sign checks on two
explicit numerators.  The third cone inequality is encoded by the height
functional \(H=R U'_1-U'_2-U'_3\) with \(R=(g-3)/2\), or equivalently by
\(H_2=2H=(g-3)U'_1-2U'_2-2U'_3\).
These three tests are sufficient because the cone has exactly three defining
inequalities after normalization: \(x\geq 1\), \(y\geq 1\), and \(x+y<R\).
Thus \(X'-1\) certifies the first lower bound, \(Y'-1\) certifies the second,
and \(H_2>0\) certifies the upper-height bound.  No extra hidden face remains
to be checked once these three signs are positive.
To expose every coefficient in the remaining test, substitute the three rows
of \(\Cg\) before collecting terms:
\begin{align*}
\frac{H_2}{U_1}
&=(g-3)(g+7+6x+6y)\\
&\quad-2(2g+4+5x+4y)
      -2\bigl(2(g-1)+2(g-1)x+(g-1)y\bigr)\\
&=g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)x+4(g-6)y.
\end{align*}
Consequently
\[
H_2=(g-3)U'_1-2U'_2-2U'_3=U_1Q,
\]
where
\[
Q=g^2+2gx+4gy-4g-24x-24y-25,
\qquad
H=\frac{H_2}{2}=R U'_1-U'_2-U'_3.
\]
The lower-height parameter is \(R=(g-3)/2\).  The split
\(8\leq g\leq 11\) versus \(g\geq 12\) is the only place where the inequality
needs a case distinction.  For the smaller range the coefficient of \(x\) in
\(Q\) is negative, so one uses the cone inequality \(x<(g-3)/2-y\) to
eliminate \(x\):
\begin{align*}
Q
&=g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)x+4(g-6)y\\
&>g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)\Bigl(\frac{g-3}{2}-y\Bigr)+4(g-6)y\\
&=2g^2-19g+11+2gy\\
&\geq 2g^2-17g+11.
\end{align*}
This yields
\[
Q>2g^2-17g+11\geq 3 \qquad (8\leq g\leq 11),
\]
because the last comparison has the exact factorization
\[
2g^2-17g+11=3+(g-8)(2g-1).
\]
For the larger range all coefficients of \(x\) and \(y\) are
nonnegative, so the trivial lower bounds \(x,y\geq 1\) are enough:
\begin{align*}
Q
&=g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)x+4(g-6)y\\
&\geq g^2-4g-25+2(g-12)+4(g-6)\\
&=g^2+2g-73.
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
Q\geq g^2+2g-73>0 \qquad (g\geq 12).
\]
Here the lower endpoint is also explicit:
\[
g^2+2g-73=95+(g-12)(g+14),
\]
so the large-\(g\) estimate has a positive margin already at \(g=12\) and
only increases afterwards.
No numerical trial is hidden here; the split is forced only by the sign change
of the \(x\)-coefficient in \(Q\).  Therefore \(H_2>0\), hence \(H>0\), and so
\(x'+y'<(g-3)/2\).

The geometry behind the split is simple but essential.  For \(8\leq g\leq 11\),
the coefficient \(2(g-12)\) is negative, so increasing \(x\) makes the height
test worse rather than better.  In that range the sharp move is to push \(x\)
to the boundary value \((g-3)/2-y\) supplied by the cone and then use only the
lower bound \(y\geq 1\).  For \(g\geq 12\), the sign reverses and both \(x\)
and \(y\) become helpful directions, so the coarse lower bounds \(x,y\geq 1\)
already suffice.  The proof therefore uses the weakest valid estimate in each
range and never appeals to a sampled picture of the cone.

\begin{proposition}[Cone invariance]\label{prop:cone}
If \(U\in \cK_g\), then \(U'=\Cg U\in \cK_g\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Because \(D>0\), the sign of \(X'-1\) is the sign of \(g-3-x-2y\).  On the
cone one has \(x+2y<(g-3)/2+y\leq g-4<g-3\), so \(X'-1>0\).  Likewise
\(Y'-1>0\) because \(g-9\geq -1\) and the coefficients of \(x\) and \(y\) are
nonnegative, with at least one strictly positive contribution once \(x,y\geq
1\).  The height inequality follows from the already verified positivity of
\(H_2=U_1Q\).  Hence \(X',Y'\geq 1\) and \(X'+Y'<(g-3)/2\), which is exactly
the condition \(U'\in \cK_g\).
\end{proof}

The force of Proposition~\ref{prop:cone} is that it returns the iterate to the
\emph{open} cone and not merely to its closure.  That distinction is what lets
the selector proof restart with strict inequalities at every step.  A closed
cone argument would leave open the possibility of landing on a boundary where a
future selector tie could occur.  The present proof needs the stronger
conclusion: once inside the cone, every full iterate comes back with room to
spare, so the same two selector winners remain available indefinitely.

\begin{remark}[Boundary case \(g=7\)]\label{rem:g7}
At \(g=7\), the seed \((x,y)=(1,1)\) lies on the boundary
\(x+y=(g-3)/2\), and the first gap is \(M_S=1\).  The seed is therefore a
strict-cone boundary example, not a selector tie.
\end{remark}

\section{Phase/carry induction and characteristic-zero no-cancellation}\label{sec:induction}

\subsection{Phase vectors}\label{sec:phase}

Let \(u_0=\1=(1,1,1)^{\mathsf T}\), and let \(v_{n+1}\) and \(u_{n+1}\) be the
selected \(p\)- and \(q\)-degree vectors after the first and second shears at
time \(n\).  The phase convention is
\[
v_{n+1}=\Ag u_n,\qquad u_{n+1}=\Cg u_n.
\]
The first equality reads the first selected face, and the second equality reads
the complete step.  The distinction matters throughout the section.  The
intermediate vector \(v_{n+1}\) is the selected support after applying only
\(S\), while \(u_{n+1}\) is the selected support after the full map \(T\circ S\).
The proof keeps those two phases separate so that the half-step matrix
\(\Ag\) is never confused with the full-step matrix \(\Cg\).

There are two kinds of bookkeeping to maintain at once.  One is geometric:
which face wins inside a given support row.  The other is temporal: whether the
newly created term at time \(n\) is still larger than the term carried from
time \(n-1\).  The selector gaps \(\MS\) and \(\MT\) resolve the first issue,
while Lemma~\ref{lem:monotone} resolves the second by showing that the full-step
degree vector grows strictly in every coordinate.

The iterate structure is easiest to read as a chain:
\[
u_0 \xrightarrow{S} v_1 \xrightarrow{T} u_1 \xrightarrow{S} v_2
\xrightarrow{T} u_2 \xrightarrow{S} \cdots
\]
At each \(S\)-arrow the first matrix \(\Ag\) acts, and at each \(T\)-arrow the
second matrix \(\Bg\) acts on the selected support to produce the full-step
matrix \(\Cg\).  The point of the chain is not decorative; it prevents the
common mistake of comparing a carried term from time \(n-1\) directly against a
full-step term at time \(n+1\) without accounting for the intermediate phase.

The first symbolic iterate already exhibits the later mechanism in miniature:
\[
v_1=\Ag\1=(g-1,5,5)^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
u_1=\Cg\1=(g+19,2g+13,5(g-1))^{\mathsf T}.
\]
The first vector shows the immediate \(S\)-phase transfer, while the second
already contains the three row sums that later reappear in the Perron bound.
It also shows that the full-step third coordinate is largest at the seed
itself, since \(5(g-1)>2g+13>g+19\) for \(g\geq 8\).  The induction below
should therefore be read as a persistence argument for a pattern that is
visible from the first full iterate and not as a wholly different asymptotic
phenomenon.

One more exact step already displays the quadratic growth pattern hidden behind
the linear recurrence:
\[
v_2=\Ag u_1=
\bigl((g-1)(g+19),\;14g+41,\;11g+59\bigr)^{\mathsf T},
\]
\[
u_2=\Cg u_1=
\bigl(g^2+68g+181,\;2g^2+72g+121,\;11g^2+48g-59\bigr)^{\mathsf T}.
\]
These formulas are not needed for the abstract induction, but they provide a
useful audit of what the induction is claiming.  The full-step third coordinate
already remains visible at the second iterate because
\[
u_{2,3}-u_{2,2}=3(g-6)(3g+10)>0,
\qquad
u_{2,3}-u_{2,1}=10(g-6)(g+4)>0
\]
for every \(g\geq 8\).  In other words, the visible-row phenomenon is not only
eventually true; it is already visible in the first two exact full iterates,
and the later cone argument explains why this symbolic dominance persists for
all subsequent iterates.

\subsection{The inductive state in all six coordinates}\label{sec:six-state}

Write the \(n\)-th iterate in coordinates as
\[
\Fg^n(q,p)=\bigl(Q^{(n)}(q,p),P^{(n)}(q,p)\bigr),
\]
where \(Q^{(n)}=(Q^{(n)}_1,Q^{(n)}_2,Q^{(n)}_3)\) and similarly for
\(P^{(n)}\).  Applying one more complete step first forms
\[
\widetilde P^{(n+1)}
=P^{(n)}+\grad\Vpoly\bigl(Q^{(n)}\bigr)
\]
and then forms
\[
Q^{(n+1)}
=Q^{(n)}+\grad\Wpoly\bigl(\widetilde P^{(n+1)}\bigr),
\qquad
P^{(n+1)}=\widetilde P^{(n+1)}.
\]
These identities show exactly where the two carried vectors enter.  The old
\(P^{(n)}\) competes with the fresh \(V\)-gradient during the first phase;
the old \(Q^{(n)}\) competes with the fresh \(W\)-gradient during the second.
Thus a proof that checks only the internal pure-versus-mixed selectors is not
yet an induction: the carried coordinate in each displayed sum must also lose
strictly.

Let
\[
\mathbf d_q^{(n)}
=\bigl(\deg Q^{(n)}_1,\deg Q^{(n)}_2,\deg Q^{(n)}_3\bigr)^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
\mathbf d_p^{(n)}
=\bigl(\deg P^{(n)}_1,\deg P^{(n)}_2,\deg P^{(n)}_3\bigr)^{\mathsf T}.
\]
The induction will certify the six-coordinate state
\[
\mathbf d_q^{(n)}=u_n,
\qquad
\mathbf d_p^{(n)}=v_n\quad(n\geq1),
\]
with \(\mathbf d_q^{(0)}=\mathbf d_p^{(0)}=\1\).  This statement is not
assumed in defining \(u_n,v_n\): it is the conclusion obtained after strict
selection, carried-term dominance, and coefficient survival have all been
checked.  The two phases can be summarized as follows.

\begin{lemma}[Strict coordinate growth]\label{lem:monotone}
For \(g\geq 8\), the matrix
\[
\Cg-I=
\begin{pmatrix}
g+6&6&6\\
2g+4&4&4\\
2g-2&2g-2&g-2
\end{pmatrix}
\]
is entrywise positive.  Consequently, whenever \(u_{n+1}=\Cg u_n\) with
\(u_n\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3\), one has \(u_{n+1}>u_n\) coordinatewise.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every entry of \(\Cg-I\) is strictly positive for \(g\geq 8\).  Therefore
\((\Cg-I)u_n>0\) for every positive vector \(u_n\), which is equivalent to
\(u_{n+1}-u_n>0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Carried \(q\)-terms in the \(T\)-phase]\label{lem:T-carry}
Suppose that the \(S\)-phase has selected
\(v_{n+1}=\Ag u_n\).  Then the fresh \(T\)-gradient degree vector strictly
dominates the carried \(q\)-degree vector, including at the base step:
\[
 \Bg v_{n+1}-u_n
 =\Cg u_n-u_n
 =(\Cg-I)u_n>0,
 \qquad n\geq 0.
\]
Consequently the carried coordinates \(q_1,q_2,q_3\) cannot alter the
selected full-step vector \(u_{n+1}=\Cg u_n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The identity \(\Bg v_{n+1}=\Bg\Ag u_n=\Cg u_n\) is the phase product.
Because every \(u_n\) is positive and \(\Cg-I\) is entrywise positive by
Lemma~\ref{lem:monotone}, the displayed difference is positive in all three
coordinates.  For \(n=0\) this reads
\((\Cg-I)\1>0\), so the argument includes the initially carried coordinate
functions of degree one rather than starting only after the first iterate.
The selector gap \(\MT\) has a separate role: it chooses the pure term over
the mixed term inside the competitive third \(W\)-row.  The present lemma is
what compares those freshly selected \(W\)-gradient rows with the three
carried \(q\)-coordinates.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Base step]\label{prop:base}
At \(n=0\), the selected \(S\)-face dominates the carried term, and
\(v_1=\Ag u_0>\1\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:seed} places the seed in the cone when \(g\geq 8\).
Lemma~\ref{lem:selector} then gives \(\MS>0\), so the pure-versus-mixed
comparison on the \(S\)-phase is strict.  Since
\[
\Ag\1=(g-1,5,5)^{\mathsf T},
\]
the selected gradient terms strictly dominate the carried \(p\)-terms.  At the
base step the carried \(p\)-coordinates are simply the original coordinate
functions \(p_1,p_2,p_3\), all of degree one, so there is no hidden competitor
with the same weight.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Old-term induction]\label{prop:induction}
For every \(n\geq 1\), if \(p_n=\Ag u_{n-1}\), then the \(S\)-selector remains
strict, the intermediate vector satisfies \(\Ag u_n>p_n\), and the \(T\)-phase
selects \(u_{n+1}=\Cg u_n\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume \(p_n=\Ag u_{n-1}\).  By Proposition~\ref{prop:cone}, every \(u_n\) lies
in \(\cK_g\), and by Lemma~\ref{lem:monotone} one has \(u_n>u_{n-1}\)
coordinatewise.  Since \(\Ag\) has nonnegative entries and each row has at
least one strictly positive entry, it follows that
\[
\Ag u_n>\Ag u_{n-1}=p_n.
\]
Writing \(\Delta_n=u_n-u_{n-1}\), the strict difference is
\[
\Ag\Delta_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
(g-1)\Delta_{n,1}\\
2\Delta_{n,1}+\Delta_{n,2}+2\Delta_{n,3}\\
2\Delta_{n,1}+2\Delta_{n,2}+\Delta_{n,3}
\end{pmatrix}
>0.
\]
This is the old-term dominance statement: the newly selected \(S\)-term is
strictly larger than the carried \(p\)-term from the previous iterate.  The
\(T\)-selector is then controlled by the second explicit gap \(\MT\), whose
strict positivity was proved in Lemma~\ref{lem:selector}.  After that internal
face comparison, Lemma~\ref{lem:T-carry} proves separately that
\(\Cg u_n-u_n>0\), so all three freshly selected \(T\)-gradient rows beat the
carried \(q\)-coordinates.  Thus the same two faces are selected again and the
full step is exactly \(u_{n+1}=\Cg u_n\).  At no stage do we invoke a
preservation of arbitrary face hyperplanes; we use the two explicit gaps
\(\MS\) and \(\MT\), the \(S\)-carry margins, and the explicit \(T\)-carry
margin \((\Cg-I)u_n\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Unique selected exponent at each phase]\label{lem:unique-selection}
For every \(n\geq 0\), each support row contributing to the recurrence has a
unique selected exponent vector.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The rigid rows in Table~\ref{tab:support} are singletons, so uniqueness is
automatic there.  In the \(S\)-competitive row, Lemma~\ref{lem:selector} gives
\(\MS>0\) on \(\cK_g\), so the pure exponent \((g-1,0,0)\) strictly beats the
mixed exponent \((1,2,2)\).  In the \(T\)-competitive row, the same lemma gives
\(\MT>0\), so the pure exponent \((0,0,g-1)\) strictly beats the mixed exponent
\((2,2,1)\).  Because the cone is invariant, these strict inequalities are
available at every iterate.  Hence each selected row in the recurrence has a
unique winner before any coefficient bookkeeping is considered.
\end{proof}

\subsection{No-cancellation in characteristic zero}\label{sec:nocancel}

\begin{lemma}[Positive-support no-cancellation]\label{lem:nocancel}
Let \(R=\mathbb N[z_1,\dots,z_6]\).  If a polynomial is written as a finite sum
of distinct monomials with positive coefficients, then substituting polynomials
from \(R\) preserves positivity of every selected Minkowski-sum coefficient.
Over a field of characteristic zero, the resulting selected coefficient is
nonzero.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Distributivity expands the substituted polynomial as a finite sum of products
of positive integers.  No sign changes occur, and characteristic zero prevents
the positive integer coefficient from vanishing.  In the present application
the selected monomial support is obtained by Minkowski-summing already selected
supports from the previous phase.  Every contribution to the selected
coefficient is therefore a positive integer, so the coefficient after
substitution is a positive integer as well.  The lemma is exactly the
support-level version of the carry induction above: once the selector gaps are
strict, the only remaining obstruction would be cancellation, and that
obstruction is absent in characteristic zero.
\par
The role of Lemma~\ref{lem:unique-selection} is precisely to make this
coefficient statement meaningful.  Strict selector gaps identify a unique
exponent vector in each competitive row, and the semiring argument then shows
that the coefficient attached to that unique exponent remains positive after
substitution.  The proof does not rely on a general theorem about all
substitutions of positive polynomials; it uses the much narrower fact that the
selected exponent is unique and every contributing coefficient path is a
positive integer path.
\par
A representative example is the pure \(T\)-row.  Once the selected \(S\)-phase
support for \(p_3\) is fixed, the pure monomial \(p_3^{g-1}\) expands into a
product of \(g-1\) identical positive-support polynomials.  Every path
contributing to the selected exponent adds a positive integer coefficient, and
no negative sign is ever introduced.  The same mechanism applies to the mixed
rows, where the selected exponent is obtained by a Minkowski sum of already
selected supports.  Thus the no-cancellation lemma is not an abstract semiring
tagline; it is the coefficient-level reason the exact selected row survives the
entire iteration.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Explicit positive coefficient witnesses]\label{lem:coefficient-witness}
Assume the selected monomial in each \(Q_i^{(n)}\) has a positive integer
coefficient \(a_i\).  Then the three selected \(S\)-gradient monomials receive
positive contributions with coefficients
\[
g a_1^{g-1},
\qquad
2a_1^2a_2a_3^2,
\qquad
2a_1^2a_2^2a_3.
\]
If the corresponding selected monomials of
\(\widetilde P^{(n+1)}\) have positive coefficients \(b_1,b_2,b_3\), then
the three selected \(T\)-gradient monomials receive positive contributions
with coefficients
\[
2b_1b_2^2b_3^2,
\qquad
2b_1^2b_2b_3^2,
\qquad
g b_3^{g-1}.
\]
Every one of these witnesses is nonzero in characteristic zero.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first list is read directly from
\[
\partial_{q_1}\Vpoly
=2q_1q_2^2q_3^2+gq_1^{g-1},\quad
\partial_{q_2}\Vpoly=2q_1^2q_2q_3^2,\quad
\partial_{q_3}\Vpoly=2q_1^2q_2^2q_3.
\]
The strict \(S\)-selector chooses the pure term in the first row, while the
other two rows are singletons.  Choosing one already selected monomial from
each factor in the indicated products gives exactly the three displayed
positive contributions.  Likewise,
\[
\partial_{p_1}\Wpoly=2p_1p_2^2p_3^2,\quad
\partial_{p_2}\Wpoly=2p_1^2p_2p_3^2,\quad
\partial_{p_3}\Wpoly=2p_1^2p_2^2p_3+gp_3^{g-1}.
\]
The first two rows are singletons and the strict \(T\)-selector chooses the
pure term in the third row, producing the second list.

Other products may contribute to the same exponent, so the displayed numbers
are coefficient witnesses rather than claims that no other path exists.  That
distinction is harmless and useful: all forward coordinate polynomials lie in
the positive semiring \(\mathbb N[q_1,q_2,q_3,p_1,p_2,p_3]\), so any additional
path only adds another nonnegative integer.  Since \(2\) and the positive
integer \(g\) remain nonzero in characteristic zero, each selected exponent
survives with a nonzero coefficient.  The lemma therefore turns the abstract
semiring statement into six explicit recursive witnesses, one for each fresh
gradient row.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact phase recurrence]\label{thm:recurrence}
The complete selected recurrence is
\[
v_{n+1}=\Ag u_n,\qquad u_{n+1}=\Cg u_n\qquad (n\geq 0),
\]
and the selected support coefficient never cancels.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Combine Proposition~\ref{prop:base}, Lemma~\ref{lem:T-carry},
Proposition~\ref{prop:induction}, Lemma~\ref{lem:unique-selection}, and
Lemmas~\ref{lem:nocancel}--\ref{lem:coefficient-witness}.  The recurrence is
phase-labelled: the first matrix
is the first shear, the second matrix is the complete step, and the support
coefficient remains positive at every iteration.  Both carried-coordinate
comparisons are explicit: \(\Ag u_n\) beats the carried \(p\)-vector, while
\(\Cg u_n\) beats the carried \(q\)-vector.  In particular, the induction does
not merely identify the maximal degree numerically; it identifies a specific
support row whose coefficient remains nonzero through the entire iteration
process.
\par
This is the point at which characteristic zero is genuinely used.  If the field
had positive characteristic, a positive integer coefficient could vanish after
substitution, and the support-level argument would no longer imply that the
selected row survives.  The paper therefore keeps the field hypothesis in the
main theorem rather than hiding it in a remark, because it is part of the
mechanism that makes the exact recurrence possible.
\par
The conclusion of the theorem is stronger than a bare degree recurrence.  It
records a persistent support mechanism.  At each stage one can point to the
same selector rows, the same carried-coordinate comparisons, and the same
positivity argument on coefficients.  This is why the later visibility proof
can safely read the degree from one coordinate functional: the recurrence is
not an accidental equality of numbers but the shadow of a support pattern that
has been preserved through every phase of the iteration.
\end{proof}

\section{Visibility, exact degree, and Perron bound}\label{sec:visibility}

\subsection{Visibility of the third coordinate}\label{sec:visible}

The matrix difference is
\[
\Cg-\Ag=
\begin{pmatrix}
8&6&6\\
2g+2&4&2\\
2g-4&2g-4&g-2
\end{pmatrix},
\]
which is entrywise positive for \(g\geq 8\).  Furthermore,
\[
U_3-U_2=[-6+(2g-7)x+(g-5)y]U_1>0,
\qquad
U_3-U_1=[g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y]U_1>0.
\]
The entrywise positivity of \(\Cg-\Ag\) controls the \(q\)- versus \(p\)-phase
comparison, while the two displayed differences control the internal ordering
of the \(q\)-coordinates.  Together they show that the visible coordinate is
not merely one of several maximal rows: it is the unique row that dominates the
other five coordinate degrees after each full iterate.  Hence \(q_3\) is the
maximal coordinate degree among all six coordinates.

\begin{lemma}[Six-coordinate dominance]\label{lem:six-dominance}
For every \(n\geq1\), the full-step degree vectors satisfy
\[
u_n>v_n
\]
coordinatewise, and the third component of \(u_n\) is strictly larger than
the first two components.  Consequently the third \(q\)-coordinate has degree
strictly larger than each of the other five coordinate functions of
\(\Fg^n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write the input to the \(n\)-th full step as
\(w=u_{n-1}=(W_1,W_2,W_3)^{\mathsf T}\), with
\(x=W_2/W_1\) and \(y=W_3/W_1\).  The exact recurrence gives
\[
u_n-v_n=(\Cg-\Ag)w>0
\]
because \(\Cg-\Ag\) is entrywise positive.  This compares corresponding
\(q_i\)- and \(p_i\)-rows.

For the internal \(q\)-ordering, put
\(u_n=(U_1,U_2,U_3)^{\mathsf T}\).  Subtracting rows of \(\Cg\) gives
\begin{align*}
U_3-U_2
&=\bigl[-6+(2g-7)x+(g-5)y\bigr]W_1,\\
U_3-U_1
&=\bigl[g-9+(2g-8)x+(g-7)y\bigr]W_1.
\end{align*}
Since \(x,y\geq1\), their bracketed coefficients have the explicit lower
bounds
\[
-6+(2g-7)+(g-5)=3g-18\geq6,
\qquad
g-9+(2g-8)+(g-7)=4g-24\geq8.
\]
Thus \(U_3>U_2,U_1\).  Finally, the dominance chains
\[
U_3>U_1>\deg P^{(n)}_1,
\qquad
U_3>U_2>\deg P^{(n)}_2,
\qquad
U_3>\deg P^{(n)}_3
\]
compare the visible row with all three \(p\)-coordinates.  Hence it is the
unique maximal degree among all six coordinate functions.
\end{proof}

\(p\)-coordinate by an explicit positive margin.  The third row then dominates
the first two rows because the two displayed inequalities show \(U_3-U_2>0\)
and \(U_3-U_1>0\).  The visible row is therefore fixed by two independent
comparisons: one across the phase boundary and one within the \(q\)-phase.
Table~\ref{tab:visibility} records these comparisons in the order they are
actually used.  The proof does not maximize over all six coordinates at once.
First it reduces the question from six rows to the three full-step
\(q\)-coordinates by using \(\Cg-\Ag>0\).  Only after that reduction does it
compare the three \(q\)-rows among themselves.  This order matters because the
functional \(\ethree^{\mathsf T}\) is an output of the visibility proof and not
an assumption built into the recurrence from the beginning.

\begin{proposition}[Exact degree row]\label{prop:degree}
For every \(n\geq 0\),
\[
\degmap(\Fg^n)=\ethree^{\mathsf T}\Cg^n\1.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The case \(n=0\) is immediate:
\(\degmap(\Fg^0)=\degmap(\id)=1=\ethree^{\mathsf T}\1\).  For \(n\geq 1\), the
phase recurrence gives the selected \(q\)-degree vector \(u_n=\Cg^n\1\) and the
intermediate \(p\)-degree vector \(v_n=\Ag u_{n-1}\).  Because
\[
u_n-v_n=(\Cg-\Ag)u_{n-1},
\]
and \(\Cg-\Ag\) is entrywise positive for \(g\geq 8\), every \(q\)-coordinate
degree after the full step exceeds the corresponding intermediate
\(p\)-coordinate degree.  Among the \(q\)-coordinates, the displayed
inequalities give \(U_3>U_2\) and \(U_3>U_1\).  Since the visible coefficient
never cancels, the degree of \(\Fg^n\) is read off by the third-coordinate
functional and equals \(\ethree^{\mathsf T}\Cg^n\1\).
\end{proof}

Table~\ref{tab:degreeaudit} shows that the degree identity is not a late

same symbolic margins continue to separate the visible row from its
competitors.  This is why the degree functional can be written as the fixed row
vector \(\ethree^{\mathsf T}\) rather than as a changing ``take the maximum''
operation.

One can read the first values of the exact degree sequence directly from this
audit:
\[
\degmap(\Fg^0)=1,\qquad
\degmap(\Fg^1)=5(g-1),\qquad
\degmap(\Fg^2)=11g^2+48g-59.
\]
These values are not obtained by guessing a scalar recurrence first and then
verifying it afterward.  They are obtained by following the visible row of the
matrix recurrence that has already been certified symbolically.  This point
becomes important in the next section: once the characteristic polynomial of
\(\Cg\) is known, it governs the \emph{exact} degree sequence because the
degree has already been tied to one fixed row of \(C_g^n\), not merely bounded
above by it.

\begin{corollary}[Perron bound]\label{cor:perron}
The matrix \(\Cg\) is primitive, hence
\[
\lambda_1(\Fg)=\rhoPF(\Cg).
\]
Its row sums are \(g+19\), \(2g+13\), and \(5(g-1)\), all strictly below
\((g-1)^2\) for \(g\geq 8\).  Therefore
\[
\rhoPF(\Cg)<(g-1)^2.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
\(\Cg\) itself is entrywise positive for \(g\geq 8\), hence primitive.
Perron--Frobenius therefore provides a positive right eigenvector
\(r\in\mathbb R_{>0}^3\) for the simple eigenvalue \(\rhoPF(\Cg)\).  Choose
positive constants \(a,b\) such that
\[
a r\leq\1\leq b r
\]
coordinatewise; for example, take
\(a=\min_i r_i^{-1}\) and \(b=\max_i r_i^{-1}\).  Positivity of \(\Cg\)
preserves these inequalities, so
\[
a\rhoPF(\Cg)^n r_3
\leq \ethree^{\mathsf T}\Cg^n\1
\leq b\rhoPF(\Cg)^n r_3.
\]
Taking \(n\)-th roots proves directly that
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\bigl(\ethree^{\mathsf T}\Cg^n\1\bigr)^{1/n}=\rhoPF(\Cg).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:degree},
\[
\degmap(\Fg^n)=\ethree^{\mathsf T}\Cg^n\1,
\]
so the dynamical degree
\(\lambda_1(\Fg)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\degmap(\Fg^n)^{1/n}\) is exactly the Perron
root of \(\Cg\).  The row sums are
\[
(g+7)+6+6=g+19,\qquad (2g+4)+5+4=2g+13,\qquad 2(g-1)+2(g-1)+(g-1)=5(g-1).
\]
Their maximum bounds the spectral radius from above, and the differences
\begin{align*}
(g-1)^2-(g+19)&=(g-6)(g+3),\\
(g-1)^2-(2g+13)&=(g-6)(g+2),\\
(g-1)^2-5(g-1)&=(g-1)(g-6)
\end{align*}
are all positive for \(g\geq 8\).  Hence \(\rhoPF(\Cg)<(g-1)^2\).
\par
The estimate is intentionally elementary.  The exact Perron root has already
been identified as the dynamical degree, so the remaining task is only to place
that root below a simple closed expression.  The row-sum bound does this with
visible factorizations and no need to solve the cubic or estimate eigenvectors.
What matters is not sharpness for its own sake, but the fact that the upper
bound can be checked directly from the displayed matrix entries.
\end{proof}

\section{Characteristic polynomial and mod-five cubic Perron subfamilies}\label{sec:cubic}

\begin{lemma}[Characteristic polynomial]\label{lem:charpoly}
The characteristic polynomial of \(\Cg\) is
\[
\chi_{\Cg}(t)=t^3-(2g+11)t^2+(g^2-16g+19)t-9(g-1)^2.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a \(3\times 3\) matrix, the characteristic polynomial is determined by the
trace, the sum of principal \(2\times 2\) minors, and the determinant.  Here
\[
\operatorname{tr}(\Cg)=(g+7)+5+(g-1)=2g+11.
\]
The principal \(2\times 2\) minors are
\[
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}g+7&6\\2g+4&5\end{pmatrix}=11-7g,
\qquad
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}g+7&6\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}=(g-1)(g-5),
\]
and
\[
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}5&4\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}=-3(g-1).
\]
Their sum is
\[
(11-7g)+(g-1)(g-5)-3(g-1)=g^2-16g+19.
\]
Finally,
\[
\det(\Cg)=\det(\Bg)\det(\Ag)=\bigl(-3(g-1)\bigr)^2=9(g-1)^2.
\]
Substituting these three quantities into the standard \(3\times 3\)
characteristic-polynomial formula yields the stated expression.  The
coefficient pattern is rigid and uses no hidden CAS identity.
\par
This trace--minor--determinant route is the shortest exact path from the
displayed matrix to the cubic polynomial.  A direct expansion of
\(\det(tI-\Cg)\) would certainly be possible, but it would obscure the meaning
of the coefficients.  Here each coefficient is attached to a standard matrix
invariant, so a reader can audit the cubic one invariant at a time.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Mod-five audit]\label{prop:mod5}
If \(g\equiv 2,3,4 \pmod 5\), then \(\chi_{\Cg}(t)\) is irreducible over
\(\mathbb Q\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Reduce the characteristic polynomial modulo \(5\).  The three residue classes
\(g\equiv 2,3,4\pmod 5\) produce exactly the three cubics listed in
Table~\ref{tab:mod5}.  A cubic polynomial over \(\mathbb F_5\) is reducible if
and only if it has a root in \(\mathbb F_5\).  The table records the values at
all five field elements and shows that none of the three cubics vanishes.  Each
reduced polynomial is therefore irreducible in \(\mathbb F_5[t]\).  Gauss's
lemma then gives irreducibility of \(\chi_{\Cg}(t)\) in \(\mathbb Q[t]\).
\par
The arithmetic input is intentionally minimal.  For \(g\equiv 2\pmod 5\) the
reduced cubic is \(t^3+t+1\); for \(g\equiv 3\pmod 5\) it is \(t^3-2t^2-1\);
and for \(g\equiv 4\pmod 5\) it is \(t^3+t^2+t-1\).  In each case the table is
already a complete no-root certificate because it records the value at every
element of \(\mathbb F_5\).  No hidden finite search or external algebra system
is being appealed to: the proof is exactly the five-point audit visible in the
main text.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cubic Perron subfamilies]\label{cor:cubic}
For \(g\equiv 2,3,4 \pmod 5\), the Perron root \(\rhoPF(\Cg)\) is a degree-three
algebraic integer.  These are infinite cubic Perron subfamilies of the family
\(\Fg\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Combine Proposition~\ref{prop:mod5} with Corollary~\ref{cor:perron}.  Since
\(\rhoPF(\Cg)\) is the dominant eigenvalue of an integral \(3\times 3\) matrix
with irreducible characteristic polynomial over \(\mathbb Q\), it has degree
three over \(\mathbb Q\) and is a Perron algebraic integer.  The word
``cubic'' therefore refers only to the algebraic degree of the Perron root in
these residue classes; it is not a claim about a broader classification of the
family.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why only the residues \(2,3,4\)]\label{rem:mod5}
The mod-five test is intentionally one-sided.  It proves irreducibility for the
listed residue classes and stays silent for \(g\equiv 0,1\pmod 5\).  The paper
does not claim that the omitted classes are reducible, nor that mod five is the
only useful modulus.  The corollary is exactly the arithmetic statement that
the displayed table certifies and nothing broader.
\end{remark}

\section{Boundary audit, limitations, anti-claims, and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The borderline case \(g=7\) is a helpful anti-example.  The seed \(\1\) lies
on the strict-cone boundary and the \(S\)-gap is positive, so the obstruction is
geometric rather than a selector tie.  This is why the theorem is stated for
\(g\geq 8\): the proof uses the open cone and the \(g=8,\dots,11\) versus
\(g\geq 12\) split.

The limitations are not cosmetic.  We do not claim a generic finite Newton-fan
theorem, a classification of all symplectic or affine-triangular automorphisms,
an entropy theorem, a periodic-point theorem, a torus theorem, a conjugacy
theorem, or a priority claim.  We also do not claim any CAS output as proof,
nor any numerical sampling as evidence.  The theorem is about one displayed
family, one displayed cone, and one exact recurrence.

The bounded novelty wording should be read in the same restrained way.  It is
not an absolute novelty certificate.  If a later comparison changed the
surrounding context language, the proof of the present theorem would remain the
same because it depends only on the explicit gradients, matrices, inequalities,
and residue checks already displayed in the main text.

This is why the conclusion keeps returning to exactness rather than breadth.
The paper succeeds by freezing one family tightly enough that every
theorem-critical step fits in the main body: two gradients, six support rows,
two selector gaps, one invariant cone, one visible degree row, and one finite
residue audit.  Any broader theorem would first need to replace at least one of
those exact components with a genuinely new argument.  The present manuscript
does not attempt that broader project.

There is also a methodological lesson in the proof order itself.  The paper
never uses spectral information to discover the recurrence, never uses the
final degree formula to justify visibility, and never uses the mod-five
corollary to motivate the support ledger retroactively.  Each stage is proved
with the data available at that stage and then handed forward.  In a short
paper one might compress these steps into a single narrative paragraph, but for
an exact theorem the separation is part of the rigor: it tells the reader which
calculation depends on which earlier certification and prevents later algebra
from hiding an earlier selector gap.

From the proof one should remember three facts.  First, the selected matrices
\(\Ag\), \(\Bg\), and \(\Cg\) are read from a six-row support ledger and not
from an abstract template.  Second, the correct degree observable is the third
\(q\)-coordinate, not a half-step intermediate.  Third, the algebraic
consequence of the mod-five audit is a genuine family of cubic Perron numbers,
not a casual irreducibility remark.

The paper therefore closes where it began: with one exact family, one exact
degree recurrence, and one exact algebraic corollary.  The rest is bookkeeping
around those identities.  Nothing theorem-critical is hidden offstage: a reader
who wants to verify the result can replay Lemmas~\ref{lem:canonical} through
Proposition~\ref{prop:mod5} in order and recover the whole theorem from the
main body alone.

For that reason the manuscript is self-contained at the proof level: a reader
only needs the fixed proof order L1 through L9, the two contextual citations,
and the displayed support ledger.  There is no missing appendix dependency, no
figure dependency, and no computational certificate to request before the
theorem can be checked line by line.
