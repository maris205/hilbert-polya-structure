\begin{abstract}
Let \(K\) be any field of characteristic zero, let \(r\geq4\), and let
\(g\geq2r+1\).  We determine the degree growth of the polynomial symplectic
automorphism obtained by composing the gradient shears generated by
\[
V_{r,g}(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}q_i^2+q_1^g,
\qquad
W_{r,g}(p)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_i^2+p_r^g.
\]
Define
\[
S(q,p)=(q,p+\nabla V_{r,g}(q)),\qquad
T(q,p)=(q+\nabla W_{r,g}(p),p),\qquad
F_{r,g}=T\circ S.
\]
Put \(h=g-1\), let \(M=2\one\one^{\transpose}-I_r\), replace the first row
of \(M\) by \(he_1^{\transpose}\) to obtain \(A\), replace the last row of
\(M\) by \(he_r^{\transpose}\) to obtain \(B\), and set \(C=BA\).  An explicit
sufficient invariant selector cone proves, for actual coordinate degrees,
\(v_{n+1}=Au_n\) and \(u_{n+1}=Cu_n\).  The proof includes both phase
selectors, every cone wall, carried coordinates, and leading-form survival
in a polynomial domain.  The last \(q\)-coordinate has strict visibility for
\(n\geq1\); the tied initial case at \(n=0\) is handled separately.  Thus
\[
\degt(F_{r,g}^n)=e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one,
\qquad
\lambda_1(F_{r,g})=\rho(C).
\]
Middle-coordinate differences form an \((r-3)\)-dimensional eigenspace on
which \(C\) is the identity.  Its invariant complement has dimension three,
so, with \(m=r-2\),
\[
\chi_C(t)=(t-1)^{r-3}P_{m,h}(t),
\qquad
P_{m,h}(1)=-4m(m+1)(h+1)^2\neq0,
\]
where \(P_{m,h}\) is an explicit cubic and a cubic annihilator of the exact
degree sequence.  The value \(g=2r\) is sharp only at the ordinary seed and
the chosen strict selected face.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and bounded positioning}\label{sec:introduction}

The concrete question in this article is whether one can read the degree of
every iterate of a fixed two-shear word directly from its gradient supports.
For the endpoint-spiked product potentials
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-potentials}
V_{r,g}(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}q_i^2+q_1^g,
\qquad
W_{r,g}(p)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_i^2+p_r^g,
\end{equation}
the answer is not supplied by a formal exponent matrix alone.  The pure
power in the first \(V\)-derivative must beat a mixed product, and the pure
power in the last \(W\)-derivative must win after the first phase has already
changed the degree vector.  At later iterates, each fresh gradient must also
beat the coordinate carried from the preceding phase.  Finally, even a
unique degree score would be inconclusive if its highest homogeneous form
could vanish.  These are separate obligations, resolved below in the order
in which they occur in the map.

The payoff is a dimension-independent spectral statement.  Although the
degree vector has \(r\) entries, increasing \(r\) adds only middle-difference
directions on which the complete-step matrix is the identity.  All
nontrivial growth remains in one explicitly identified three-dimensional
subspace.  The following theorem states the complete result before the
bounded contextual discussion.

\begin{theorem}[Main theorem]\label{thm:main}
Let \(K\) be a field of characteristic zero, and let \(r,g\in\mathbb Z\)
satisfy \(r\geq4\) and \(g\geq2r+1\).  Define
\[
S(q,p)=(q,p+\nabla V_{r,g}(q)),
\qquad
T(q,p)=(q+\nabla W_{r,g}(p),p),
\qquad
F_{r,g}=T\circ S,
\]
where \(V_{r,g}\) and \(W_{r,g}\) are given by
\eqref{eq:intro-potentials}.  Put \(h=g-1\), \(m=r-2\), and
\[
M=2\one\one^{\transpose}-I_r.
\]
Let \(A\) be \(M\) with its first row replaced by
\(he_1^{\transpose}\), let \(B\) be \(M\) with its last row replaced by
\(he_r^{\transpose}\), and put \(C=BA\).  Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item \(S\), \(T\), and \(F_{r,g}\) are polynomial automorphisms preserving
the standard symplectic form
\(\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{r}dq_i\wedge dp_i\).
\item For \(u>0\), define \(x_i=u_i/u_1\) only for \(2\leq i\leq r\) and
\(\sigma=\sum_{i=2}^{r}x_i\).  The region
\[
\cK_{r,g}=
\left\{u>0:x_i\geq1\ (2\leq i\leq r),\quad
\sigma<\frac{h-1}{2}\right\}
\]
is an explicit sufficient invariant selector cone containing \(\one\).
For the actual coordinate-degree vectors,
\[
v_{n+1}=Au_n,
\qquad
u_{n+1}=Cu_n
\qquad(n\geq0).
\]
\item The last \(q\)-coordinate strictly dominates all other coordinate
degrees for \(n\geq1\).  At \(n=0\), all \(2r\) coordinate degrees tie at
one.  For every \(n\geq0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-degree}
\degt(F_{r,g}^n)=e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one,
\qquad
\lambda_1(F_{r,g})=\rho(C).
\end{equation}
\item For
\[
U=\left\{z\in K^r:z_1=z_r=0,\quad
\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}z_i=0\right\},
\qquad
E=\left\{(a,b,\ldots,b,c)^{\transpose}:a,b,c\in K\right\},
\]
one has \(K^r=U\oplus E\), \(\dim U=r-3\),
\(A|_U=B|_U=-I_U\), and \(C|_U=I_U\).  In the convention
\((a,b,c)\mapsto(a,b,\ldots,b,c)\), with \(m\) equal middle coordinates,
\[
Q_{m,h}=
\begin{pmatrix}
h+4m+4 & 2m(2m+1) & 2(2m+1)\\
2h+4m+2 & 4m^2+1 & 4m\\
2h & 2mh & h
\end{pmatrix}
\]
represents \(C|_E\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-factor}
\chi_C(t)=(t-1)^{r-3}P_{m,h}(t),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-cubic}
\begin{aligned}
P_{m,h}(t)
={}&t^3-(2h+4m^2+4m+5)t^2\\
&+(h^2-8hm(m+1)+2h+4)t\\
&-h^2(2m+1)^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\[
P_{m,h}(1)=-4m(m+1)(h+1)^2\neq0,
\]
so the eigenvalue \(1\) has algebraic and geometric multiplicity exactly
\(r-3\).
\item If \(d_n=e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one\), then \(P_{m,h}\) is a cubic
annihilator:
\[
d_{n+3}=T_0d_{n+2}-S_0d_{n+1}+D_0d_n,
\]
where
\[
T_0=2h+4m^2+4m+5,\quad
S_0=h^2-8hm(m+1)+2h+4,\quad
D_0=h^2(2m+1)^2,
\]
and \(d_0=1\), \(d_1=h(2m+3)\).  At \(g=2r\), the ordinary seed lies on
the boundary of the selected open cone and the pure and mixed scores of the
first selector tie; this is a seed/selected-face boundary only.  The same
conclusions hold for four arbitrary nonzero coefficients on the same fixed
supports, as stated in Corollary~\ref{cor:coefficients}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The theorem has three falsifiable contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item We derive both phase matrices from literal gradient supports and prove
one explicit sufficient invariant selector cone, including both selectors,
every cone wall, every carried coordinate, and leading-form survival.
\item We prove the exact degree and Perron formulas, then decompose the
\(r\)-dimensional degree system into an \((r-3)\)-dimensional unit sector
and the displayed three-dimensional equal-middle restriction.
\item We compute the characteristic factor and cubic recurrence, prove exact
unit multiplicity, and isolate the \(g=2r\) seed/selected-face boundary and
the fixed-support coefficient corollary.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why the matrix computation needs a proof chain}

There are four points at which a plausible matrix calculation could fail to
describe the polynomial iterates.  First, each competitive derivative row
contains two exponent vectors.  Selecting the pure endpoint row at the
ordinary seed does not show that the same row wins after the degree vector
has moved.  The open height face in \(\cK_{r,g}\) gives a uniform first
selector, while the lower faces keep the ratios in a range on which that
height comparison can be reused.

Second, the two endpoint comparisons occur at different times.  The first
one is evaluated on the \(q\)-degree vector \(u\).  The second one is
evaluated on \(v=Au\), because the \(W\)-gradient consumes the new
\(p\)-coordinates produced by \(S\).  Replacing that second input by \(u\)
would change its margin and would not analyze the displayed composition.
The sign split in \eqref{eq:DeltaT-negative} and
\eqref{eq:DeltaT-positive} is the algebraic record of this phase
dependence.

Third, a gradient update is a sum of a carried coordinate and several fresh
monomial sources.  Even after one monomial is uniquely largest inside the
gradient, the carried coordinate might match or exceed it.  The inequalities
\(A\one>\one\), \(C-I_r>0\), and the nonnegative nonzero rows of \(A\)
close this separate comparison.  They also explain why the induction is
labelled by half-steps rather than written only for full iterates.

Fourth, degrees of summands do not certify survival of a polynomial leading
form.  A top-degree product must be nonzero, and a second source of the same
degree could in principle cancel it.  Strict uniqueness removes the second
possibility, while the integral-domain identity
\(\LH(fg)=\LH(f)\LH(g)\neq0\) removes the first.  This argument is robust
under arbitrary nonzero scalar changes on the fixed supports, unlike an
argument based only on positivity of coefficients.

Only after these four gaps are closed does the last coordinate become an
observable for ordinary total degree.  The invariant splitting then analyzes
the spectrum of a matrix already known to govern actual degrees.  This order
prevents the cubic factor from being used retroactively as evidence for a
support selection or for noncancellation.

\subsection*{Bounded context}

Explicit degree calculations for affine-triangular automorphisms provide a
broader comparison setting in arbitrary dimension, including realization
questions for weak Perron numbers; dimension-four affine-triangular maps have
also received a specialized algebraic-degree analysis
\citep{BlancVanSanten2021,ShaoSun2025}.  Those works motivate finite matrix
descriptions but do not identify the displayed gradient family, select its
two endpoint rows, or provide the cone used here.  Every family-specific
identity in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is proved from the two potentials.

Higher-dimensional polynomial automorphisms can exhibit degree-growth
behavior not exhausted by the simplest planar alternatives
\citep{Deserti2016}.  This article addresses one narrow exponential
mechanism rather than classifying those behaviors.  Its main issue is the
passage from formal support scores to actual coordinate degrees at every
iterate.

Spectral interpretations of dynamical degrees provide general motivation for
asking when a spectral radius is visible to a degree observable
\citep{DangFavre2021}.  Here visibility is proved by a last-coordinate
comparison.  The equality \(\lambda_1(F_{r,g})=\rho(C)\) follows from the
exact degree formula, positivity of \(C\), and a positive functional, not
from contextual terminology.

Polynomial symplectic-map constructions and coupled H\'enon systems form
neighboring geometric settings \citep{Rangarajan2002,FujiokaKogawaLiShudo2023}.
Their construction and hyperbolicity questions differ from the support
selection problem treated here.  No hyperbolic, topological, or metric
conclusion is imported into this algebraic-degree calculation.

\subsection*{Proof roadmap}

The proof follows the composition word in twelve steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Section~\ref{sec:family} expands the gradients and proves subtraction
inverses and symplecticity.
\item Section~\ref{sec:selectors} reads every support literally and derives
\(M\), \(A\), \(B\), and \(C=BA\).
\item It then proves the first and second strict selectors in phase order.
\item Section~\ref{sec:cone} verifies the seed and every cone wall.
\item Section~\ref{sec:carry} treats the carried \(p\)-coordinates.
\item It treats the carried \(q\)-coordinates and proves leading-form
survival in the polynomial domain.
\item Section~\ref{sec:visibility} proves last-coordinate visibility for
\(n\geq1\) and the tied \(n=0\) degree identity.
\item Positivity and visibility then give the Perron--Frobenius limit.
\item Section~\ref{sec:cubic} constructs \(U\oplus E\) and computes \(Q\).
\item It derives the cubic, evaluates \(P(1)\), proves unit multiplicity, and
obtains the scalar recurrence.
\item Section~\ref{sec:boundary} audits \(g=2r\) and formal low-mode
consistency.
\item It proves the four-nonzero-coefficient fixed-support corollary and
records the limitations.
\end{enumerate}
No later step certifies an earlier support choice.

\section{The family, polynomial inverses, and symplectic geometry}\label{sec:family}

Fix a field \(K\) of characteristic zero and integers \(r\geq4\),
\(g\geq2r+1\).  Put \(h=g-1\) and \(m=r-2\), so
\(m\geq2\) and \(h\geq2m+4\).  Use coordinates
\[
q=(q_1,\ldots,q_r),\qquad p=(p_1,\ldots,p_r)
\]
on \(\Aff_K^{2r}\).  Ordinary total degree gives every initial coordinate
degree one.  For a polynomial map \(G=(G_1,\ldots,G_{2r})\), write
\(\degt(G)=\max_j\degt(G_j)\).  Submultiplicativity under composition gives
the algebraic limit
\[
\lambda_1(G)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\degt(G^n)^{1/n}.
\]

\subsection{Fixed potentials and phase order}

The two endpoint-spiked product potentials are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:family-potentials}
V_{r,g}(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}q_i^2+q_1^g,
\qquad
W_{r,g}(p)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_i^2+p_r^g.
\end{equation}
They define the block-triangular gradient shears
\begin{equation}\label{eq:family-shears}
S(q,p)=(q,p+\nabla V_{r,g}(q)),
\qquad
T(q,p)=(q+\nabla W_{r,g}(p),p),
\end{equation}
and throughout the article the word is fixed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:family-word}
F_{r,g}=T\circ S.
\end{equation}
Thus \(S\) updates the \(p\)-block from the current \(q\)-block, and only
then does \(T\) update the \(q\)-block from the newly produced
\(p\)-block.  Reversing the word would reverse the matrix product and would
define a different problem.

The term \emph{gradient} is used literally: the added vector is the gradient
of the displayed polynomial.  The maps in \eqref{eq:family-shears} are
finite polynomial shears; no convergence, time parameter, or analytic flow
is involved.  All later degree comparisons take place in the coordinate
ring of \(\Aff_K^{2r}\).

Two degree vectors appear because a full iterate contains two triangular
phases.  If \(f_1,\ldots,f_r\) are nonzero coordinate polynomials with
\(\degt f_i=u_i\), a monomial
\(\prod_i f_i^{a_i}\) has candidate degree \(\sum_i a_iu_i\).  Its top
homogeneous part is \(\prod_i\LH(f_i)^{a_i}\), so the candidate is the
actual degree whenever that product is nonzero.  Over a field, the
polynomial ring is a domain, and the product is indeed nonzero.  A sum of
different support monomials still requires a strict comparison: two equal
candidate degrees could interact.  Sections~\ref{sec:selectors} and
\ref{sec:carry} deliberately separate the score comparison from the
leading-form argument.

The matrices below act on ordinary degree vectors, not on the coordinate
polynomials themselves.  Their entries are exponent data.  The coefficient
field enters in two other ways: derivative scalars must remain nonzero, and
the direct-sum argument in Section~\ref{sec:cubic} divides by the nonzero
integer \(m\).  Characteristic zero is sufficient for both uses.  Neither
the inverse formulas nor the symplectic block calculation needs algebraic
closedness.

The full map can be written explicitly as
\[
F_{r,g}(q,p)=
\bigl(q+\nabla W_{r,g}(p+\nabla V_{r,g}(q)),
\ p+\nabla V_{r,g}(q)\bigr).
\]
This formula makes the phase order visible: the second gradient is evaluated
on the output of the first.  It also shows why the \(p\)-degree vector after
a full iterate is naturally the intermediate vector from the preceding
half-step.

\subsection{Gradients and derivative supports}

Differentiating the two potentials gives
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial V_{r,g}}{\partial q_1}
&=2q_1\prod_{j=2}^{r}q_j^2+gq_1^{g-1},
\label{eq:grad-V-first}\\
\frac{\partial V_{r,g}}{\partial q_i}
&=2q_i\prod_{j\neq i}q_j^2
\qquad(2\leq i\leq r),
\label{eq:grad-V-middle}\\
\frac{\partial W_{r,g}}{\partial p_i}
&=2p_i\prod_{j\neq i}p_j^2
\qquad(1\leq i<r),
\label{eq:grad-W-middle}\\
\frac{\partial W_{r,g}}{\partial p_r}
&=2p_r\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}p_j^2+gp_r^{g-1}.
\label{eq:grad-W-last}
\end{align}
Characteristic zero ensures that \(2\) and \(g\) are nonzero in \(K\), so
none of these supports disappears.

\subsection{Polynomial inverses}

The maps are triangular by blocks.  Since \(S\) fixes \(q\), and \(T\)
fixes \(p\), their inverses are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverses}
S^{-1}(q,p)=(q,p-\nabla V_{r,g}(q)),
\qquad
T^{-1}(q,p)=(q-\nabla W_{r,g}(p),p).
\end{equation}
Direct substitution verifies both identities.  Thus \(S\), \(T\), and
\(F_{r,g}=T\circ S\) are polynomial automorphisms over \(K\); algebraic
closedness is not used.

For completeness, composition in the reverse order of the inverse phases
gives the full inverse
\begin{equation}\label{eq:full-inverse}
F_{r,g}^{-1}(q,p)
=
\left(
q-\nabla W_{r,g}(p),\
p-\nabla V_{r,g}\bigl(q-\nabla W_{r,g}(p)\bigr)
\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, \(T^{-1}\) first recovers the old \(q\)-block while leaving \(p\)
fixed, and \(S^{-1}\) then recovers the old \(p\)-block from that
\(q\)-block.  This formula is polynomial over \(K\) and uses no division.

\subsection{Symplecticity}

Let
\[
\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{r}dq_i\wedge dp_i,\qquad
\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_r\\-I_r&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Writing \(H_V=\nabla^2V_{r,g}\) and \(H_W=\nabla^2W_{r,g}\), the Jacobians
in the coordinate order \((q,p)\) are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symplectic-blocks}
J_S=\begin{pmatrix}I_r&0\\H_V&I_r\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
J_T=\begin{pmatrix}I_r&H_W\\0&I_r\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Both Hessians are symmetric.  Block multiplication gives
\[
J_S^{\transpose}\Omega J_S
=\begin{pmatrix}H_V-H_V^{\transpose}&I_r\\-I_r&0\end{pmatrix}
=\Omega
\]
and
\[
J_T^{\transpose}\Omega J_T
=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_r\\-I_r&H_W^{\transpose}-H_W\end{pmatrix}
=\Omega.
\]
Equivalently, the added \(dq_i\wedge dq_j\) and \(dp_i\wedge dp_j\) terms
cancel in symmetric pairs.

One can see the same cancellation directly.  Under \(S\),
\[
dp_i\longmapsto dp_i+\sum_j
\frac{\partial^2V_{r,g}}{\partial q_i\partial q_j}\,dq_j.
\]
The extra contribution to \(S^*\omega\) is a sum of
\(V_{ij}\,dq_i\wedge dq_j\).  The terms for \((i,j)\) and \((j,i)\)
cancel because \(V_{ij}=V_{ji}\), and the diagonal wedge terms vanish.
The pullback by \(T\) is the analogous calculation with \(p\)-variables and
\(H_W\).  This coordinate check also shows that no analytic notion of a
Hamiltonian flow is being used; the assertion is a polynomial identity over
\(K\).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:symplectic}
The maps \(S\), \(T\), and \(F_{r,g}\) are polynomial symplectic
automorphisms of \(\Aff_K^{2r}\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Polynomial invertibility follows from \eqref{eq:inverses}.  The two block
identities following \eqref{eq:symplectic-blocks} prove preservation of
\(\omega\) by each shear and hence by their composition.
\end{proof}

Symplecticity fixes the geometric class of the maps but does not decide which
monomial wins in either competitive derivative.

\section{Gradient supports and the two strict selectors}\label{sec:selectors}

Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h-m}
h=g-1,\qquad m=r-2.
\end{equation}
Then \(m\geq2\) and \(h\geq2m+4\).  Let
\(u=(u_1,\ldots,u_r)^{\transpose}>0\) be a degree vector.  The score of a
monomial with exponent row \(a\) is \(au\).

\subsection{Literal support rows}

Every mixed derivative in
\eqref{eq:grad-V-first}--\eqref{eq:grad-W-last} has exponent one in its own
coordinate and exponent two in every other coordinate.  Its score in row
\(i\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-score}
u_i+2\sum_{j\neq i}u_j=2\sum_{j=1}^{r}u_j-u_i.
\end{equation}
Consequently the mixed rows form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
M=2\one\one^{\transpose}-I_r.
\end{equation}

There are exactly two competitive rows.  If their pure supports win, the
phase matrices are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-B-def}
\begin{aligned}
A&=M+e_1\bigl(he_1^{\transpose}-e_1^{\transpose}M\bigr),\\
B&=M+e_r\bigl(he_r^{\transpose}-e_r^{\transpose}M\bigr),\\
C&=BA.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The order is forced by \(F_{r,g}=T\circ S\): \(A\) acts first, and \(B\)
acts on the resulting vector.

The replacement description can also be checked derivative by derivative.
For \(2\leq i\leq r\), the \(i\)-th derivative of \(V_{r,g}\) has only
the mixed exponent row \(e_i^{\transpose}M\); therefore the corresponding
row of \(A\) cannot change with the input vector.  Only the first derivative
contains the additional exponent row \(he_1^{\transpose}\).  The roles are
reversed for \(W_{r,g}\): rows \(1,\ldots,r-1\) are forced mixed rows, and
only row \(r\) can switch.  Thus the matrix description has exactly two
logical hypotheses.  There is no unrecorded comparison in a middle row.

This literal ledger also explains why coefficients are absent from the
matrix entries.  A nonzero scalar changes a leading coefficient, not an
exponent score.  Coefficients can be ignored only after checking that they
are nonzero and after proving that the selected leading form cannot cancel.
Those checks are postponed to the phase induction, where the coordinate
polynomials are available.

\subsection{The normalized cone and first selector}

For \(u>0\), define only
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratios}
x_i=\frac{u_i}{u_1}\quad(2\leq i\leq r),
\qquad
\sigma=\sum_{i=2}^{r}x_i.
\end{equation}
The index \(1\) is excluded from \(\sigma\).

\begin{definition}\label{def:selector-cone}
The \emph{explicit sufficient invariant selector cone} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cone}
\cK_{r,g}=
\left\{u\in\R_{>0}^r:
x_i\geq1\ (2\leq i\leq r),\quad
\sigma<\frac{h-1}{2}=\frac{g-2}{2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The lower inequalities alone imply
\[
\sigma=x_2+\cdots+x_r\geq r-1=m+1.
\]
The parameter bound leaves genuine room above this lower corner, because
\[
\frac{h-1}{2}-(m+1)
=\frac{h-2m-3}{2}\geq\frac12.
\]
Thus the cone conditions are compatible already at the least allowed
parameter.  The same quantity \(h-2m-3\) will appear in every reduced
strict margin, making it possible to audit the threshold without replacing
the open height inequality by a non-strict closure.

In the first \(V\)-row, pure minus mixed is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-margin}
hu_1-\left(u_1+2\sum_{i=2}^{r}u_i\right)
=u_1(h-1-2\sigma)>0.
\end{equation}
Put \(\delta=h-1-2\sigma>0\).  The first pure support is uniquely selected,
and all other \(V\)-rows have one support.  For \(v=Au\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v-normalized}
\frac{v_1}{u_1}=h,\qquad
\frac{v_i}{u_1}=2+2\sigma-x_i\quad(2\leq i\leq r).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The second selector after the first phase}

The last pure support of the \(W\)-gradient is tested on \(v=Au\), never
directly on \(u\).  Its pure-minus-mixed margin is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DeltaT-def}
\Delta_T=(h-1)v_r-2\sum_{j<r}v_j.
\end{equation}
There are \(m\) middle indices before \(r\), and
\[
\frac1{u_1}\sum_{j<r}v_j
=h+\sum_{j=2}^{r-1}(2+2\sigma-x_j)
=h+2m+(2m-1)\sigma+x_r.
\]
Also \(v_r/u_1=2+2\sigma-x_r\).  Substitution in
\eqref{eq:DeltaT-def} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DeltaT}
\frac{\Delta_T}{u_1}
=(2h-4m)\sigma-(h+1)x_r-4m-2.
\end{equation}
There are \(m\) middle variables, each at least one, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xr-upper}
x_r\leq\sigma-m.
\end{equation}
Indeed,
\[
\sigma-x_r=\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}x_i\geq m.
\]
This inequality retains the last ratio as the distinguished endpoint
variable; it does not symmetrize \(x_r\) with the \(m\) middle ratios.
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DeltaT-reduced}
\frac{\Delta_T}{u_1}
\geq(h-4m-1)\sigma+m(h-3)-2.
\end{equation}
If \(h-4m-1<0\), multiplying
\(\sigma<(h-1)/2\) by that negative coefficient reverses the inequality:
\begin{align}
\frac{\Delta_T}{u_1}
&>\frac{(h-4m-1)(h-1)}2+m(h-3)-2 \notag\\
&=\frac{(h+1)(h-2m-3)}2>0.
\label{eq:DeltaT-negative}
\end{align}
If \(h-4m-1\geq0\), the lower bound \(\sigma\geq m+1\) gives
\begin{align}
\frac{\Delta_T}{u_1}
&\geq(h-4m-1)(m+1)+m(h-3)-2 \notag\\
&=(2m+1)(h-2m-3)>0.
\label{eq:DeltaT-positive}
\end{align}
Both cases are strict because \(h-2m-3\geq1\).

At \(h=2m+4\), the common decisive factor is exactly one.  The remaining
factors in the two bounds are \(h+1>0\) and \(2m+1>0\), respectively.
Hence the endpoint selector is already strict at the least parameter; no
unwritten strengthening of \(g\geq2r+1\) is used.  The alternatives
\(h-4m-1<0\) and \(h-4m-1\geq0\) are exhaustive, including the zero
coefficient in the second case.

\begin{proposition}[Strict phase selectors]\label{prop:selectors}
For \(u\in\cK_{r,g}\), the first gradient phase uniquely selects the rows
of \(A\).  For \(v=Au\), the second phase uniquely selects the rows of
\(B\).  These are the only two competitions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first margin is \eqref{eq:first-margin}.  The second is
\eqref{eq:DeltaT-negative} or \eqref{eq:DeltaT-positive}.  The literal
ledger shows that every other derivative row has one support.
\end{proof}

The two sign cases are not a partition of the parameter range into different
theorems.  They identify which boundary of the same cone minimizes a linear
margin.  When the coefficient of \(\sigma\) is negative, the infimum is
approached at the open height face, and the strict input inequality is what
produces a strict lower bound.  When it is nonnegative, the lower faces
\(x_i\geq1\) supply the minimum.  Retaining both cases avoids replacing a
valid inequality by one with the wrong direction near the height boundary.

\section{Seed containment and strict cone invariance}\label{sec:cone}

The selectors apply at a single input vector.  To iterate them, one must
show that a complete phase pair returns the vector to the same cone.  This
requires the full product \(C=BA\).

\subsection{The complete-step rows}

We multiply \(B\) and \(A\) row by row.  The first row of \(B\) has entries
\(1,2,\ldots,2\), while the first column of \(A\) is
\((h,2,\ldots,2)^{\transpose}\).  Hence
\[
C_{11}=h+4(r-1)=h+4m+4.
\]
For \(j\geq2\), the sum of column \(j\) of \(A\) over rows
\(2,\ldots,r\) is \(1+2(r-2)=2m+1\), so \(C_{1j}=4m+2\).

For a middle row \(2\leq i<r\), the first entry of the \(B\)-row is two,
and the sum of its remaining entries is \(2m+1\).  Thus
\(C_{i1}=2h+4m+2\).  For \(j\geq2\),
\[
C_{ij}
=\sum_{k=2}^{r}
(2-\mathbf1_{\{i=k\}})(2-\mathbf1_{\{k=j\}})
=4m+\mathbf1_{\{i=j\}}.
\]
The last row of \(B\) is \(he_r^{\transpose}\), while the last row of
\(A\) is the last row of \(M\), giving \(C_{rj}=2h\) for \(j<r\) and
\(C_{rr}=h\).  The complete entry ledger is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C-ledger}
\begin{aligned}
C_{11}&=h+4m+4,&
C_{1j}&=4m+2 &&(2\leq j\leq r),\\
C_{i1}&=2h+4m+2 &&(2\leq i<r),&
C_{ij}&=4m+\mathbf1_{\{i=j\}}
&&(2\leq i<r,\ 2\leq j\leq r),\\
C_{rj}&=2h &&(1\leq j<r),&
C_{rr}&=h.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For a middle row, normalization can be seen directly:
\[
\frac{(Cu)_i}{u_1}
=2h+4m+2+
\sum_{j=2}^{r}(4m+\mathbf1_{\{i=j\}})x_j
=2h+4m+2+4m\sigma+x_i.
\]
For the last row,
\[
\frac{(Cu)_r}{u_1}
=2h\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{r-1}x_j\right)+hx_r
=h(2+2\sigma-x_r).
\]
After division by \(u_1\), the three row types are
\begin{align}
\frac{(Cu)_1}{u_1}
&=h+4m+4+(4m+2)\sigma,
\label{eq:C-row-first}\\
\frac{(Cu)_i}{u_1}
&=2h+4m+2+4m\sigma+x_i
\qquad(2\leq i<r),
\label{eq:C-row-middle}\\
\frac{(Cu)_r}{u_1}
&=h(2+2\sigma-x_r).
\label{eq:C-row-last}
\end{align}

\subsection{The normalized complete-step map}

The row formulas also give the complete normalized transformation explicitly.
Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-denominator}
D=h+4m+4+(4m+2)\sigma=\frac{(Cu)_1}{u_1}.
\end{equation}
Because \(D>0\), the ratios of \(Cu\) relative to its first coordinate are
\begin{align}
x_i'
&=\frac{2h+4m+2+4m\sigma+x_i}{D}
\qquad(2\leq i<r),
\label{eq:normalized-middle}\\
x_r'
&=\frac{h(2+2\sigma-x_r)}{D}.
\label{eq:normalized-last}
\end{align}
Thus every output inequality can be checked without an implicit
projectivization convention.  The lower wall \(x_i'\geq1\) is equivalent to
\((Cu)_i-(Cu)_1\geq0\), since the common denominator is positive.  The
output height inequality
\(\sum_{i=2}^{r}x_i'<(h-1)/2\) is equivalent to
\[
(h-1)(Cu)_1-2\sum_{i=2}^{r}(Cu)_i>0.
\]
These are exactly the quantities treated below.

For reference, summing \eqref{eq:normalized-middle} over the \(m\) middle
indices and then adding \eqref{eq:normalized-last} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-height-map}
\sum_{i=2}^{r}x_i'
=\frac{
m(2h+4m+2+4m\sigma)+(\sigma-x_r)
+h(2+2\sigma-x_r)}
{D}.
\end{equation}
No estimate has entered this identity.  It makes clear that the normalized
system has precisely \(m+1\) lower walls and one height wall: the denominator
introduces no new sign condition, and the radial coordinate has disappeared.
The proof below works with the cross-multiplied forms because their
factorizations expose the sharp parameter \(h-2m-3\).

\subsection{Seed containment}

At the ordinary seed \(u_0=\one\), all ratios equal one and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:seed-sigma}
\sigma(\one)=r-1=m+1.
\end{equation}
Since \(h\geq2m+4\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:seed-inside}
m+1<\frac{2m+3}{2}\leq\frac{h-1}{2}.
\end{equation}
Thus \(\one\) lies strictly in \(\cK_{r,g}\).

\subsection{Every lower wall}

Let \(U'=Cu\).  For \(2\leq i<r\), subtracting
\eqref{eq:C-row-first} from \eqref{eq:C-row-middle} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:middle-wall}
\frac{U'_i-U'_1}{u_1}
=(h-1-2\sigma)+(x_i-1)>0.
\end{equation}
The first summand is the strict selector margin, and the second is
nonnegative.  Hence every middle input lower face maps strictly above its
output lower face.

For the last coordinate,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:last-wall-L1}
L_1:=\frac{U'_r-U'_1}{u_1}
=h-4m-4+(2h-4m-2)\sigma-hx_r.
\end{equation}
Using \(x_r\leq\sigma-m\) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:last-wall-reduced}
L_1\geq(m+1)(h-4)+(h-4m-2)\sigma.
\end{equation}
If \(h-4m-2<0\), the open height inequality reverses after multiplication:
\begin{align}
L_1
&>(m+1)(h-4)+\frac{(h-4m-2)(h-1)}2 \notag\\
&=\frac{(h+2)(h-2m-3)}2>0.
\label{eq:last-wall-negative}
\end{align}
If \(h-4m-2\geq0\), the lower bound \(\sigma\geq m+1\) gives
\begin{align}
L_1
&\geq(m+1)(h-4)+(h-4m-2)(m+1) \notag\\
&=2(m+1)(h-2m-3)>0.
\label{eq:last-wall-positive}
\end{align}
The last lower face therefore also maps strictly above one.

\subsection{The open height wall}

The output height inequality is equivalent, because \(U'_1>0\), to
positivity of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H2-def}
H_2=(h-1)U'_1-2\sum_{i=2}^{r}U'_i.
\end{equation}
Substitution of the three normalized rows gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H2-expanded}
\frac{H_2}{u_1}
=h^2-h-8m^2-8m-4+c\sigma+2(h+1)x_r,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c-height}
c=(4m-2)h-8m^2-4m-4.
\end{equation}

For clarity, the collection in \eqref{eq:H2-expanded} uses
\[
\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}x_i=\sigma-x_r
\]
and exactly \(m\) middle rows.  Before final collection, the normalized
height form is
\begin{align*}
\frac{H_2}{u_1}
={}&(h-1)\bigl(h+4m+4+(4m+2)\sigma\bigr)\\
&-2m(2h+4m+2+4m\sigma)
-2(\sigma-x_r)\\
&-2h(2+2\sigma-x_r).
\end{align*}
The coefficient of \(x_r\) becomes \(2+2h=2(h+1)\); the remaining
ratio terms combine into \(c\sigma\).  This intermediate line accounts for
all \(m+1\) output ratios and prevents the last coordinate from being
counted twice.

At \(h=2m+4\), one has \(c=8m-12>0\), and \(c\) increases with \(h\).
Both variable coefficients in \eqref{eq:H2-expanded} are positive.  Using
\(\sigma\geq m+1\) and \(x_r\geq1\), exact collection yields
\begin{align}
\frac{H_2}{u_1}
&\geq h^2-h-8m^2-8m-4+c(m+1)+2(h+1) \notag\\
&=(h-2m-3)(h+4m^2+4m+2)>0.
\label{eq:height-bound}
\end{align}
This is precisely the strict output height condition.  At the least
parameter \(h=2m+4\), every decisive factor \(h-2m-3\) equals one; there
is no hidden threshold equality.

\begin{theorem}[Strict cone invariance]\label{thm:cone-invariance}
The seed lies in \(\cK_{r,g}\), both selectors are strict there, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cone-invariance}
C\cK_{r,g}\subset\cK_{r,g}.
\end{equation}
Every output lower inequality and the output height inequality is strict.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Seed containment is \eqref{eq:seed-inside}.  The selectors are
Proposition~\ref{prop:selectors}.  The middle, last, and height output
conditions are respectively \eqref{eq:middle-wall},
\eqref{eq:last-wall-negative}--\eqref{eq:last-wall-positive}, and
\eqref{eq:height-bound}.
\end{proof}

The theorem proves sufficiency and invariance.  It does not say that the cone
is maximal, necessary, unique, exact, or a classification.

The wall audit is complete because the cone is homogeneous.  Division by the
positive first coordinate removes the radial direction, leaving the closed
lower inequalities \(x_i\geq1\) and one open affine height inequality.
Equation \eqref{eq:middle-wall} treats each of the \(m\) middle lower faces,
the two-case calculation treats the last lower face, and
\eqref{eq:height-bound} treats the only upper face.  Positivity of the first
output coordinate is automatic from \eqref{eq:C-row-first}.  No additional
normalized wall remains.

Strict inward movement is stronger than mere invariance and is used twice
later.  It keeps the selectors away from a tie at every finite iterate, and
the last-lower-wall inequality becomes the comparison between the last and
first \(q\)-degrees in Section~\ref{sec:visibility}.  The same symbolic
margin therefore serves two logically distinct conclusions only after each
has been stated with its proper input.

\section{Carried coordinates and leading-form survival}\label{sec:carry}

The cone selects a support within each fresh gradient.  Actual coordinate
updates also contain an old coordinate, so the fresh degree must beat that
carried term.  Once it does, the uniquely highest source still needs a
nonzero leading form.

\subsection{Phase-labelled vectors and carried terms}

Write
\[
F_{r,g}^n(q,p)=(q^{(n)},p^{(n)})
\]
and let
\[
u_n=(\degt q_1^{(n)},\ldots,\degt q_r^{(n)})^{\transpose}.
\]
After applying \(S\) to the \(n\)-th full iterate, let \(v_{n+1}\) be the
degree vector of
\[
p^{(n)}+\nabla V_{r,g}(q^{(n)}).
\]
Since \(T\) fixes that \(p\)-block, \(v_{n+1}\) is also the \(p\)-degree
vector after the next full iterate.  Initially \(u_0=\one\), and the
initial \(p\)-degree vector is \(\one\).

There are two maxima in each half-step.  For the \(i\)-th coordinate of the
first phase, the degree of
\[
p_i^{(n)}+\frac{\partial V_{r,g}}{\partial q_i}(q^{(n)})
\]
is the larger of the carried degree and the winning gradient degree, once
leading-form survival is known.  Proposition~\ref{prop:selectors} determines
the winner inside the gradient, but says nothing about \(p_i^{(n)}\).
Likewise, the \(i\)-th coordinate of the second phase compares
\(q_i^{(n)}\) with the winning degree in
\(\partial W_{r,g}/\partial p_i\), evaluated at the new \(p\)-block.  This
is why one needs both inequalities in Lemma~\ref{lem:carries}, row by row.

The entry ledger shows that \(C-I_r\) is entrywise positive.  In the first
row, subtraction leaves diagonal entry \(h+4m+3\) and other entries
\(4m+2\).  In a middle row it leaves first entry \(2h+4m+2\) and every
entry in columns \(2,\ldots,r\) equal to \(4m\).  In the last row it
leaves entries \(2h\) before the final position and \(h-1\) at that
position.  Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C-grows}
Cu>u\qquad(u>0).
\end{equation}
At the seed,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-seed}
A\one=(h,2r-1,\ldots,2r-1)^{\transpose}
=(h,2m+3,\ldots,2m+3)^{\transpose}>\one.
\end{equation}
Every row of \(A\) is nonnegative and nonzero.

\begin{lemma}[Carried-coordinate gaps]\label{lem:carries}
If \(u_n=Cu_{n-1}\) and \(v_n=Au_{n-1}\) for \(n\geq1\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p-carry-gap}
Au_n>Au_{n-1}=v_n
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-carry-gap}
BAu_n-u_n=(C-I_r)u_n>0.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:C-grows} gives \(u_n>u_{n-1}\).  Each nonnegative
nonzero row of \(A\) preserves strict componentwise growth, proving
\eqref{eq:p-carry-gap}.  Positivity of \(C-I_r\) proves
\eqref{eq:q-carry-gap}.
\end{proof}

Equation \eqref{eq:A-seed} handles the first carried \(p\)-block, and
\eqref{eq:q-carry-gap} with \(u_0=\one\) handles the first carried
\(q\)-block.  Lemma~\ref{lem:carries} supplies both gaps at later phases.

\subsection{Leading forms in a polynomial domain}

Let
\[
R=K[q_1,\ldots,q_r,p_1,\ldots,p_r].
\]
For nonzero \(f\in R\), denote its highest-total-degree homogeneous form by
\(\LH(f)\).

\begin{lemma}[Leading-form survival]\label{lem:leading-forms}
For nonzero \(f,g\in R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LH-product}
\LH(fg)=\LH(f)\LH(g)\neq0.
\end{equation}
If \(\degt f>\degt g\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LH-sum}
\LH(f+g)=\LH(f).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The component of degree \(\degt f+\degt g\) in \(fg\) is
\(\LH(f)\LH(g)\).  The polynomial ring is an integral domain, so this
product is nonzero.  If \(\degt f>\degt g\), no term of \(g\) has the top
degree of \(f\), proving the sum identity.
\end{proof}

In an \(S\)-phase, Proposition~\ref{prop:selectors} gives one uniquely
highest derivative support.  After substitution, its top form is a nonzero
scalar times a product of powers of prior nonzero leading forms.  The scalar
is \(2\) or \(g\), nonzero in characteristic zero, and
\eqref{eq:LH-product} makes the product nonzero.  The carried
\(p\)-coordinate has strictly smaller degree by \eqref{eq:A-seed} or
\eqref{eq:p-carry-gap}, so it cannot cancel that form.

The \(T\)-phase is identical in logic but uses the second selector on
\(v=Au\).  Its selected product survives by the domain property, and the
old \(q\)-coordinate has smaller degree by \eqref{eq:q-carry-gap}.  No
positive-coefficient assumption is used.

To see the induction at the level of an individual support, consider a mixed
row \(i\).  Its substituted monomial has top form
\[
2\LH(f_i)\prod_{j\neq i}\LH(f_j)^2,
\]
where the \(f_j\) are the current coordinates in the relevant block.  Each
\(\LH(f_j)\) is nonzero by induction, so the product is nonzero.  In an
endpoint pure row, the corresponding form is
\[
g\LH(f_1)^h
\quad\text{or}\quad
g\LH(f_r)^h.
\]
The selector makes exactly one of the mixed and pure forms higher in degree;
they are never added at equal top degree on the cone.  The carry comparison
then makes the entire winning gradient form higher than the old coordinate.
Thus every use of \eqref{eq:LH-sum} has a strict premise.

This separation also handles possible dependence among the coordinate
polynomials.  The factors need not involve disjoint variables, nor must
their coefficients have a common sign.  The integral-domain property alone
ensures that a product of their nonzero highest forms is nonzero.  No
monomial-order refinement or coefficient ordering is required.

\begin{theorem}[Exact phase recurrence]\label{thm:phase-recurrence}
For every \(n\geq0\), the actual coordinate-degree vectors satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase-recurrence}
v_{n+1}=Au_n,\qquad u_{n+1}=Cu_n.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:un-vn}
u_n=C^n\one\quad(n\geq0),\qquad
v_n=AC^{n-1}\one\quad(n\geq1),
\end{equation}
and \(u_n\in\cK_{r,g}\) for every \(n\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use a simultaneous half-step induction.  Its assertions at full time
\(n\) are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item every \(q_i^{(n)}\) and \(p_i^{(n)}\) has a nonzero highest
homogeneous form;
\item the \(q\)-degree vector is \(u_n=C^n\one\) and belongs to
\(\cK_{r,g}\);
\item for \(n\geq1\), the \(p\)-degree vector is
\(v_n=AC^{n-1}\one\).
\end{enumerate}
At time zero, all coordinate polynomials are variables, so their highest
forms are nonzero, \(u_0=\one\), and seed containment gives
\(u_0\in\cK_{r,g}\).

Apply \(S\) first.  Proposition~\ref{prop:selectors} chooses the row of
\(A\) in every derivative.  In a mixed row, the selected substituted
highest form is
\[
2\LH(q_i^{(0)})\prod_{j\neq i}\LH(q_j^{(0)})^2;
\]
in the first row it is \(g\LH(q_1^{(0)})^h\).
Both are nonzero.  Their degrees form \(Au_0\), while
\eqref{eq:A-seed} says that they strictly exceed the coordinatewise degrees
of the carried initial \(p\)-block.  Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-forms} therefore
shows that the new \(p\)-coordinates have nonzero highest forms and degree
vector
\[
v_1=Au_0.
\]

The second selector is now evaluated on this new block, as required by the
composition order.  It chooses the rows of \(B\), and the same product
argument makes every selected \(W\)-gradient form nonzero.  The fresh
degree vector is \(Bv_1=Cu_0\).  Since
\((C-I_r)u_0>0\), each fresh degree strictly exceeds the degree of the
corresponding carried \(q_i^{(0)}\).  Another application of the sum part
of Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-forms} gives
\[
u_1=Cu_0
\]
and nonzero highest forms in the new \(q\)-block.  The \(T\)-phase leaves
the new \(p\)-block fixed.  Finally, strict cone invariance places \(u_1\)
back in \(\cK_{r,g}\).  This proves all three assertions at time one and
also records both half-steps of the base case.

Suppose now that the assertions hold at some \(n\geq1\).  The first
selector applies because \(u_n\in\cK_{r,g}\).  It gives selected
gradient degrees \(Au_n\), with nonzero selected highest forms by the
domain product identity.  The carried \(p\)-degree vector is
\[
v_n=Au_{n-1}.
\]
Moreover \(u_n=Cu_{n-1}>u_{n-1}\) by \eqref{eq:C-grows}, and every row
of \(A\) is nonnegative and nonzero.  Hence
\[
Au_n>Au_{n-1}=v_n.
\]
The carried term is strictly lower in every coordinate, so after the
\(S\)-phase the new \(p\)-degree vector is exactly
\[
v_{n+1}=Au_n,
\]
and all its highest forms remain nonzero.

For the following \(T\)-phase, the second selector applies to
\(v_{n+1}=Au_n\) and chooses \(B\).  Its selected gradient degrees are
\[
Bv_{n+1}=BAu_n=Cu_n.
\]
They strictly exceed the carried \(q\)-degrees \(u_n\), because
\((C-I_r)u_n>0\).  Their selected highest forms are again nonzero products
of the just-established \(p\)-highest forms.  Thus no selected term can
vanish and no carried coordinate can cancel it.  The new \(q\)-degree
vector is
\[
u_{n+1}=Cu_n,
\]
all \(q\)-highest forms are nonzero, and cone invariance gives
\(u_{n+1}\in\cK_{r,g}\).  This closes the simultaneous induction.
Iterating the two vector identities yields \eqref{eq:un-vn}.
\end{proof}

The induction proves more than the scalar degree of the full map.  It records
the degree of every coordinate after each half-step, preserves a strict
selector certificate for the next input, and supplies nonzero leading forms
for the next product substitution.  These three outputs are all needed to
repeat the argument.  Keeping them together rules out a circular proof in
which the matrix recurrence is assumed in order to establish the cone that
was supposed to justify the recurrence.

The recurrence now describes actual coordinate degrees.  Ordinary map degree
still requires a coordinate that sees the maximum.

\section{Last-coordinate visibility, exact degrees, and Perron growth}\label{sec:visibility}

At a full iterate, the \(q\)-degree vector is \(Cu\) and the accompanying
\(p\)-degree vector is \(Au\).  We compare those blocks coordinatewise and
then compare the last \(q\)-row with every other \(q\)-row.

\subsection{Full-step versus half-step degrees}

Direct subtraction shows that \(C-A\) is entrywise positive.  Its first row
is
\[
(4m+4,4m+2,\ldots,4m+2).
\]
For a middle row \(2\leq i<r\), its first entry is \(2h+4m\), and its
entry in column \(j\geq2\) is
\[
4m-2+2\mathbf1_{\{i=j\}}.
\]
Its last row has entries \(2h-2\) in columns \(1,\ldots,r-1\) and
\(h-1\) in the final column.  All are positive for \(m\geq2\) and
\(h\geq2m+4\).  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C-minus-A}
Cu>Au\qquad(u>0).
\end{equation}
Every full-step \(q_i\)-degree therefore exceeds the intermediate
\(p_i\)-degree with the same index.

This indexwise comparison suffices for the whole \(p\)-block once the
largest \(q\)-row is known.  If \(q_r\) exceeds every \(q_i\), and each
\(q_i\) exceeds the corresponding \(p_i\), then \(q_r\) exceeds every
\(p_i\) as well.  Thus the remaining work is entirely a comparison among
the rows of \(C\); no separate last-row comparison with every row of \(A\)
is needed.

\subsection{The last row versus all other rows}

For \(u\in\cK_{r,g}\), the inequality
\((Cu)_r>(Cu)_1\) is exactly the last-lower-wall calculation
\eqref{eq:last-wall-negative}--\eqref{eq:last-wall-positive}.  Fix a
middle index \(2\leq i<r\) and put
\[
s=\sum_{j=2}^{r-1}x_j.
\]
Then \(s\geq m\), \(\sigma=s+x_r\), and the open height condition gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xr-height}
x_r<\frac{h-1}{2}-s.
\end{equation}
Subtracting the middle normalized row from the last one gives
\begin{align}
L_i
&:=\frac{(Cu)_r-(Cu)_i}{u_1} \notag\\
&=(2h-4m)\sigma-hx_r-x_i-4m-2 \notag\\
&=(2h-4m)s+(h-4m)x_r-x_i-4m-2.
\label{eq:last-vs-middle}
\end{align}

If \(h-4m<0\), multiplication of \eqref{eq:xr-height} by this negative
number reverses the inequality:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:last-middle-neg-step}
L_i>hs-x_i+\frac{(h-4m)(h-1)}2-4m-2.
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{align}
hs-x_i
&=(h-1)x_i+
h\sum_{\substack{2\leq j<r\\j\neq i}}x_j \notag\\
&\geq(h-1)+h(m-1)=hm-1.
\label{eq:hs-bound}
\end{align}
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:last-middle-negative}
L_i>\frac{(h+2)(h-2m-3)}2>0.
\end{equation}

If \(h-4m\geq0\), every coefficient in
\eqref{eq:last-vs-middle} is minimized on the lower faces
\(x_2=\cdots=x_r=1\).  The coefficient of \(x_i\) is
\(2h-4m-1>0\), the other middle coefficients are \(2h-4m>0\), and the
\(x_r\)-coefficient is nonnegative.  Substitution yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:last-middle-positive}
L_i\geq(2m+1)(h-2m-3)>0.
\end{equation}
Thus the last component of \(Cu\) strictly exceeds every other component.

\begin{proposition}[Strict visibility for \(n\geq1\)]\label{prop:visibility}
For every \(n\geq1\), the last \(q\)-coordinate of \(F_{r,g}^n\) has
degree strictly larger than each of the other \(2r-1\) coordinate degrees.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The \(q\)-degree vector is \(u_n=C(C^{n-1}\one)\), and the \(p\)-degree
vector is \(v_n=A(C^{n-1}\one)\).  Cone invariance puts
\(C^{n-1}\one\) in \(\cK_{r,g}\).  The last-wall and
\eqref{eq:last-middle-negative}--\eqref{eq:last-middle-positive} comparisons
make the last component of \(u_n\) strictly largest in its block.
Equation \eqref{eq:C-minus-A} gives \(u_n>v_n\), completing the comparison
with the \(p\)-block.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact ordinary degree and the initial tie}

For \(n\geq1\), Proposition~\ref{prop:visibility} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-positive}
\degt(F_{r,g}^n)=e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one.
\end{equation}
The restriction \(n\geq1\) applies to strict visibility, not to the formula.
At \(n=0\), every coordinate degree is one, and
\[
e_r^{\transpose}C^0\one=e_r^{\transpose}\one=1.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Exact degrees]\label{thm:exact-degree}
For every \(n\geq0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-degree}
\degt(F_{r,g}^n)=e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one.
\end{equation}
Strict visibility holds for \(n\geq1\), whereas \(n=0\) is the tied
initial case.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Perron visibility}

The degree matrix has positive integer entries, so Perron--Frobenius is
applied to \(C\) as a real matrix; no ordering or embedding of the
coefficient field \(K\) is involved.  Positivity makes \(C\) primitive.
Hence \(\rho(C)>0\) is a simple eigenvalue, it has positive right and left
eigenvectors, and every other eigenvalue has modulus strictly below
\(\rho(C)\).

Choose positive Perron eigenvectors \(w\) and \(\ell\), normalized by
\(\ell^{\transpose}w=1\).  The rank-one spectral projection onto the
Perron line is \(w\ell^{\transpose}\).  Put
\[
\kappa=(e_r^{\transpose}w)(\ell^{\transpose}\one).
\]
This scalar is positive because the last-coordinate functional, the seed,
and both Perron vectors have positive entries.  Therefore there is a real
number \(0<\theta<\rho(C)\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:perron-expansion}
e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one
=
\kappa\rho(C)^n
+O(\theta^n).
\end{equation}
If a subdominant Jordan block is present, \(\theta\) may be chosen strictly
between its spectral modulus and \(\rho(C)\), absorbing the polynomial
factor in the usual way.

Thus the observable used in the exact degree formula cannot annihilate the
Perron component.  Dividing
\eqref{eq:perron-expansion} by \(\rho(C)^n\) gives a positive limiting
coefficient, and taking \(n\)-th roots yields
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\left(e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one\right)^{1/n}
=\rho(C).
\]
Combining this limit with Theorem~\ref{thm:exact-degree} proves:

\begin{corollary}[First dynamical degree]\label{cor:perron}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-rho}
\lambda_1(F_{r,g})=\rho(C).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

This is an algebraic-degree statement.  It is not an equality with
topological, metric, arithmetic, or measure-theoretic entropy.

\section{Unit modes, the equal-middle quotient, and cubic recurrence}\label{sec:cubic}

The middle rows of \(C\) are permutation-symmetric.  Difference vectors
among the middle coordinates separate from the three directions recording
the first endpoint, a common middle value, and the last endpoint.

\subsection{The unit space and direct complement}

Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U-def}
U=\left\{z\in K^r:z_1=z_r=0,\quad
\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}z_i=0\right\}.
\end{equation}
There are \(m\) middle coordinates and one relation, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U-dim}
\dim_K U=m-1=r-3.
\end{equation}
Every \(z\in U\) has total coordinate sum zero; therefore
\(Mz=-z\).  The replaced first row of \(A\) evaluates to \(hz_1=0\),
which equals \(-z_1\), and the replaced last row of \(B\) evaluates to
\(hz_r=0\).  Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U-actions}
Az=-z,\qquad Bz=-z,\qquad Cz=z\qquad(z\in U).
\end{equation}

Concretely, \(U\) is spanned by differences of middle coordinate vectors,
for example
\[
e_2-e_3,\ e_3-e_4,\ \ldots,\ e_{r-2}-e_{r-1}.
\]
There are \(r-3\) independent differences.  Each phase reverses them, while
a full step restores them.  This explains why adding a middle mode adds a
unit direction rather than another nontrivial spectral coordinate.

Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E-def}
E=\left\{(a,b,\ldots,b,c)^{\transpose}:a,b,c\in K\right\},
\end{equation}
with exactly \(m\) middle copies of \(b\).  A vector in \(U\cap E\) has
\(a=c=0\) and \(mb=0\).  Since \(m\neq0\) is invertible in the
characteristic-zero field, \(b=0\).  Dimension counting gives the invariant
direct sum
\begin{equation}\label{eq:direct-sum}
K^r=U\oplus E.
\end{equation}
The matrices preserve \(E\) because their middle rows are symmetric under
permutations of the middle coordinates.

The decomposition also has an explicit projection.  Given
\(z=(z_1,\ldots,z_r)^{\transpose}\in K^r\), set
\[
\bar z=\frac1m\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}z_i
\]
and define
\[
z_E=(z_1,\bar z,\ldots,\bar z,z_r)^{\transpose},
\qquad
z_U=z-z_E.
\]
Then \(z_E\in E\), while \(z_U\) has zero endpoint coordinates and its
middle coordinates sum to zero, so \(z_U\in U\).  This gives existence of
the splitting without relying only on dimensions; the earlier intersection
calculation gives uniqueness.  The factor \(1/m\) is the precise reason for
retaining the characteristic-zero assumption in this linear-algebra step.

Middle-coordinate permutations preserve both subspaces.  More concretely,
the output of any middle row of \(A\) or \(B\) depends on its distinguished
middle input and on the sum of all inputs.  Equal middle inputs therefore
produce equal middle outputs, proving \(A(E)\subseteq E\) and
\(B(E)\subseteq E\) directly.  Together with \eqref{eq:U-actions}, this
shows that the displayed direct sum is simultaneously invariant under both
half-step matrices, not merely under their product.

\subsection{The fixed equal-middle convention}

Fix the coordinate convention
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E-convention}
(a,b,c)\longmapsto
(a,\underbrace{b,\ldots,b}_{m\ {\rm entries}},c).
\end{equation}
Applying \(A\) gives first coordinate \(ha\), any middle coordinate
\[
2(a+mb+c)-b=2a+(2m-1)b+2c,
\]
and last coordinate
\[
2(a+mb+c)-c=2a+2mb+c.
\]
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AE}
A_E=
\begin{pmatrix}
h&0&0\\
2&2m-1&2\\
2&2m&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For \(B\), the first and middle rows retain their mixed form and the last
row is pure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BE}
B_E=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2m&2\\
2&2m-1&2\\
0&0&h
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Multiplication in phase order yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
Q_{m,h}=B_EA_E=
\begin{pmatrix}
h+4m+4 & 2m(2m+1) & 2(2m+1)\\
2h+4m+2 & 4m^2+1 & 4m\\
2h & 2mh & h
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\[
(1,2m,2)A_E
=\bigl(h+4m+4,\ 2m(2m+1),\ 2(2m+1)\bigr),
\]
which gives its first row.  The matrix \(Q_{m,h}\) is the restriction of
\(C\) to \(E\) in the fixed coordinate convention.

The second row follows from
\[
(2,2m-1,2)A_E
=\bigl(2h+4m+2,\ (2m-1)^2+4m,\ 4m\bigr),
\]
and \((2m-1)^2+4m=4m^2+1\).  The third row is
\[
(0,0,h)A_E=(2h,2mh,h).
\]
These calculations fix every entry relative to
\eqref{eq:E-convention}; changing the meaning of \(b\) from a common
coordinate to a sum of middle coordinates would produce a different matrix.

\begin{proposition}[Invariant splitting]\label{prop:splitting}
The sum \eqref{eq:direct-sum} is invariant under \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\).
Their restrictions to \(U\) are given by \eqref{eq:U-actions}; their
restrictions to \(E\) are \eqref{eq:AE}, \eqref{eq:BE}, and
\eqref{eq:Q}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The calculation on \(U\), the characteristic-zero intersection argument,
and the dimension count prove the direct sum.  Middle permutation symmetry
proves invariance of \(E\), and the displayed row calculations give the
three restriction matrices.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Trace, principal minors, and determinant}

The trace is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q-trace}
\tr Q_{m,h}=2h+4m^2+4m+5.
\end{equation}
The principal \(2\times2\) minors for coordinate pairs \(12\), \(13\), and
\(23\) are
\begin{align}
\Delta_{12}
&=h\bigl(1-4m(m+1)\bigr)+4,
\label{eq:minor-12}\\
\Delta_{13}
&=h(h-4m),
\label{eq:minor-13}\\
\Delta_{23}
&=h(1-4m^2).
\label{eq:minor-23}
\end{align}
For instance,
\[
\Delta_{13}
=h(h+4m+4)-4h(2m+1)=h(h-4m);
\]
the other two follow from the corresponding two-term determinants.  Their
sum is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minor-sum}
\Delta_{12}+\Delta_{13}+\Delta_{23}
=h^2-8hm(m+1)+2h+4.
\end{equation}

For transcription control, the other two expansions are
\begin{align*}
\Delta_{12}
&=(h+4m+4)(4m^2+1)
-2m(2m+1)(2h+4m+2)\\
&=h\bigl(1-4m(m+1)\bigr)+4,
\\
\Delta_{23}
&=h(4m^2+1)-4m(2mh)
=h(1-4m^2).
\end{align*}
Adding these to \(\Delta_{13}\) produces
\eqref{eq:minor-sum} without appealing to a determinant expansion of the
full \(r\times r\) matrix.
The determinant is clearest before multiplication:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det-AE-BE}
\det A_E=-h(2m+1),\qquad
\det B_E=-h(2m+1).
\end{equation}
Indeed, expansion along the first row of \(A_E\) and the last row of
\(B_E\), respectively, gives
\[
\det A_E
=h\bigl((2m-1)-4m\bigr),
\qquad
\det B_E
=h\bigl((2m-1)-4m\bigr).
\]
Both determinants are therefore nonzero in \(K\), although only their
product is needed for the characteristic polynomial of \(Q\).
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det-Q}
\det Q_{m,h}=h^2(2m+1)^2.
\end{equation}

\centering
\small
\caption{Three-dimensional invariant ledger for \(Q_{m,h}\).}
\label{tab:invariant-ledger}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{0.24\textwidth}X p{0.25\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Invariant & exact value & role in \(\det(tI-Q)\) \\
\midrule

\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

These invariants give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P-definition}
\begin{aligned}
\det(tI_3-Q_{m,h})
=P_{m,h}(t)
={}&t^3-(2h+4m^2+4m+5)t^2\\
&+(h^2-8hm(m+1)+2h+4)t\\
&-h^2(2m+1)^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
On \(U\), the characteristic polynomial is \((t-1)^{r-3}\).  The direct
sum therefore yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chi-factorization}
\chi_C(t)=(t-1)^{r-3}P_{m,h}(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The value at one and exact unit multiplicity}

Substitution into \eqref{eq:P-definition} gives
\begin{align}
P_{m,h}(1)
={}&1-(2h+4m^2+4m+5) \notag\\
&+(h^2-8hm(m+1)+2h+4)-h^2(2m+1)^2 \notag\\
= {}&-4m(m+1)h^2-8m(m+1)h-4m(m+1) \notag\\
= {}&-4m(m+1)(h+1)^2.
\label{eq:P-one}
\end{align}
This is nonzero in characteristic zero.  Hence \(C|_E\) has no unit
eigenvalue.  All generalized unit eigenvectors lie in \(U\), where \(C\)
is exactly the identity.

\begin{theorem}[Exact unit multiplicity]\label{thm:unit-multiplicity}
The eigenvalue \(1\) of \(C\) has algebraic and geometric multiplicity
exactly \(r-3\).  There is no quotient unit eigenvalue and no hidden unit
Jordan block.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The direct factorization gives at least the \(r-3\) unit roots contributed
by \(U\).  Equation \eqref{eq:P-one} excludes another root on \(E\).
Because \(C|_U=I_U\), the unit factor on \(U\) is semisimple.  Both
multiplicities are therefore \(\dim U=r-3\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The cubic annihilator}

The seed \(\one\) belongs to \(E\), represented by \((1,1,1)\) in
\eqref{eq:E-convention}.  Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:TSD}
\begin{aligned}
T_0&=2h+4m^2+4m+5,\\
S_0&=h^2-8hm(m+1)+2h+4,\\
D_0&=h^2(2m+1)^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Cayley--Hamilton on \(E\) gives
\[
Q_{m,h}^3-T_0Q_{m,h}^2+S_0Q_{m,h}-D_0I_3=0.
\]
Applying this identity to the orbit of \(\one\) and then applying
\(e_r^{\transpose}\) proves:

In the convention \eqref{eq:E-convention}, the seed is the column
\((1,1,1)^{\transpose}\), the action of \(C\) is \(Q_{m,h}\), and
restriction of \(e_r^{\transpose}\) is projection onto the third
coordinate.  Consequently,
\[
d_n
=
\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
Q_{m,h}^n
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Multiplying the matrix identity by the seed column on the right and the
third-coordinate row on the left gives the scalar relation with exactly the
same signs as \(P_{m,h}\).

The direct-sum viewpoint matters here.  Cayley--Hamilton for the full matrix
\(C\) would give an order-\(r\) relation containing the unit factor.  Since
the seed has equal middle coordinates, its \(U\)-component is zero, and its
entire orbit stays in \(E\).  The cubic identity therefore annihilates the
degree orbit directly, with no residual term from the \((r-3)\)-dimensional
unit sector.

\begin{theorem}[Cubic degree recurrence]\label{thm:cubic-recurrence}
For
\[
d_n=e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one=\degt(F_{r,g}^n),
\]
one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-recurrence}
d_{n+3}=T_0d_{n+2}-S_0d_{n+1}+D_0d_n
\qquad(n\geq0),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d0-d1}
d_0=1,\qquad d_1=h(2m+3).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The recurrence is the scalar image of Cayley--Hamilton.  The initial value
\(d_0=1\) is immediate.  The last row of \(C\) has \(m+1\) entries
equal to \(2h\) and one entry equal to \(h\), so
\[
d_1=2h(m+1)+h=h(2m+3).
\]
\end{proof}

The cubic is an annihilator.  It is not asserted to be minimal or
irreducible for every parameter, and \(\rho(C)\) need not always have
algebraic degree three.  The Perron class nevertheless belongs to \(E\):
\(C\) commutes with middle-coordinate permutations, and uniqueness of its
positive Perron ray forces that ray to be fixed by every such permutation.
Thus \(\rho(C)\) is a root of \(P_{m,h}\) without an irreducibility claim.

\section{Sharp boundary, low-mode consistency, fixed-support coefficients, limitations, and conclusion}\label{sec:boundary}

The theorem has three delicate edges: the first parameter excluded from the
strict seed cone, the formal three-mode substitution, and changes of
coefficients that preserve every support.  Each edge is an exact calculation,
not an invitation to broaden the theorem.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{\(g=2r\)}{g=2r} seed/selected-face boundary}

At the ordinary-degree seed, the first pure support has score
\(h=g-1\).  The competing mixed row has score
\[
1+2(r-1)=2r-1.
\]
Their difference is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary-gap}
(g-1)-\bigl(1+2(r-1)\bigr)=g-2r.
\end{equation}
When \(g=2r\), the two scores tie.  At the same parameter,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary-height}
\sigma(\one)=r-1=\frac{g-2}{2},
\end{equation}
so the seed lies on the boundary of the open height face in
\eqref{eq:cone}.  These are two descriptions of the same obstruction to
starting the present strict selection proof.

At the first allowed value \(g=2r+1\), the score gap
\eqref{eq:boundary-gap} is one.  Since then \(h=2m+4\), the height slack is
\[
\frac{h-1}{2}-(m+1)
=\frac{2m+3}{2}-(m+1)=\frac12.
\]
All later factored margins contain \(h-2m-3=1\), so the proof is strict
from its first admissible parameter onward.

\begin{proposition}[Boundary statement]\label{prop:boundary}
The inequality \(g\geq2r+1\) is sharp for the ordinary seed and the chosen
strict selected face: at \(g=2r\), the first selector ties and the seed lies
on the open cone-height boundary.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equations \eqref{eq:boundary-gap} and \eqref{eq:boundary-height} give the two
claims.  The gap-one and half-unit-slack calculations establish strictness at
\(g=2r+1\).
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:boundary} does not determine the actual degree dynamics
at \(g=2r\) or below.  A different selector region, a tie analysis, or a
different observable could behave differently.  Accordingly, the proposition
is not a global failure theorem and does not claim that the parameter range
is optimal for every possible degree description.

\subsection{Formal low-mode consistency}

The headline theorem begins at \(r\geq4\).  A useful transcription test is
to substitute formally \(r=3\), hence \(m=1\), only after the arbitrary-mode
proof is complete.  With \(h=g-1\), \eqref{eq:Q} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:formal-r3-Q}
Q_{1,g-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
g+7&6&6\\
2g+4&5&4\\
2(g-1)&2(g-1)&g-1
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The trace coefficient specializes to \(2g+11\).  The principal-minor sum is
\[
(g-1)^2-16(g-1)+2(g-1)+4
=g^2-16g+19,
\]
and the determinant is \(9(g-1)^2\).  Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:formal-r3-P}
P_{1,g-1}(t)
=t^3-(2g+11)t^2+(g^2-16g+19)t-9(g-1)^2.
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:formal-r3-Q} and \eqref{eq:formal-r3-P} constitute
formal low-mode consistency only.  They lie outside the stated range
\(r\geq4\), prove no additional \(r=3\) theorem, and do not alter the
headline result.

\subsection{Four nonzero coefficients on the fixed supports}

The support proof is insensitive to nonzero scalar changes but not to
support changes.  Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-potentials}
\begin{aligned}
V_{\alpha,\beta}(q)
&=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{r}q_i^2+\beta q_1^g,\\
W_{\gamma,\delta}(p)
&=\gamma\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_i^2+\delta p_r^g,
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in K^\times.
\end{equation}
Define the two gradient shears and their composition in the same order as
before.

The derivative scalars attached to the four displayed support types are
\[
2\alpha,\qquad g\beta,\qquad 2\gamma,\qquad g\delta.
\]
They are nonzero because the four coefficients are nonzero and \(K\) has
characteristic zero.  Thus every exponent row in
Table~\ref{tab:support-ledger} remains present.  Selector margins compare
degrees of distinct supports and are unchanged; the cone, its wall
inequalities, \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) are therefore unchanged as well.

It remains to justify arbitrary signs or phases of the coefficients.  At
each coordinate update, the strict selector and carry inequalities identify
one source of uniquely highest degree.  Its highest homogeneous form is one
of the nonzero scalars above times a product of powers of preceding nonzero
highest homogeneous forms.  The polynomial ring over \(K\) is a domain, so
that product is nonzero.  Because every other source has smaller degree,
none can cancel it.  This is the same induction as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-forms}, and it never uses coefficient positivity.

\begin{corollary}[Fixed-support coefficient extension]\label{cor:coefficients}
For the potentials in \eqref{eq:coefficient-potentials}, with
\(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in K^\times\), the phase recurrence, exact
degree identity, first dynamical degree, invariant splitting, quotient
matrix, characteristic factorization, exact unit multiplicity, and cubic
annihilator are the same as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The gradient shears retain subtraction inverses and symmetric Hessian blocks.
The four derivative scalars are nonzero, so the literal supports and every
strict comparison are unchanged.  The domain leading-form argument proves
noncancellation at every phase.  All subsequent conclusions depend only on
the resulting matrices and the visible degree identity.
\end{proof}

If one coefficient vanishes, its support can disappear.  If a monomial is
added, a new competitive face can appear.  An altered support, a different
shear word, or a positive-characteristic specialization therefore falls
outside Corollary~\ref{cor:coefficients}.

\subsection{Limitations}

The selector cone is explicit, sufficient, and invariant, but no maximality,
necessity, uniqueness, or classification statement is proved.  The theorem
fixes both supports and the word \(T\circ S\); it does not cover arbitrary
potentials, added monomials, arbitrary Hamiltonian maps, or other shear
words.  Characteristic zero is used to keep derivative coefficients and the
integer \(m\) nonzero.  Strict last-coordinate visibility begins at
\(n=1\), while \(n=0\) remains a tie.  The threshold calculation is sharp
only at the ordinary seed and the selected strict face.  Finally, the cubic
annihilates the degree sequence but need not be minimal or irreducible, so no
uniform algebraic-degree-three conclusion follows for the Perron root.

\subsection{Conclusion}

For every characteristic-zero field, \(r\geq4\), and \(g\geq2r+1\), the
displayed endpoint-spiked Hamiltonian product shears have the exact degree
and Perron identities
\[
\degt(F_{r,g}^n)=e_r^{\transpose}C^n\one,
\qquad
\lambda_1(F_{r,g})=\rho(C).
\]
The \(r\)-dimensional degree system splits as an
\((r-3)\)-dimensional semisimple unit space and a three-dimensional
equal-middle restriction.  Its characteristic polynomial is
\[
(t-1)^{r-3}P_{m,h}(t),
\]
and \(P_{m,h}\) supplies the exact cubic annihilating recurrence for the
degree sequence.  Increasing the number of modes enlarges the unit sector
without enlarging the nontrivial spectral dimension of this fixed family.
