\title{\textbf{Coupled Hamiltonian Shear Degree Matrices in \(\A^4\):}\\
An Asymmetric \(g\geq 5\) Family}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We prove an exact degree formula for a fixed family of canonical polynomial
automorphisms of four-dimensional affine space.  Let \(K\) be algebraically
closed of characteristic zero, let \(g\geq5\), and compose the two triangular
Hamiltonian shears generated by
\(V(q)=q_1^2q_2^2+q_1^g\) and
\(W(p)=p_1^2p_2^2+p_2^g\).
The mixed supports force a genuinely coupled two-phase degree calculation.
We show that the first shear selects the degree-transfer matrix
\(A_g=((g-1,0),(2,1))\), the second selects
\(B_g=((1,2),(0,g-1))\), and the complete step is governed by
\(C_g=B_gA_g\), not by the off-diagonal matrix that records the two half-steps.
A half-open ratio cone is invariant under \(C_g\); strict old-term gaps and
positive coefficients in characteristic zero prevent cancellation at every
iterate.  The second \(q\)-coordinate sees the Perron class, so
\[
 \deg(F_g^n)=e_2^{\mathsf T}C_g^n(1,1)^{\mathsf T},
 \qquad
 \lambda_1(F_g)=\rho(C_g)=(\sqrt g+1)^2.
\]
Each elementary shear has degree \(g-1\), whereas
\((\sqrt g+1)^2<(g-1)^2\).  The phrase \emph{non-product} is used only for the
displayed coordinate support and this strict degree comparison; no universal
non-conjugacy statement is claimed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Polynomial automorphisms admit degree-growth mechanisms that are invisible if
one only records the degree of each factor.  In the planar generalized Hénon
setting, reduced-word structure leads to a degree product law; this is a useful
baseline, not a theorem about every higher-dimensional composition
\citep{FriedlandMilnor1989,HenonSurvey2024}.  In higher dimension, several
growth regimes occur and algebraic stability requires hypotheses that must be
checked rather than inferred from a formal matrix
\citep{Deserti2018,GuedjSibony2002,FavreJonsson2011,DangFavre2021}.  Our aim is
to isolate one small family in which all of the checks can be made explicitly.

We study a composition of two canonical shears on
\(\A^4_K\), with \(q=(q_1,q_2)\) and \(p=(p_1,p_2)\).  The potentials
\[
 V(q)=q_1^2q_2^2+q_1^g,\qquad
 W(p)=p_1^2p_2^2+p_2^g
\]
are asymmetric: one pure power occurs in the first \(q\)-gradient row and the
other in the second \(p\)-gradient row.  The mixed quartic monomials keep the
two coordinates coupled.  We fix the word \(F_g=T\circ S\) before doing any
degree analysis:
\[
 S(q,p)=(q,p+\grad V(q)),\qquad
 T(q,p)=(q+\grad W(p),p).
\]
This order matters.  The first shear produces an intermediate \(p\)-degree
vector, and the second shear consumes that vector before the complete iterate
returns to a \(q\)-degree vector.

The central point is a phase-sensitive selector calculation.  If
\(u=(u_1,u_2)^{\mathsf T}\) records the degrees of the \(q\)-coordinates, the
selected rows of the first shear are
\[
 A_g=\begin{pmatrix}g-1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
After the intermediate vector \(v=A_gu\) is formed, the second shear selects
\[
 B_g=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&g-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The full return map is therefore
\[
 C_g=B_gA_g=
 \begin{pmatrix}g+3&2\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The matrix \(C_g\), rather than a half-step matrix, is the object whose Perron
root can control the degree sequence.

Three technical issues make the statement nonformal.  First, the selected
faces must dominate both competing monomials and the carried coordinate.  This
requires a cone in the \(q\)-phase and a separate comparison in the
intermediate \(p\)-phase.  Second, the cone must be invariant under the
complete matrix for every \(g\geq5\), not just at one initial step.  Third, a
spectral radius is not automatically a total degree: one must prove that the
initial vector reaches the positive Perron class and that a concrete coordinate
functional sees it.  We address these points by strict inequalities and an
explicit visibility argument.

The paper makes four bounded, internally provable contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item It gives an explicit symplectic polynomial automorphism with triangular
  inverses and a connected mixed support in the displayed split.
\item It proves a two-phase Newton-face selector for every integer \(g\geq5\),
  including the carried-coordinate inequalities and an invariant ratio cone.
\item It proves coefficientwise no-cancellation and the exact complete-step
  recurrence \(u_{n+1}=C_gu_n\), then identifies total degree through the
  \(q_2\)-coordinate.
\item It obtains the closed form
  \(\lambda_1(F_g)=(\sqrt g+1)^2\) and compares it strictly with
  \(\deg(S)\deg(T)=(g-1)^2\), without making a universal product or priority
  claim.
\end{enumerate}

The nearest four-dimensional coupled-symplectic literature studies a different
question, namely horseshoes and uniform hyperbolicity of coupled Hénon maps
\citep{Fujioka2023}.  We study algebraic degree and prove the relevant formula
from the displayed supports.  The remainder of the paper is organized around
that proof: Section~\ref{sec:related} fixes the bounded comparison boundary;
Section~\ref{sec:setup} defines the family and phase notation;
Sections~\ref{sec:canonical}--\ref{sec:selectors} establish canonicality and
face selection; Sections~\ref{sec:cone}--\ref{sec:visibility} prove the
recurrence and its observable degree; and Sections~\ref{sec:comparison}--\ref{sec:conclusion}
state the narrow non-product comparison, limitations, and conclusion.

\section{Bounded related work and collision boundary}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Planar degree products.}
The plane classification of polynomial automorphisms provides the classical
degree-product intuition for reduced compositions
\citep{FriedlandMilnor1989}.  We use that result only as a two-dimensional
baseline.  Our map lives in dimension four, is constrained to be canonical, and
has a mixed support that produces a non-diagonal complete-step matrix.  We do
not infer a contradiction with the planar law and do not claim a classification
of higher-dimensional degree growth.

\paragraph{Higher-dimensional degree growth.}
Examples in dimension at least three show that degree sequences need not fall
into the simplest planar regimes \citep{Deserti2018}.  General terminology for
algebraic stability and projective degree growth is useful here, but it does
not replace the face-by-face induction needed for this family
\citep{GuedjSibony2002}.  Valuation and compactification methods in dimension
two provide historical context for degree growth
\citep{FavreJonsson2011}; they are not invoked in the proof below.

\paragraph{Spectral language.}
Dynamical degrees are often interpreted through spectral radii under explicit
stability or visibility hypotheses \citep{GuedjSibony2002,DangFavre2021}.  In
our setting the relevant operator is finite and elementary, but the same
logical caution applies: the positive matrix alone is insufficient.  We prove
that its Perron class is reached from \((1,1)^{\mathsf T}\), and that
\(e_2^{\mathsf T}\) observes the maximal coordinate degree.

\paragraph{A four-dimensional neighbor.}
A coupled-Hénon literature in four dimensions studies symplectic coupling for
topological horseshoes and uniform hyperbolicity
\citep{Fujioka2023}.  This is a neighboring object, not a collision: our
potentials, degree-transfer matrices, cone, and closed-form algebraic degree
are different, and no hyperbolicity or topological-entropy statement appears
here.

\section{Fixed family, coordinates, and phase notation}\label{sec:setup}

Throughout, \(K\) is algebraically closed and has characteristic zero, and
\(g\geq5\) is an integer.  We work on
\(\A^4_K\) with coordinates
\((q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)\) and standard symplectic form
\[
 \omega=dq_1\wedge dp_1+dq_2\wedge dp_2.
\]
Set
\[
 V(q)=q_1^2q_2^2+q_1^g,\qquad
 W(p)=p_1^2p_2^2+p_2^g.
\]
The two triangular maps and their composition are
\[
 S(q,p)=(q,p+\grad V(q)),\qquad
 T(q,p)=(q+\grad W(p),p),\qquad
 F_g=T\circ S.
\]
Writing
\(\widehat p=p+\grad V(q)\), the coordinates of one complete step are
\begin{align}
 \widehat p_1&=p_1+2q_1q_2^2+gq_1^{g-1},&
 \widehat p_2&=p_2+2q_1^2q_2,\label{eq:phat}\\
 F_{g,1}&=q_1+2\widehat p_1\widehat p_2^2,&
 F_{g,2}&=q_2+2\widehat p_1^2\widehat p_2+
 g\widehat p_2^{g-1},\label{eq:qout}\\
 F_{g,3}&=\widehat p_1,&
 F_{g,4}&=\widehat p_2.\label{eq:pout}
\end{align}
The formulas, rather than a formal Jacobian, determine the degree-transfer
rows below.

For a polynomial \(h\), \(\deg h\) means total degree.  For a polynomial map,
\(\deg F\) is the maximum of its coordinate degrees.  Define
\[
 q_{i,n}=q_i\circ F_g^n,\qquad
 p_{i,n}=p_i\circ F_g^n,
\]
and set
\[
 u_n=\begin{pmatrix}\deg q_{1,n}\\ \deg q_{2,n}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The intermediate vector \(v_{n+1}\) records the \(p\)-degrees immediately
after the \(S\)-phase applied to \(F_g^n\), before the \(T\)-phase:
\[
 v_{n+1}=
 \begin{pmatrix}\deg(p_{1,n}+\partial_{q_1}V(q_n))\\
 \deg(p_{2,n}+\partial_{q_2}V(q_n))\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For the carried initial coordinates we set \(u_0=v_0=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\).
Thus \(v_n\) and \(u_n\) have different phase meanings even when their
entries happen to have comparable size.

The four selected monomials and their degree rows are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{phase row} & \text{selected monomial} & \text{degree in the input vector}\\
\midrule
S,\ 1 & q_1^g & (g-1)u_1\\
S,\ 2 & q_1^2q_2^2 & 2u_1+u_2\\
T,\ 1 & p_1^2p_2^2 & v_1+2v_2\\
T,\ 2 & p_2^g & (g-1)v_2
\end{array}
\]
Consequently,
\[
 A_g=\begin{pmatrix}g-1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B_g=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&g-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
These rows are Newton-face exponent rows for the displayed monomials.  They
are not free Jacobian data, and every use of them below is accompanied by a
strict selector inequality.

\begin{definition}[Two-phase cone]
For \(u=(u_1,u_2)^{\mathsf T}\in\R_{>0}^2\), write \(r(u)=u_2/u_1\) and define
\[
 \cC_g=\left\{u\in\R_{>0}^2:
 1\leq r(u)<\frac{g-2}{2}\right\}.
\]
The \(q\)-phase is the cone \(\cC_g\); the intermediate \(p\)-phase is the
image \(A_g\cC_g\), with its own selector inequalities.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Asymmetric coupled-shear degree matrix]\label{thm:main}
For every integer \(g\geq5\), \(F_g\) is a symplectic polynomial automorphism
of \(\A^4_K\).  If
\[
 C_g=B_gA_g=
 \begin{pmatrix}g+3&2\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix},
\]
then, for every \(n\geq1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-main}
 \deg(F_g^n)=e_2^{\mathsf T}C_g^n
 \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-main}
 \lambda_1(F_g)
 =\lim_{n\to\infty}\deg(F_g^n)^{1/n}
 =\rhoPF(C_g)
 =(\sqrt g+1)^2.
\end{equation}
Each elementary shear has degree \(g-1\), so
\[
 \lambda_1(F_g)<(g-1)^2=\deg(S)\deg(T).
\]
The mixed support makes the displayed coordinate split coupled.  No
non-conjugacy statement is asserted for other coordinate systems.
\end{theorem}

\section{Degree bookkeeping and support filtration}\label{sec:bookkeeping}

We use ordinary total degree, but it is helpful to separate the elementary
properties of degree that make the selector argument rigorous.  If
\(u=(u_1,u_2)\in\R_{\geq0}^2\) and
\(m=q_1^{a_1}q_2^{a_2}\), define its \(u\)-weight by
\[
 \wt_u(m)=a_1u_1+a_2u_2.
\]
For a polynomial in \(q_1,q_2\), its \(u\)-weighted degree is the maximum of
the weights of its monomials with nonzero coefficient.  Substitution of
coordinate polynomials of degrees \(u_1,u_2\) gives an upper bound by this
weighted degree.  If a monomial attaining the maximum has a nonzero
coefficient and no competing summand has the same degree with an opposite
coefficient, the upper bound is attained.

The only weighted-degree comparisons used in this paper are finite.  For the
first gradient row they are
\[
 \wt_u(q_1q_2^2)=u_1+2u_2,\qquad
 \wt_u(q_1^{g-1})=(g-1)u_1.
\]
For the second shear they are
\[
 \wt_v(p_1p_2^2)=v_1+2v_2,\quad
 \wt_v(p_1^2p_2)=2v_1+v_2,\quad
 \wt_v(p_2^{g-1})=(g-1)v_2.
\]
The strict inequalities in Sections~\ref{sec:selectors} and
\ref{sec:cone} say that the displayed maximum is unique at the level of
degree.  They are stronger than an assertion that a row is generically
selected: they hold for every degree vector in the invariant cone.

\begin{lemma}[Max-plus substitution bound]\label{lem:maxplus}
Let \(h=\sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha\) and let \(x_i\) be replaced by
polynomials of degrees \(d_i\).  Then
\[
 \deg h\leq\max_{\alpha:c_\alpha\neq0}\sum_i\alpha_i d_i.
\]
If one monomial on the right has a coefficient that remains nonzero and has
strictly larger degree than all other summands in the same addition, equality
holds.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The degree of a product is the sum of the degrees of its factors, and the
degree of a sum is at most the maximum of the summand degrees.  Applying these
facts to each monomial and then taking the maximum proves the bound.  A strict
maximum cannot be cancelled by a lower-degree summand; if its coefficient is
nonzero, it realizes the bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Complete support and phase ledger}

We now make the finite support calculation explicit, including the terms that
are carried through a triangular shear.  Differentiation removes one exponent
from a potential monomial; the remaining exponent vectors are the candidate
rows acting on a degree vector.  The potential supports themselves are
\[
 \operatorname{supp}(V)=\{(2,2),(g,0)\},\qquad
 \operatorname{supp}(W)=\{(2,2),(0,g)\}.
\]
After differentiation, the nonzero gradient supports and their coefficients
are
\begin{align}
 \partial_{q_1}V&=2q_1q_2^2+gq_1^{g-1},&
 \partial_{q_2}V&=2q_1^2q_2,\label{eq:V-supports}\\
 \partial_{p_1}W&=2p_1p_2^2,&
 \partial_{p_2}W&=2p_1^2p_2+gp_2^{g-1}.
 \label{eq:W-supports}
\end{align}
There are no omitted derivative rows: every term in the two potentials appears
in exactly the gradient coordinates predicted by its exponent.  This finite
fact is what allows us to use strict inequalities rather than a generic
Newton-fan assertion.

For a $q$-degree vector $u$ and an intermediate $p$-degree vector $v$, the
complete ledgers are as follows.  The \emph{carry} column records the coordinate
already present before the relevant shear; it must be compared separately with
the gradient candidates.
\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
\toprule
phase & output & source kind & coefficient & exponent row & weighted degree\\
\midrule
$S$ & $\widehat p_1$ & carry $p_1$ & $1$ & -- & $v_1$\\
$S$ & $\widehat p_1$ & mixed gradient & $2$ & $(1,2)$ & $u_1+2u_2$\\
$S$ & $\widehat p_1$ & pure gradient & $g$ & $(g-1,0)$ & $(g-1)u_1$\\
$S$ & $\widehat p_2$ & carry $p_2$ & $1$ & -- & $v_2$\\
$S$ & $\widehat p_2$ & mixed gradient & $2$ & $(2,1)$ & $2u_1+u_2$\\
\midrule
$T$ & $q'_1$ & carry $q_1$ & $1$ & -- & $u_1$\\
$T$ & $q'_1$ & mixed gradient & $2$ & $(1,2)$ & $v_1+2v_2$\\
$T$ & $q'_2$ & carry $q_2$ & $1$ & -- & $u_2$\\
$T$ & $q'_2$ & mixed gradient & $2$ & $(2,1)$ & $2v_1+v_2$\\
$T$ & $q'_2$ & pure gradient & $g$ & $(0,g-1)$ & $(g-1)v_2$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Complete support and phase ledger.}\label{tab:support-ledger}
\end{table}
The table is an occurrence ledger, not an additional map definition.  In the
$S$ rows $u$ measures the old $q$ coordinates and $v$ measures the carried
$p$ coordinates; in the $T$ rows the roles are reversed.  The selected entries
are the pure first-row term in $S$, the unique second-row term in $S$, the
mixed first-row term in $T$, and the pure second-row term in $T$.  This is
exactly the row selection encoded by $A_g$ and $B_g$.

For a formal support notation, let
\[
 \operatorname{supp}_q(P)=\{\alpha\in\mathbb N^2:[q^\alpha]P\ne0\},
 \qquad
 \deg_u(P)=\max_{\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}_q(P)}
 \langle\alpha,u\rangle.
\]
The analogous $\operatorname{supp}_p$ and $\deg_v$ are used in the $T$ phase.
If $P_i$ are substituted for $q_i$, a term indexed by $\alpha$ expands into
products of terms from the $P_i$, and every such product has degree at most
$\langle\alpha,u\rangle$.  Thus Lemma~\ref{lem:maxplus} is a finite support
statement: it takes the maximum of homogeneous linear forms, one for each
support exponent.  The carry rows in the table are not support exponents of a
potential; they arise from the identity summand in the triangular map and are
therefore tested by a separate old-term inequality.

The strict tests can be organized into a phase ledger.  There are two
different intermediate vectors in the $S$ comparison: the carried vector
from the preceding iterate and the newly produced vector.  Write
$\bar v$ for the carried $p$-degree vector and put $v^+=A_gu$ for the new
output of the $S$ phase.  (In the induction below,
$\bar v=v_n=A_gu_{n-1}$ and $v^+=v_{n+1}=A_gu_n$.)  The
selected-minus-competitor margins are
\begin{align}
 (g-1)u_1-(u_1+2u_2)&=u_1(g-2-2u_2/u_1),
 \label{eq:ledger-S-face}\\
 (g-1)u_1-\bar v_1&>0,\qquad
 (2u_1+u_2)-\bar v_2>0,\label{eq:ledger-S-carry}\\
 (v^+_1+2v^+_2)-u_1&=(g+2)u_1+2u_2,\label{eq:ledger-T-carry1}\\
 (g-1)v^+_2-(2v^+_1+v^+_2)&=(g-2)u_2-2u_1,\label{eq:ledger-T-face}\\
 (g-1)v^+_2-u_2&=2(g-1)u_1+(g-2)u_2.\label{eq:ledger-T-carry2}
\end{align}
The first and fourth expressions are face comparisons; the remaining three
compare a selected gradient contribution with an identity carry.  Keeping
these classes separate prevents a high-degree old coordinate from being
silently discarded.  The simultaneous induction in
Proposition~\ref{prop:oldterms} proves the carry inequalities at every step,
including the base step.  Notice that substituting $v^+=A_gu$ into the two
$S$ carry expressions would give zero identities, not strict inequalities;
those expressions must therefore use the genuinely old vector $\bar v$.

\begin{remark}[Why a face row is not a Jacobian row]
The matrices \(A_g\) and \(B_g\) encode weighted degrees of four specific
support monomials after differentiating the potentials.  A Jacobian matrix
records first derivatives of the full map and has a different meaning.  In
particular, replacing \(A_g\) or \(B_g\) by a Jacobian or Hessian matrix would
discard the carried-coordinate comparisons and would not prove
\eqref{eq:phase}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The phase filtration}

The complete step has the filtration
\[
 \underbrace{u_n}_{q\text{-phase}}
 \xrightarrow{\ S\ }
 \underbrace{v_{n+1}}_{p\text{-phase}}
 \xrightarrow{\ T\ }
 \underbrace{u_{n+1}}_{q\text{-phase}}.
\]
The first arrow is not an iteration of \(F_g\), and the second arrow cannot be
applied to \(u_n\) directly.  This distinction is relevant even if one only
wants the maximal degree: the inequalities for the \(S\)-phase involve the
ratio \(u_2/u_1\), whereas the second \(T\)-row involves
\(v_2/v_1=(2+u_2/u_1)/(g-1)\).  The latter ratio is not the former ratio.

The initial values are deliberately assigned to both phase ledgers:
\(u_0=v_0=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\).  The value \(v_0\) represents the original
degree-one \(p\)-coordinates only for the base comparison.  For every genuine
step \(n\to n+1\), \(v_{n+1}\) is defined by the \(S\)-phase and is then
consumed by \(T\).  This convention lets the base case and the induction use
the same displayed inequalities without silently identifying the phases.

\subsection{A useful matrix comparison}

The complete matrix is positive, but the phase matrices are only
nonnegative:
\[
 A_g=\begin{pmatrix}g-1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B_g=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&g-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The off-diagonal stacked matrix
\[
 M_g=\begin{pmatrix}0&B_g\\A_g&0\end{pmatrix}
\]
satisfies
\[
 M_g^2=
 \begin{pmatrix}B_gA_g&0\\0&A_gB_g\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus \(\rhoPF(M_g)^2=\rhoPF(B_gA_g)\).  A statement about one application of
\(M_g\) would therefore describe a half-step; the degree per complete iterate
is controlled by \(\rhoPF(C_g)\), not by \(\rhoPF(M_g)\) without the square.
This elementary observation prevents a common phase-counting error.


\section{Canonical shears and explicit inverses}\label{sec:canonical}

The canonicality calculation fixes the algebraic object before the degree
argument begins.  We first record the inverse maps in coordinates, and then
verify the symplectic identity both by pullback and by the block Jacobian.

\begin{lemma}[Triangular inverses]\label{lem:inverse}
The maps \(S\) and \(T\) are polynomial automorphisms with
\[
 S^{-1}(q,p)=(q,p-\grad V(q)),\qquad
 T^{-1}(q,p)=(q-\grad W(p),p).
\]
Consequently \(F_g^{-1}=S^{-1}\circ T^{-1}\) is polynomial.  More explicitly,
if
\[
 q_1^-:=q_1-2p_1p_2^2,\qquad
 q_2^-:=q_2-2p_1^2p_2-gp_2^{g-1},
\]
then
\begin{align}
 F_g^{-1}(q,p)
 =\bigl(&q_1^-,q_2^-,
 p_1-2q_1^-(q_2^-)^2-g(q_1^-)^{g-1},\nonumber\\
 &p_2-2(q_1^-)^2q_2^-\bigr).
 \label{eq:explicit-inverse}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The map \(S\) leaves \(q\) fixed, so subtracting
\(\grad V(q)\) recovers \(p\).  The map \(T\) leaves \(p\) fixed, so
subtracting \(\grad W(p)\) recovers \(q\).  This proves the two displayed
triangular inverses by direct composition in both orders.

For the complete map, \(T^{-1}\) first produces
\[
 (q_1^-,q_2^-,p_1,p_2)
 =
 \bigl(q-\grad W(p),p\bigr).
\]
Applying \(S^{-1}\) then subtracts \(\grad V(q^-)\) from \(p\), which gives
\eqref{eq:explicit-inverse}.  Every entry is polynomial, so this is an
explicit polynomial inverse rather than an appeal to a Jacobian criterion.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Symplecticity and block Jacobian]\label{lem:symplectic}
Both \(S\) and \(T\), hence \(F_g\), preserve
\(\omega=dq_1\wedge dp_1+dq_2\wedge dp_2\).  In the coordinate order
\((q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)\), their Jacobians and the Jacobian of \(F_g\) have the
block forms
\begin{align}
 DS(q,p)
 &=\begin{pmatrix}I_2&0\\ Q&I_2\end{pmatrix},
&
 DT(q,\widehat p)
 &=\begin{pmatrix}I_2&P\\0&I_2\end{pmatrix},
 \label{eq:shear-block-jacobians}\\
 DF_g(q,p)
 &=\begin{pmatrix}I_2+PQ&P\\Q&I_2\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 Q=H_V(q),\quad P=H_W(\widehat p).
 \label{eq:complete-block-jacobian}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write
\[
 H_V(q)=D^2V(q)
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 2q_2^2+g(g-1)q_1^{g-2}&4q_1q_2\\
 4q_1q_2&2q_1^2
 \end{pmatrix}
\]
and
\[
 H_W(p)=D^2W(p)
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 2p_2^2&4p_1p_2\\
 4p_1p_2&2p_1^2+g(g-1)p_2^{g-2}
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both matrices are symmetric.  For the first shear,
\begin{align*}
 S^*\omega
 &=\sum_{i=1}^2dq_i\wedge
 d\bigl(p_i+\partial_{q_i}V(q)\bigr)\\
 &=\omega+\sum_{i,j=1}^2
 \partial_{q_iq_j}V(q)\,dq_i\wedge dq_j
 =\omega.
\end{align*}
The last equality follows by pairing the \((i,j)\) and \((j,i)\) terms:
their coefficient is
\(\partial_{q_iq_j}V-\partial_{q_jq_i}V=0\).

For the second shear,
\begin{align*}
 T^*\omega
 &=\sum_{i=1}^2d\bigl(q_i+\partial_{p_i}W(p)\bigr)\wedge dp_i\\
 &=\omega+\sum_{i,j=1}^2
 \partial_{p_ip_j}W(p)\,dp_j\wedge dp_i
 =\omega,
\end{align*}
again by Hessian symmetry.  Therefore
\[
 F_g^*\omega=S^*(T^*\omega)=S^*\omega=\omega.
\]

The same calculation in block notation makes the phase order explicit.  Set
\[
 J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_2\\-I_2&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Direct multiplication gives
\[
 DS^{\mathsf T}JDS
 =\begin{pmatrix}Q-Q^{\mathsf T}&I_2\\-I_2&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 DT^{\mathsf T}JDT
 =\begin{pmatrix}0&I_2\\-I_2&P^{\mathsf T}-P\end{pmatrix}.
\]
These matrices equal \(J\) because \(Q\) and \(P\) are symmetric.  Since
\(DF_g=DT(q,\widehat p)\,DS(q,p)\), multiplication of the two triangular
blocks gives \eqref{eq:complete-block-jacobian}.  If
\[
 M:=DF_g=\begin{pmatrix}I_2+PQ&P\\Q&I_2\end{pmatrix},
\]
then a direct composite check is
\[
 M^{\mathsf T}JM
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}
 (I_2+QP)Q-Q(I_2+PQ)&(I_2+QP)-QP\\
 PQ-(I_2+PQ)&P-P
 \end{pmatrix}
 =J.
\]
The block triangular factors also give
\(\det DS=\det DT=1\), hence \(\det DF_g=1\).  This determinant identity is
only a consistency check; polynomial invertibility was established by the
explicit inverse above.  The block matrices certify canonicality, whereas
the support monomials and strict degree gaps below certify degree growth.

For completeness, the inverse Jacobians have the same triangular shape:
\[
 DS^{-1}(q,p)=
 \begin{pmatrix}I_2&0\\-Q&I_2\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 DT^{-1}(q,p)=
 \begin{pmatrix}I_2&-P\\0&I_2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Here \(Q\) and \(P\) are evaluated at the source point of the corresponding
inverse shear.  Multiplying \(DS^{-1}DT^{-1}\) in the reverse phase order
recovers \(DF_g^{-1}\), and multiplying either pair with the forward blocks
gives the identity.  Thus the inverse in Lemma~\ref{lem:inverse} is reflected
both in coordinates and in the Jacobian factorization; no constant-Jacobian
criterion is being used as a substitute for the explicit polynomial inverse.

\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Characteristic zero is not needed for the formal Hessian-symmetry identity.
It is used later when the positive integer coefficients \(2\) and \(g\) are
interpreted as nonzero coefficients in \(K\).  No selector or degree statement
for other potentials is asserted here.
\end{remark}

\section{The two Newton-face selectors}\label{sec:selectors}

We now prove that the rows \(A_g\) and \(B_g\) are forced by the displayed
supports on the relevant cone.  The argument is degree-theoretic: a selected
monomial supplies a lower bound, and the strict inequalities bound every
competing summand from above.

\subsection{The \(S\)-phase}

\begin{lemma}[First-shear selector]\label{lem:Sselector}
If $u\in\cC_g$, then the two gradient rows in the $S$ phase have degree
vector $A_gu$.  If a carried degree vector $w$ satisfies
$w<A_gu$ componentwise, then the corresponding $S$ output also has degree
vector $A_gu$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first gradient coordinate is
\[
 \partial_{q_1}V=2q_1q_2^2+gq_1^{g-1}.
\]
Its two candidate degrees are $u_1+2u_2$ and $(g-1)u_1$.  Since
$u_2/u_1<(g-2)/2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sface}
 u_1+2u_2<(g-1)u_1.
\end{equation}
The pure term therefore gives the first entry of $A_gu$.  The second gradient
coordinate is
\[
 \partial_{q_2}V=2q_1^2q_2,
\]
whose degree is $2u_1+u_2$, giving the second entry.  If
$w<A_gu$, adding the carried coordinates cannot change either maximum.
The selected summands are nonzero because their scalar coefficients are $2$
or $g$ and $K[q,p]$ is a domain of characteristic zero.  Thus the strict
support gaps give exact degrees, not only upper bounds.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Phase-labelled interpretation of the first gap.}
The inequality \eqref{eq:Sface} is equivalent to
\[
 (g-1)u_1-(u_1+2u_2)
 =2\left(\frac{g-2}{2}-\frac{u_2}{u_1}\right)u_1>0.
\tag{S-gap}\label{eq:S-gap-margin}
\]
Thus the margin is pointwise in the ratio, rather than merely asymptotic in
$g$.  At $n=0$, the carried $p$-coordinates have degree one and the two
selected gradient degrees are $g-1$ and $3$.  For $n\geq1$, the carried vector
is exactly $v_n=A_gu_{n-1}$; it is not an independent vector that needs a new
cone assumption.  Once the complete-step lemma gives
$u_n>u_{n-1}$, multiplication by the nonnegative matrix $A_g$ gives
\[
 v_n=A_gu_{n-1}<A_gu_n.
\tag{S-carried}\label{eq:S-carried}
\]
The two coordinates of \eqref{eq:S-carried} are precisely the two carried
terms in the $S$ phase.  This is why the induction is ordered as
\[
 u_n\in\cC_g
\ \Longrightarrow\
 \text{$S$ selector and }v_{n+1}=A_gu_n
\ \Longrightarrow\
 \text{$T$ selector and }u_{n+1}=C_gu_n
\ \Longrightarrow\
 u_{n+1}>u_n.
\]
No conclusion about $v_{n+1}$ is used before the $S$ selector has been
established.

\subsection{The \(T\)-phase}

\begin{lemma}[Second-shear selector]\label{lem:Tselector}
If \(u\in\cC_g\) and \(v=A_gu\), then the gradient contribution in the
\(T\)-phase has degree vector \(B_gv\), and it strictly dominates the carried
\(q\)-coordinates.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first \(p\)-gradient coordinate is
\[
 \partial_{p_1}W=2p_1p_2^2,
\]
so its degree is \(v_1+2v_2\).  It dominates the carried \(q_1\)-degree because
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tfirstgap}
 v_1+2v_2-u_1
 =(g-1)u_1+2(2u_1+u_2)-u_1
 =(g+2)u_1+2u_2>0.
\end{equation}
For the second row,
\[
 \partial_{p_2}W=2p_1^2p_2+gp_2^{g-1}.
\]
The cross term has degree \(2v_1+v_2\), while the pure term has degree
\((g-1)v_2\).  If \(r=u_2/u_1\), then
\[
 v_1=(g-1)u_1,\qquad v_2=(2+r)u_1,
\]
and therefore
\[
 \frac{v_2}{v_1}=\frac{2+r}{g-1}
 \geq\frac{3}{g-1}>\frac{2}{g-2},
\]
where the final inequality is equivalent to \(g>4\).  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tface}
 2v_1+v_2<(g-1)v_2.
\end{equation}
The pure term selects the second row.  It also dominates the carried
\(q_2\)-degree because
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tsecondgap}
 (g-1)v_2-u_2
 =2(g-1)u_1+(g-2)u_2>0.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The intermediate phase has a different ratio.}
Writing $r=u_2/u_1$ gives
\[
 v_1=(g-1)u_1,\qquad v_2=(2+r)u_1,\qquad
 s:=\frac{v_2}{v_1}=\frac{2+r}{g-1}.
\]
For $1\leq r<R=(g-2)/2$,
\[
 \frac{3}{g-1}\leq s<\frac{g+2}{2(g-1)}.
\]
The lower endpoint leaves the explicit margin
\[
 \frac{3}{g-1}-\frac{2}{g-2}
 =\frac{g-4}{(g-1)(g-2)}>0.
\tag{T-face-margin}\label{eq:T-face-margin}
\]
Consequently the mixed monomial in the second $T$ row is strictly below the
pure monomial for every $r$ in the half-open cone.  The carried-coordinate
gaps can be collected in the following phase ledger:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{output}&\text{selected degree}&\text{carried degree}
 &\text{strict gap}\\
\hline
T_1&q_1\text{-phase }v_1+2v_2&u_1&(g+2)u_1+2u_2\\
T_2&\text{pure }(g-1)v_2&u_2&2(g-1)u_1+(g-2)u_2\\
T_2&\text{pure }(g-1)v_2&\text{mixed }2v_1+v_2
 &(g-1)v_2-(2v_1+v_2)
\end{array}
\tag{Phase gaps}\label{tab:phase-gaps}
\]
The last entry is positive by \eqref{eq:Tface}; the first two are positive
because $u_1,u_2>0$.  Table~\ref{tab:phase-gaps} is a list of three
different comparisons, not three descriptions of one maximum.

\subsection{The complete matrix and the half-step warning}

Combining the selected rows gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-product}
 B_gA_g
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&g-1\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}g-1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}g+3&2\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}
 =C_g.
\end{equation}
The phase recurrence is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase}
 v_{n+1}=A_gu_n,\qquad
 u_{n+1}=B_gv_{n+1}=C_gu_n.
\end{equation}
One can instead stack the two phase vectors and write a formal off-diagonal
matrix, but that matrix advances only one shear at a time.  Its nonzero
spectrum must be interpreted with a two-phase period correction.  Equation
\eqref{eq:matrix-product} is the complete-step theorem matrix.

\section{Two-stage cone invariance}\label{sec:cone}

The selectors above are conditional on a cone.  We now show that the complete
matrix returns the \(q\)-degree vector to that same cone for every iterate.

\begin{lemma}[Ratio map]\label{lem:cone}
Let
\[
 C_g=\begin{pmatrix}g+3&2\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
If \(u\in\cC_g\), then \(C_gu\in\cC_g\) and \(C_gu>u\) componentwise.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write \(r=u_2/u_1\) and \(R=(g-2)/2\).  The new ratio is
\[
 f_g(r)=\frac{(C_gu)_2}{(C_gu)_1}
 =\frac{(g-1)(2+r)}{g+3+2r}.
\]
Its derivative is
\[
 f'_g(r)=\frac{(g-1)^2}{(g+3+2r)^2}>0.
\]
At the lower endpoint,
\[
 f_g(1)=\frac{3(g-1)}{g+5}>1
\]
for \(g>4\).  At the upper endpoint,
\[
 f_g(R)=
 \frac{(g-1)(g+2)}{2(2g+1)}.
\]
After multiplying by the positive denominator, the inequality \(f_g(R)<R\)
is equivalent to
\[
 (g-1)(g+2)<(g-2)(2g+1),
\]
whose right-minus-left side is \(g(g-4)>0\).  Monotonicity therefore gives
\(1\leq f_g(r)<R\) whenever \(1\leq r<R\).  Finally,
\[
 (C_gu)_1-u_1=(g+2)u_1+2u_2>0,
\]
\[
 (C_gu)_2-u_2=2(g-1)u_1+(g-2)u_2>0.
\]
This proves cone invariance and strict componentwise growth.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Endpoint margins and the half-open boundary.}
The endpoint computations can be written with their margins exposed:
\[
 f_g(1)-1=\frac{2(g-4)}{g+5},
 \qquad
 R-f_g(R)=\frac{g(g-4)}{2(2g+1)}.
\tag{Cone margins}\label{eq:cone-margins}
\]
Both quantities are strictly positive exactly in the range needed here,
$g\geq5$.  The upper endpoint is excluded from $\cC_g$ because the
$S$-phase gap vanishes there:
\[
 (g-1)u_1-(u_1+2Ru_1)=0.
\]
The lower endpoint is included because $f_g(1)>1$, so the next ratio moves
into the interior.  Thus the induction never needs to decide which face wins
at $r=R$, and it never loses the lower bound $r\geq1$ after one step.

The cone also gives a useful two-phase picture.  The map $r\mapsto f_g(r)$
acts only after both shears have been applied.  During the intermediate
$S$-phase the ratio is instead
\[
 r\longmapsto s_g(r)=\frac{2+r}{g-1},
\]
and the $T$ selector uses the inequality
$s_g(r)>2/(g-2)$ from \eqref{eq:T-face-margin}.  The two maps have different
fixed points and different boundary tests; treating them as one ratio map
would conflate a half-step with a complete iterate.

\begin{remark}[Why the cone has two phases]\label{rem:twophase}
It is tempting to write one strict cone in which both
\(A_gu>v\) and \(B_gv>u\) hold.  That is not the phase structure of the map.
The intermediate vector is exactly \(v_{n+1}=A_gu_n\); it is not a variable
that should be compared strictly with itself.  The correct induction has a
\(q\)-phase cone, followed by the \(S\)-selector, followed by the \(T\)-selector,
and then a complete-step return through \(C_g\).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Old-term dominance and the simultaneous phase induction}

There is one carried vector that must be identified explicitly.  Since the map
$T$ fixes the $p$-coordinates, the final $p$-degree vector of $F_g^n$ is
$v_n$ for every $n\geq1$; at $n=0$ this remains true by the convention
$v_0=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$.  Thus $v_n$ is the old input to the next $S$ phase,
whereas $v_{n+1}$ is the new output of that phase.

A simple coefficient fact will be used inside the induction.  The coordinate
polynomials of $F_g$ have nonnegative integer coefficients, and composition
preserves the semiring
$\mathbb Z_{\geq0}[q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2]$.  Hence a strictly highest-degree
summand cannot be cancelled.  Lemma~\ref{lem:nocancel} records the complete
coefficient-path version after the degree induction.  Here we use only this
semiring closure to pass from a strict support maximum to an exact degree.

\begin{proposition}[Simultaneous carried-term induction]\label{prop:oldterms}
For every $n\geq0$, $u_n\in\cC_g$, the two phase equations
\[
 v_{n+1}=A_gu_n,\qquad u_{n+1}=B_gv_{n+1}=C_gu_n
\]
hold, and $u_{n+1}>u_n$ componentwise.  At the same step all carried and
competing terms satisfy
\begin{align}
 \max\{v_{1,n},u_{1,n}+2u_{2,n}\}
   &<(g-1)u_{1,n},&
 v_{2,n}&<2u_{1,n}+u_{2,n},\label{eq:oldS}\\
 u_{1,n}&<v_{1,n+1}+2v_{2,n+1},&
 \max\{u_{2,n},2v_{1,n+1}+v_{2,n+1}\}
   &<(g-1)v_{2,n+1}.\label{eq:oldT}
\end{align}
Consequently, for $n\geq1$, $v_n=A_gu_{n-1}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We induct on the complete-step index.  At $n=0$ both carried vectors are
$(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$.  The three gaps in the $S$ phase are
\[
 (g-1)-(1+2)=g-4>0,\qquad
 (g-1)-1=g-2>0,\qquad
 (2+1)-1=2>0.
\]
They compare, respectively, the pure first-row term with the mixed first-row
term, the pure first-row term with carried $p_1$, and the second-row mixed
term with carried $p_2$.  Hence
\[
 v_1=A_gu_0=\begin{pmatrix}g-1\\3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For the $T$ phase the first selected term exceeds carried $q_1$ by
\[
 v_{1,1}+2v_{2,1}-u_{1,0}=g+4>0.
\]
In the second row, the pure term exceeds the mixed term and carried $q_2$ by
\[
 (g-1)v_{2,1}-(2v_{1,1}+v_{2,1})=g-4>0,
 \qquad (g-1)v_{2,1}-u_{2,0}=3g-4>0.
\]
Thus $u_1=B_gv_1=C_gu_0$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:cone} puts $u_1$ in $\cC_g$
and gives $u_1>u_0$.

Now let $n\geq1$ and suppose the assertions have been established through
step $n-1$.  Put
\[
 \Delta_n=u_n-u_{n-1}\in\mathbb R_{>0}^2,
 \qquad r_n=\frac{u_{2,n}}{u_{1,n}}.
\]
The old $p$ vector is $v_n=A_gu_{n-1}$.  The first $S$-face gap is
\[
 (g-1)u_{1,n}-(u_{1,n}+2u_{2,n})
 =u_{1,n}(g-2-2r_n)>0,
\]
because $u_n\in\cC_g$.  The two carried-coordinate gaps are the entries of
\[
 A_gu_n-v_n=A_g\Delta_n
 =\begin{pmatrix}(g-1)\Delta_{1,n}\\
                  2\Delta_{1,n}+\Delta_{2,n}
   \end{pmatrix}>0.
\]
This proves every inequality in \eqref{eq:oldS} and gives
$v_{n+1}=A_gu_n$.

Write $u=u_n$ and $v=v_{n+1}=A_gu$, so
$v_1=(g-1)u_1$ and $v_2=2u_1+u_2$.  The carried gap in the first $T$ row is
\[
 v_1+2v_2-u_1=(g+2)u_1+2u_2>0.
\]
The pure-versus-mixed gap in the second row can be cleared without a ratio
denominator:
\begin{align*}
 (g-1)v_2-(2v_1+v_2)
 &= (g-2)v_2-2v_1\\
 &= (g-2)u_2-2u_1\\
 &=u_1\bigl((g-2)r_n-2\bigr)
 \geq(g-4)u_1>0.
\end{align*}
Finally, the pure second-row term exceeds carried $q_2$ by
\[
 (g-1)v_2-u_2=2(g-1)u_1+(g-2)u_2>0.
\]
These are all inequalities in \eqref{eq:oldT}; hence
$u_{n+1}=B_gv_{n+1}=C_gu_n$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:cone} returns $u_{n+1}$ to
$\cC_g$ and gives $u_{n+1}>u_n$, closing the induction.
At each of the strict gaps, the selected summand is a nonzero scalar
(\(2\) or \(g\)) times a product of nonzero coordinate polynomials in the
domain \(K[q,p]\).  The strict-gap conclusion therefore supplies an exact
degree before the coefficient-path lemma is invoked; the latter will certify
that this nonzero leading piece survives every subsequent composition.
\end{proof}

\section{No cancellation, exact recurrence, and visibility}\label{sec:visibility}

\subsection{Coefficientwise no-cancellation}

\begin{lemma}[Positive coefficients prevent cancellation]\label{lem:nocancel}
For every \(n\), every coordinate polynomial of \(F_g^n\) has coefficients in
\(\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\).  Every monomial selected in
\eqref{eq:phase} has a positive coefficient, and its degree cannot be removed
by cancellation.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Perform the iteration first over the polynomial semiring
\[
 \mathbb Z_{\geq0}[q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2]
\]
and then apply the coefficient map $\mathbb Z\to K$.  At time zero each
coordinate is a single variable with coefficient one.  If all four coordinates
at time $n$ lie in this semiring, then the formulas
\eqref{eq:phat}--\eqref{eq:pout} form the next four coordinates using only
addition, multiplication, and multiplication by $2$ or $g$.  These operations
preserve the semiring, so induction proves coefficientwise nonnegativity for
all iterates.

For completeness, consider the selected top-degree pieces.  If $P^{[d]}$
denotes the degree-$d$ homogeneous part of a polynomial $P$, then the four
selected contributions have top parts of the form
\[
 g\bigl(q_{1,n}^{[u_{1,n}]}\bigr)^{g-1},\qquad
 2\bigl(q_{1,n}^{[u_{1,n}]}\bigr)^2q_{2,n}^{[u_{2,n}]},
\]
\[
 2\widehat p_{1,n+1}^{[v_{1,n+1}]}
   \bigl(\widehat p_{2,n+1}^{[v_{2,n+1}]}\bigr)^2,
 \qquad
 g\bigl(\widehat p_{2,n+1}^{[v_{2,n+1}]}\bigr)^{g-1}.
\]
Every factor displayed here is nonzero.  Products of nonzero polynomials are
nonzero, and all their coefficients are nonnegative integers; multiplication
by $2$ or $g$ produces at least one positive integer coefficient.
Proposition~\ref{prop:oldterms} places every other top-level summand at a
strictly smaller total degree, so none can alter the displayed homogeneous
part.

Equivalently, fully expanding an iterate expresses each monomial coefficient
as a finite sum over expression-tree paths.  A path weight is a product of
copies of $2$, $g$, and positive multinomial coefficients.  A selected term
has at least one such positive-weight path.  If two paths end at the same
monomial, their weights add; path uniqueness is neither asserted nor needed.
Because $\operatorname{char}K=0$, the homomorphism $\mathbb Z\to K$ is
injective, so no positive integer path sum becomes zero after base change.
Thus every selected leading term survives and no cancellation changes any of
the degrees in \eqref{eq:phase}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Leading-form propagation]\label{prop:leadingforms}
The selector inequalities determine not only the degrees but also the top
homogeneous forms carried by the two phases.  For $n\geq0$, write
\[
 Q_{i,n}:=\bigl(q_{i,n}\bigr)^{[u_{i,n}]},\qquad
 R_{i,n+1}:=\bigl(\widehat p_{i,n+1}\bigr)^{[v_{i,n+1}]},
\]
where
\(
 \widehat p_{i,n+1}=p_{i,n}+\partial_{q_i}V(q_n)
\)
and $P^{[d]}$ denotes the homogeneous component of total degree $d$.
Then
\begin{align}
 R_{1,n+1}&=gQ_{1,n}^{\,g-1},
 &R_{2,n+1}&=2Q_{1,n}^{\,2}Q_{2,n},\label{eq:leading-S-forms}\\
 Q_{1,n+1}&=2R_{1,n+1}R_{2,n+1}^{\,2},
 &Q_{2,n+1}&=gR_{2,n+1}^{\,g-1}.
 \label{eq:leading-T-forms}
\end{align}
Equivalently, the complete-step top forms obey
\begin{align}
 Q_{1,n+1}&=8g\,Q_{1,n}^{\,g+3}Q_{2,n}^{\,2},\label{eq:leading-Q1}\\
 Q_{2,n+1}&=g2^{g-1}Q_{1,n}^{\,2(g-1)}Q_{2,n}^{\,g-1}.
 \label{eq:leading-Q2}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:oldterms}, the pure $q_1^{g-1}$ term is the
unique top-degree contribution to the first $S$ output, while the mixed
$q_1^2q_2$ term is the unique top-degree contribution to the second output.
Taking homogeneous parts of the two identities in
\eqref{eq:phat} therefore gives \eqref{eq:leading-S-forms}.  The same
proposition says that the mixed term in the first $T$ output and the pure
$p_2^{g-1}$ term in the second output are respectively the unique top-degree
contributions in \eqref{eq:qout}; taking homogeneous parts gives
\eqref{eq:leading-T-forms}.  Substitution of the first line into the second
line yields \eqref{eq:leading-Q1}--\eqref{eq:leading-Q2}.  At the initial
step this reads
\[
 Q_{1,0}=q_1,\quad Q_{2,0}=q_2,\quad
 R_{1,1}=gq_1^{g-1},\quad R_{2,1}=2q_1^2q_2,
\]
so the formulas start from nonzero forms.  Every subsequent form is a
positive scalar times a product of nonzero forms in the domain $K[q,p]$;
this gives a concrete coefficient-path witness at every iterate.
\end{proof}

For a finite exponent set $E\subset\mathbb N^4$ (with coordinates ordered as
$(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)$), let
\(aE+bE' =\{ae+be':e\in E,\ e'\in E'\}\) denote its Minkowski sum.
The coefficientwise positivity in Lemma~\ref{lem:nocancel} makes the support
version of the leading-form recurrence exact.  If
\(E_{i,n}=\operatorname{supp}(Q_{i,n})\), then
\begin{align}
 E_{1,n+1}&=(g+3)E_{1,n}+2E_{2,n},\label{eq:leading-support-q1}\\
 E_{2,n+1}&=2(g-1)E_{1,n}+(g-1)E_{2,n}.
 \label{eq:leading-support-q2}
\end{align}
Indeed, the support of a product of positive-coefficient polynomials is the
Minkowski sum of their supports; coincident exponent sums only add positive
coefficients.  Thus \eqref{eq:leading-support-q1}--\eqref{eq:leading-support-q2}
are a monomial-level refinement of the degree rows of $C_g$, not a new
genericity assumption.  They also show directly that the selected degree
paths remain present under every composition.

\subsection{The exact complete-step recurrence}

\begin{proposition}[Two-phase recurrence]\label{prop:recurrence}
For every \(n\geq0\),
\[
 v_{n+1}=A_gu_n,\qquad u_{n+1}=C_gu_n.
\]
Consequently \(u_n=C_g^n(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:Sselector} identifies the exact \(S\)-phase vector.  Lemma
\ref{lem:Tselector} identifies the exact \(T\)-phase vector once
\(v_{n+1}=A_gu_n\) is known.  Lemma~\ref{lem:cone} keeps \(u_n\) in the
selector cone, while Proposition~\ref{prop:oldterms} and
Lemma~\ref{lem:nocancel} ensure that no carried term or cancellation changes a
selected degree.  Equation \eqref{eq:phase} follows, and induction from
\(u_0=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\) gives the closed recurrence.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Visibility of the total degree]\label{lem:visibility}
For every \(n\geq1\), the second \(q\)-coordinate has maximal degree among all
four coordinates of \(F_g^n\).  In particular,
\[
 \deg(F_g^n)=e_2^{\mathsf T}u_n.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Cone invariance gives \(u_{n,2}>u_{n,1}\) for \(n\geq1\).  The final
\(p\)-degrees are \(v_n=A_gu_{n-1}\).  Direct subtraction gives
\[
 C_g-A_g=
 \begin{pmatrix}
  4&2\\
  2g-4&g-2
 \end{pmatrix},
\]
whose entries are positive for \(g\geq5\).  Therefore
\(u_n=C_gu_{n-1}>A_gu_{n-1}=v_n\) componentwise.  Both final \(p\)-coordinates
are below the corresponding \(q\)-coordinates, and the second \(q\)-degree is
larger than the first.  The claim follows.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Visibility as a degree functional.}
The comparison with $A_g$ is stronger than the statement that $C_g$ has
larger row sums.  For every positive vector $x$,
\[
 (C_g-A_g)x
 =\begin{pmatrix}4x_1+2x_2\\(2g-4)x_1+(g-2)x_2\end{pmatrix}>0.
\tag{C-minus-A}\label{eq:C-minus-A}
\]
Thus the two final $p$ degrees are strictly below the corresponding $q$
degrees at every genuine step.  Cone membership gives
$u_{n,2}\geq u_{n,1}$, with strict inequality for $n\geq1$, so the maximum
over the four coordinate degrees is exactly the second entry of $u_n$.
Equivalently, the total-degree functional on the full four-coordinate ledger
restricts to the linear functional $e_2^{\mathsf T}$ on the $q$-phase.

This restriction is important for the asymptotic claim.  A positive matrix can
have a large spectral radius that is invisible to a chosen coordinate if the
initial vector lies in a complementary invariant subspace or if the output
functional annihilates the Perron eigenvector.  Equation \eqref{eq:C-minus-A}
rules out the second failure for the actual degree ledger, while positivity of
$C_g$ rules out the first.

\section{Perron calculation and dynamical degree}\label{sec:perron}

The recurrence is now exact, so the asymptotic calculation is a finite
matrix computation with an explicitly visible coordinate.

\begin{lemma}[Perron root]\label{lem:perron}
The eigenvalues of \(C_g\) are
\[
 \lambda_\pm=g+1\pm2\sqrt g=(\sqrt g\pm1)^2.
\]
The larger root is the Perron root.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The trace and determinant are
\[
 \tr(C_g)=2g+2,\qquad
 \det(C_g)=(g+3)(g-1)-4(g-1)=(g-1)^2.
\]
Thus the characteristic discriminant is
\[
 (2g+2)^2-4(g-1)^2=16g,
\]
which gives the displayed roots.  All four entries of \(C_g\) are positive,
so the larger eigenvalue is simple and has a positive eigenvector by the
Perron--Frobenius theorem.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Perron accessibility and the closed recurrence.}
The right Perron eigenvector can be chosen explicitly.  Writing
$s=\sqrt g$, one checks
\[
 C_g\begin{pmatrix}1\\s-1\end{pmatrix}
 =(s+1)^2\begin{pmatrix}1\\s-1\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{Perron vector}\label{eq:perron-vector}
\]
Both entries are positive for $g\geq5$.  The corresponding left eigenvector can
also be written without an existence argument:
\[
 \ell_+=(\sqrt g+1,1)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 \ell_+^{\mathsf T}C_g=\lambda_+\ell_+^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Indeed,
\[
 \ell_+^{\mathsf T}(1,1)^{\mathsf T}=\sqrt g+2>0,
 \qquad
 e_2^{\mathsf T}(1,\sqrt g-1)^{\mathsf T}=\sqrt g-1>0.
\]
These are the accessibility and visibility pairings needed to turn the matrix
radius into the degree limit.  In particular, no coordinate projection in the
proof is orthogonal to the Perron class.

The same point can be verified without invoking an abstract decomposition.
Cayley--Hamilton gives
\[
 C_g^{n+2}=2(g+1)C_g^{n+1}-(g-1)^2C_g^n.
\]
Applying $e_2^{\mathsf T}$ on the left and $(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ on the right
produces the scalar recurrence in Proposition~\ref{prop:scalar}.  Its leading
coefficient is
\[
 a_+=\frac{3(g-1)-\lambda_-}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}
 =\frac{(\sqrt g-1)(\sqrt g+2)}{2\sqrt g}>0.
\tag{Perron coefficient}\label{eq:perron-coefficient}
\]
Thus the $\lambda_+^n$ term is present in the observed coordinate, rather than
being removed by a special initial condition.  This is an exact algebraic
check, not a numerical estimate of the growth rate.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Lemmas~\ref{lem:inverse} and \ref{lem:symplectic} give polynomial
invertibility and preservation of \(\omega\).  Lemmas
\ref{lem:Sselector}--\ref{lem:nocancel} give the exact complete-step recurrence,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:recurrence} gives
\(u_n=C_g^n(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\).  Lemma~\ref{lem:visibility} identifies the
total degree with the second component, proving \eqref{eq:degree-main}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:perron} supplies the positive root.  Because both the initial
vector and \(e_2\) have positive pairing with the Perron class, the
subdominant eigenvalue is exponentially negligible and
\[
 \lim_{n\to\infty}
 \left(e_2^{\mathsf T}C_g^n(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\right)^{1/n}
 =\rhoPF(C_g).
\]
Finally, the pure powers \(q_1^{g-1}\) in \(\grad V\) and \(p_2^{g-1}\) in
\(\grad W\) show \(\deg(S)=\deg(T)=g-1\).  The strict comparison is
\[
 (g-1)^2-(\sqrt g+1)^2
 =\sqrt g\,(\sqrt g-2)(\sqrt g+1)^2>0
\]
for \(g\geq5\).  The mixed monomials join the two coordinate labels in the
displayed support, which is the stated narrow coupling claim.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A scalar recurrence and an exact closed form}

The matrix formula also gives a useful scalar check on the degree sequence.  Set
\[
 d_n=e_2^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
 =\deg(F_g^n)\qquad(n\geq1),
\]
and extend the notation by \(d_0=1\).

\begin{proposition}[Scalar degree recurrence]\label{prop:scalar}
The sequence \((d_n)\) satisfies
\[
 d_{n+2}=2(g+1)d_{n+1}-(g-1)^2d_n,
 \qquad d_0=1,\quad d_1=3(g-1).
\]
Equivalently, with
\(\lambda_\pm=(\sqrt g\pm1)^2\),
\[
 d_n=
 \frac{3(g-1)-\lambda_-}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}\lambda_+^n
 +\frac{\lambda_+-3(g-1)}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}\lambda_-^n.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Cayley--Hamilton identity for \(C_g\) is
\[
 C_g^2-2(g+1)C_g+(g-1)^2I=0.
\]
Multiplying on the left by \(e_2^{\mathsf T}C_g^n\) and on the right by
\((1,1)^{\mathsf T}\) gives the recurrence.  Since
\[
 C_g\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}g+5\\3(g-1)\end{pmatrix},
\]
  the initial values follow.  Writing
  \(d_n=a\lambda_+^n+b\lambda_-^n\), the equations at \(n=0\) and \(n=1\)
  are \(a+b=1\) and
  \(a\lambda_++b\lambda_-=3(g-1)\).  Since
  \(\lambda_+-\lambda_-=4\sqrt g\), they give
  \[
   a=\frac{3(g-1)-\lambda_-}{4\sqrt g}
    =\frac{(\sqrt g-1)(\sqrt g+2)}{2\sqrt g},
   \qquad
   b=\frac{\lambda_+-3(g-1)}{4\sqrt g}
    =-\frac{(\sqrt g+1)(\sqrt g-2)}{2\sqrt g}.
  \]
  Substitution yields the displayed closed form and makes the signs of both
  coefficients explicit.
\end{proof}

The closed form is not needed to establish the dynamical degree, but it is a
useful internal consistency check: the coefficient of the Perron term is
positive because \(C_g\) is positive and both \(e_2\) and the initial vector
pair positively with its Perron eigenvectors.  It also makes clear why the
visible-coordinate argument in Lemma~\ref{lem:visibility} is part of the
theorem rather than an after-the-fact interpretation of a matrix sequence.

\subsection{Why the threshold is \(g\geq5\)}

The lower threshold is forced by the second selector.  The strict inequality
\[
 \frac{3}{g-1}>\frac{2}{g-2}
\]
is equivalent to \(g>4\).  At \(g=4\), the endpoint comparison becomes a tie
for the smallest allowed ratio, and the \(p_2^g\) face is not separated from
the mixed \(p_1^2p_2\) face by the argument above.  At the same time, the
upper-endpoint cone calculation loses its strict margin because
\(g(g-4)=0\).  We therefore state the theorem only for \(g\geq5\); no
continuation to \(g=4\) is hidden in the notation.

The characteristic-zero and coefficient hypotheses have the same status.  The
formal degree inequalities are combinatorial, but the passage from a
selected summand to a nonzero leading coefficient uses the positive integers
\(2\) and \(g\).  A sign-changing potential or a positive-characteristic
specialization would require a new no-cancellation argument and is outside
the present theorem.


\section{The narrow non-product comparison}\label{sec:comparison}

The phrase \emph{non-product} has a deliberately restricted meaning.  The
support of \(V\) contains \(q_1^2q_2^2\), and the support of \(W\) contains
\(p_1^2p_2^2\).  Thus the support graph on the two labels in each phase is
connected.  At the degree level, this connectivity appears as the positive
off-diagonal entries of \(C_g\).  If the phases were independent scalar
recurrences in the displayed split, the complete transfer matrix would be
diagonal after ordering the two labels; that is not what
\eqref{eq:matrix-product} gives.

The comparison with the elementary degree product is equally specific:
\[
 \lambda_1(F_g)=(\sqrt g+1)^2
 <(g-1)^2=\deg(S)\deg(T).
\]
This inequality does not say that every coupled composition has submultiplicative
degree growth, nor that \(F_g\) cannot be conjugated to a product in a different
coordinate system.  It records the exact value for this fixed word and the
strict baseline comparison in the coordinates where the supports are given.

\begin{example}[The endpoint \(g=5\): a complete hand audit]\label{ex:g5}
At the smallest allowed parameter,
\[
 A_5=\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B_5=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 C_5=\begin{pmatrix}8&2\\8&4\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The cone is \(1\leq r<3/2\).  Starting with \(u_0=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\), the
two phase ledgers give
\[
 v_1=A_5u_0=(4,3)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 u_1=C_5u_0=(10,12)^{\mathsf T},
\]
and the next intermediate vector is
\[
 v_2=A_5u_1=(40,32)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 u_2=C_5u_1=(104,128)^{\mathsf T}.
\]
These values are not a numerical experiment: each entry is the selected
weighted degree of a displayed monomial, and the following table records all
strict comparisons at the first two complete steps.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c}
\toprule
step & phase/output & selected degree & largest competitor & gap\\
0 & $S,\widehat p_1$ & $4$ & $1+2=3$ & $1$\\
0 & $S,\widehat p_2$ & $2+1=3$ & carry $1$ & $2$\\
0 & $T,F_{g,1}$ & $4+2\cdot3=10$ & carry $1$ & $9$\\
0 & $T,F_{g,2}$ & $4\cdot3=12$ & $\max\{2\cdot4+3,1\}=11$ & $1$\\
\midrule
1 & $S,\widehat p_1$ & $4\cdot10=40$ & $\max\{10+24,4\}=34$ & $6$\\
1 & $S,\widehat p_2$ & $2\cdot10+12=32$ & carry $3$ & $29$\\
1 & $T,F_{g,1}$ & $40+2\cdot32=104$ & carry $10$ & $94$\\
1 & $T,F_{g,2}$ & $4\cdot32=128$ & $\max\{2\cdot40+32,12\}=112$ & $16$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The ratio map can also be checked by hand.  For
\[
 f_5(r)=\frac{4(2+r)}{8+2r},
\]
one has \(f_5(1)=6/5\), \(f_5(3/2)=14/11<3/2\), and
\[
 f'_5(r)=\frac{16}{(8+2r)^2}>0.
\]
Thus the interval is returned strictly inside itself after one complete
step.  The visible degree sequence begins
\[
 d_0=1,\qquad d_1=12,\qquad d_2=128,
\]
and Cayley--Hamilton gives
\[
 d_{n+2}=12d_{n+1}-16d_n.
\]
The characteristic roots are \(6\pm2\sqrt5\); the positive leading
coefficient in the closed form is the concrete \(g=5\) instance of the
Perron accessibility argument.  In particular,
\[
 \lambda_1(F_5)=6+2\sqrt5<16=(5-1)^2.
\]
\end{example}

\subsection{Support connectivity in the declared coordinate split}
\label{sec:support-connectivity}

We make the word \emph{coupled} precise only in the coordinate split fixed in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}.  Let \(\Gamma_S\) be the undirected graph with
vertices \(\{q_1,q_2\}\) and an edge whenever a monomial in \(V\) contains
both variables.  The monomial \(q_1^2q_2^2\) gives the single edge
\(q_1\mathord{-}q_2\).  Likewise \(\Gamma_T\) has vertices
\(\{p_1,p_2\}\), and \(p_1^2p_2^2\) gives the edge
\(p_1\mathord{-}p_2\).  The two support graphs are therefore connected before
any degree calculation.

The derivative ledger records how this support edge survives the shear:
\(\partial_{q_1}V\) contains \(q_1q_2^2\), while
\(\partial_{q_2}V\) contains \(q_1^2q_2\).  Their degree rows are
\((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\), so both coordinate labels occur in the first phase.
The same two exponent rows occur in the mixed terms of
\(\partial_{p_1}W,\partial_{p_2}W\).  After the two phases, the off-diagonal
entries of
\[
 C_g=\begin{pmatrix}g+3&2\\2(g-1)&g-1\end{pmatrix}
\]
are exactly the algebraic record of those mixed rows.  The diagonal pure-power
terms are still needed to select the faces; connectivity does not mean that
every monomial is selected at every phase.

This observation is deliberately weaker than a product-decomposition
classification.  It proves only that the displayed support cannot be split
into two independent scalar ledgers without discarding a mixed monomial.
It does not rule out a nonlinear change of coordinates, a different shear
word, or a factorization in another category.  The anti-claims in
Section~\ref{sec:limits} are therefore compatible with the graph statement
here.

\section{Limitations, anti-claims, and collision discipline}\label{sec:limits}

The proof is intentionally narrow.  It does not cover arbitrary sparse
potentials, arbitrary coefficients, arbitrary shear words, or positive
characteristic.  In particular, changing signs can invalidate the
coefficientwise no-cancellation step, and changing the supports can invalidate
the two selected rows or the ratio cone.

We do not claim a finite Newton-fan theorem for all symplectic polynomial
automorphisms, a classification in dimension four, or equality between
algebraic, topological, arithmetic, and measure-theoretic entropy.  We do not
study periodic points, traces, multipliers, invariant curves, centralizers,
torus translates, arithmetic specializations, or effective orbit counts.  The
paper contains no numerical or CAS certificate.

The bounded P12--P19 table in Section~\ref{sec:related} is a collision boundary,
not an exhaustive priority search.  The cited literature is context only:
Friedland--Milnor gives the planar baseline; Déserti and Guedj--Sibony provide
higher-dimensional context; Favre--Jonsson supplies historical valuation
context; Dang--Favre motivates explicit spectral visibility; and Fujioka et al.
provide a nearby four-dimensional hyperbolicity object.  None of these sources
proves the family, the selector inequalities, the no-cancellation statement,
or the closed-form root.

The following conditions are hard stops for any revision:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a selector tie or an untracked carried coordinate at any iterate;
\item use of one strict cone across the phase-return equality
  \(v_{n+1}=A_gu_n\);
\item use of a half-step matrix without a period correction;
\item cancellation made possible by signs or positive characteristic;
\item failure to show that the Perron class is reachable and visible to total
  degree;
\item removal of the mixed support or introduction of a universal conjugacy
  claim;
\item promotion of a contextual citation to proof or priority evidence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We fixed an explicit pair of Hamiltonian shears and proved its degree growth
without appealing to a generic Newton-fan principle.  The proof has four
interlocking parts: symmetric Hessians give canonicality and triangular
inverses; the literal support monomials give \(A_g\) and \(B_g\); a
two-stage cone plus old-term dominance and positive coefficients closes the
selector induction; and the complete positive matrix \(C_g\), together with
the visible \(q_2\)-coordinate, gives the Perron formula for the dynamical
degree.  The resulting value \((\sqrt g+1)^2\) is strictly below the product
\((g-1)^2\) of the elementary shear degrees for every \(g\geq5\).

The conclusion is a theorem about this family and this coordinate-level
comparison.  Extending it to other supports or words would require a new
selector and no-cancellation proof; it is not a consequence of the present
argument.
