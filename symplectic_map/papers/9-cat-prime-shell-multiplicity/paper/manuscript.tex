\title{A Multiplicity Audit for Prime-Torsion Euler Products of the Cat Map}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{Pre-review manuscript, August 14, 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
For the cat matrix
\(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\),
we audit whether the complete nonzero prime-torsion shell can supply one
Riemann-style local denominator.  We first re-derive the classical
prime-lattice orbit structure.  Every odd unramified shell has a common
least period, with primitive-orbit multiplicity
\(m_p=(p+1)h_p\) in the split case and
\(m_p=(p-1)h_p\) in the inert case.  The binary shell is one three-cycle,
whereas the ramified shell at five has two two-cycles and two ten-cycles.
We then separate two products: a point-potential return product retains the
primitive length \(|\gamma|\), while a one-time orbit label produces
\((1-p^{-s})^{-m_p}\).  Fixed nonzero scalar coefficients independent of
the local variable cannot reduce this pure denominator degree to one for
any odd prime.  Equal coefficients repair only the first logarithmic
repeat.  Fractional orbit-mass exponents do give exactly one factor, but
only as shell-global normalized counting that extends mechanically to
composite-order shells.  One development-seen exact audit at
\(p=2,3,5,7,11\) reproduces \(m_p=1,2,4,6,24\); it is a finite control,
not evidence for the all-prime theorem.  This low-novelty negative audit
therefore closes only the direct scalar normalization attempt; centralizer,
matrix, numerator, and Fredholm mechanisms remain open.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} cat map; prime torsion; finite-field orbit;
dynamical Euler product; scalar weight; multiplicity obstruction.

\section{Introduction and bounded question}
\label{sec:introduction}

Let \(T_A\colon\Ttwo\to\Ttwo\) be the automorphism induced by
\begin{equation}
  A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:cat-matrix}
\end{equation}
For each rational prime \(p\), the nonzero \(p\)-torsion shell is
\(\F_p^2\setminus\{0\}\).  The bounded question of this note is whether
the complete shell, with one label \(p\), can produce exactly one local
denominator \((1-p^{-s})^{-1}\) without discarding or globally renormalizing
its primitive orbits.  This diagnostic is not the ordinary Artin--Mazur
zeta function of the toral automorphism, and it is not a proposal for a new
dynamical zeta function.

The arithmetic entering the question is classical.  Modular and quadratic
descriptions of cat-map periods go back at least to
\citet{PercivalVivaldi1987} and \citet{DysonFalk1992}.
\citet{Gaspari1994} directly studies the same cat map on prime lattices,
including the common nonzero period away from the ramified prime and the
resulting orbit decomposition.  The cycle-generating formulas of
\citet{BaakeNeumaerkerRoberts2013}, especially their Appendix~A.1, directly
cover the binary and ramified boundaries used below.  We therefore present
the finite-field classification only as a self-contained re-derivation
needed to expose multiplicity, not as a new orbit theorem.

Finite and rational-lattice orbit products are likewise established
objects \citep{BaakeRobertsWeiss2008}, while the ordinary toral dynamical
zeta function has an arithmetic formulation independent of the prime-shell
relabeling used here \citep{BaakeLauPaskunas2010}.  The fixed-point
exponential, primitive-orbit grouping, weighted products, and analytic
operator framework are classical \citep{ArtinMazur1965,Ruelle1976,
ParryPollicott1990}.  Recent prime-power cycle work further limits any broad
finite-ring novelty claim \citep{TanLi2025}; recent finite-permutation
determinant packaging for the cat map likewise precludes a generic claim of
new cycle-product machinery \citep{Chandra2026}.

Against that prior art, the note makes a deliberately modest contribution
that is primarily diagnostic.  It juxtaposes known prime-shell arithmetic
with elementary product algebra and records where a specific one-factor
construction changes semantics.  More precisely, we:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item re-derive the split, inert, binary, and ramified orbit counts and
        isolate the uniform lower bound \(m_p\ge p-1\) for odd primes;
  \item distinguish a genuine point-potential return product from an
        externally assigned one-time orbit-label product;
  \item prove a denominator-degree obstruction only for fixed nonzero scalar
        factors, then record the zero-weight, equal-weight, fractional, and
        selector boundaries; and
  \item give safe global convergence strips and a provenance-closed finite
        audit without promoting five development-seen rows to all-prime
        evidence.
\end{enumerate}

The negative statement is intentionally narrow.  It does not exclude
matrix-valued factors, numerator or alternating cancellation, transfer or
Fredholm determinants, cohomological superdeterminants, variable-dependent
weights, enriched selectors, or centralizer quotients.  It gives no result
in \(2<\RePart s\le3\), no exact abscissa, analytic continuation, functional
equation, or zero statement.  It gives no prime-orbit or prime-zero
correspondence, no quantization, no historical priority claim, and no Route
B construction.

Section~\ref{sec:arithmetic} proves the prime-shell theorem.
Section~\ref{sec:products} separates the two products.
Section~\ref{sec:scalar} states the scalar obstruction and its exact
normalization boundary.  Section~\ref{sec:global} gives the safe analytic
strips and the open escape mechanisms.  Section~\ref{sec:audit} records the
fixed exact audit and its proof/computation firewall.

\section{Prime-shell arithmetic}
\label{sec:arithmetic}

For a prime \(p\), set
\begin{equation}
  V_p=\F_p^2\setminus\{0\},\qquad
  \Gamma_p=V_p/\langle A\rangle,\qquad
  m_p=|\Gamma_p|.
  \label{eq:shell-definitions}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\Gamma_p\) is the set of primitive \(A\)-orbits partitioning
\(V_p\), and \(|\gamma|\) denotes the least dynamical period of
\(\gamma\).  The characteristic polynomial is
\begin{equation}
  f(X)=X^2-3X+1,\qquad \disc(f)=5.
  \label{eq:characteristic}
\end{equation}
For \(p\ne5\), let \(\tau_p\) be the order of \(A\) in
\(\GL_2(\F_p)\).  For odd \(p\ne5\), the Legendre symbol
\(\left(\frac5p\right)\) separates the split and inert cases.

\begin{theorem}[Prime-shell orbit multiplicity]
\label{thm:prime-shell}
For the matrix in \eqref{eq:cat-matrix}, the following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item At \(p=2\), the three points of \(V_2\) form one orbit of length
        three, so \(m_2=1\).
  \item If \(p\) is odd, \(p\ne5\), and
        \(\left(\frac5p\right)=1\), every point of \(V_p\) has exact
        period \(\tau_p\), where \(\tau_p\mid p-1\).  With
        \(h_p=(p-1)/\tau_p\),
        \begin{equation}
          m_p=(p+1)h_p\ge p+1.
          \label{eq:split-multiplicity}
        \end{equation}
        The two eigenlines contribute \(2h_p\) cycles, and their complement
        contributes \((p-1)h_p\) cycles.
  \item If \(p\) is odd and \(\left(\frac5p\right)=-1\), every point of
        \(V_p\) has exact period \(\tau_p\), where \(\tau_p\mid p+1\).
        With \(h_p=(p+1)/\tau_p\),
        \begin{equation}
          m_p=(p-1)h_p\ge p-1.
          \label{eq:inert-multiplicity}
        \end{equation}
  \item At \(p=5\), four points have exact period two and twenty points have
        exact period ten.  They form two cycles of each length, so \(m_5=4\).
\end{enumerate}
Consequently \(p=2\) is the unique prime shell with \(m_p=1\), and
\(m_p\ge p-1\) for every odd prime.
\end{theorem}

The common-period and orbit-decomposition content of
Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-shell} directly overlaps the classical prime-lattice
analysis of \citet{Gaspari1994}; the two exceptional boundary profiles are
also directly represented in \citet{BaakeNeumaerkerRoberts2013}.  We give
the full proof because the later product audit depends on the exact number
and lengths of primitive factors.

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that \(p\) is odd, \(p\ne5\), and
\(\left(\frac5p\right)=1\).  The polynomial \(f\) has distinct roots
\(\lambda\) and \(\lambda^{-1}\) in \(\F_p\), so a change of basis
conjugates \(A\) to
\(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})\).  Both diagonal entries have
the same multiplicative order; call it \(\tau_p\).  A nonzero vector
\((x,y)\) has at least one nonzero coordinate.  The equality
\[
  A^k(x,y)=(x,y)
\]
therefore forces \(\lambda^k=1\) on every nonzero coordinate, and that
condition also suffices.  Every nonzero vector consequently has least period
\(\tau_p\).  Since \(\lambda\in\F_p^\times\), one has
\(\tau_p\mid p-1\).

The \(p^2-1\) nonzero vectors split into
\[
  m_p=\frac{p^2-1}{\tau_p}
      =(p+1)\frac{p-1}{\tau_p}=(p+1)h_p
\]
cycles.  Each eigenline contains \(p-1\) nonzero points, so the two
eigenlines contribute \(2(p-1)/\tau_p=2h_p\) cycles.  Their complement has
\((p-1)^2\) points and contributes
\((p-1)^2/\tau_p=(p-1)h_p\) cycles.  This proves the split statement and its
lower bound without assuming that \(\tau_p\) is maximal.

Now suppose \(\left(\frac5p\right)=-1\).  The polynomial \(f\) is
irreducible over \(\F_p\).  Identify the two-dimensional \(\F_p\)-space
with \(\F_{p^2}\) so that \(A\) acts as multiplication by a root
\(\lambda\) of \(f\).  Frobenius sends \(\lambda\) to the other root, so
\[
  \lambda^p=\lambda^{-1},\qquad \lambda^{p+1}=1.
\]
Thus \(\tau_p=\ord(\lambda)\mid p+1\).  For every nonzero
\(v\in\F_{p^2}\),
\[
  A^kv=v
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  (\lambda^k-1)v=0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \lambda^k=1.
\]
Every nonzero vector again has exact period \(\tau_p\), and hence
\[
  m_p=\frac{p^2-1}{\tau_p}
      =(p-1)\frac{p+1}{\tau_p}=(p-1)h_p\ge p-1.
\]

At \(p=2\), the reduction of \(f\) is \(X^2+X+1\), which is irreducible.
Cayley--Hamilton gives \(A^2+A+I=0\), and multiplication by \(A-I\) gives
\(A^3=I\).  The polynomial has no root one, so there is no nonzero fixed
vector.  The three nonzero vectors must therefore form a single
three-cycle.

It remains to treat the ramified prime.  Modulo five, put
\begin{equation}
  N=A+I=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:nilpotent-N}
\end{equation}
Direct multiplication gives
\(A=-I+N\), \(N^2=0\), and \(\rank N=1\).  The kernel of \(N\) has five
elements.  On its four nonzero elements, \(A=-I\), so every such point has
exact period two and the four points form two cycles.

For every \(k\ge1\), the nilpotent binomial formula gives
\begin{equation}
  A^k=(-1)^k(I-kN).
  \label{eq:nilpotent-power}
\end{equation}
Take \(v\notin\ker N\).  If odd \(k\) fixed \(v\), applying \(N\) to
\(A^kv=v\) would give \(-Nv=Nv\), hence \(Nv=0\) in characteristic five,
a contradiction.  For even \(k\), equation \eqref{eq:nilpotent-power}
fixes \(v\) exactly when \(kNv=0\), or equivalently when \(5\mid k\).
The least positive even multiple of five is ten.  The twenty vectors outside
\(\ker N\) therefore have exact period ten and form two cycles.  Thus
\(m_5=2+2=4\).

The four cases exhaust the primes.  The split and inert bounds are at least
two for odd primes, while \(m_5=4\); only \(p=2\) has one cycle.
\end{proof}

\section{Two products that must not be identified}
\label{sec:products}

The orbit count alone does not determine which local variable a dynamical
construction assigns to an orbit.  We distinguish a point observable from a
one-time orbit label before manipulating either product.

\subsection{The point-potential return product}

On the fixed shell \(V_p\), take the point observable
\begin{equation}
  L(x)=\log p.
  \label{eq:point-observable}
\end{equation}
Its Birkhoff sum along a primitive orbit \(\gamma\) of length
\(\ell=|\gamma|\) is \(\ell\log p\).  Consider the fixed-point exponential
\begin{equation}
  Z_{\raw,p}(s)
  =\exp\!\left(
    \sum_{n\ge1}\frac1n
    \sum_{x\in V_p\cap\Fix(A^n)}
       \exp\bigl(-sS_nL(x)\bigr)
  \right).
  \label{eq:raw-fixed-point}
\end{equation}
This is a finite-permutation instance of the classical fixed-point and
primitive-orbit ledger \citep{ArtinMazur1965,Ruelle1976,
ParryPollicott1990}.  We use it only to derive the shell-local formal factor.

A primitive orbit of length \(\ell\) contributes to the \(n\)-th return sum
only when \(n=r\ell\).  At that return it supplies \(\ell\) fixed points,
each with \(S_{r\ell}L=r\ell\log p\).  Its logarithmic contribution is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{r\ge1}\frac{\ell}{r\ell}p^{-sr\ell}
  =\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{p^{-sr\ell}}r
  =-\log(1-p^{-s\ell}).
  \label{eq:raw-orbit-contribution}
\end{equation}
Grouping over primitive cycles therefore gives
\begin{equation}
  Z_{\raw,p}(s)
  =\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}
    (1-p^{-s|\gamma|})^{-1}.
  \label{eq:raw-product}
\end{equation}
For the binary, split, and inert cases, all primitive lengths equal
\(\tau_p\), so the factor is
\((1-p^{-s\tau_p})^{-m_p}\).  At the ramified prime, the two cycle lengths
remain separate:
\begin{equation}
  Z_{\raw,5}(s)
  =(1-5^{-2s})^{-2}(1-5^{-10s})^{-2}.
  \label{eq:raw-five}
\end{equation}
This mixed factor is the sharpest finite reminder that a genuine return
retains dynamical length.

\subsection{The externally assigned orbit-label product}

Now identify the primitive cycles first and assign the shell label \(\log p\)
once to each of them, independently of length.  This different definition
gives
\begin{equation}
  Z_{\lab,p}(s)
  =\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}
  =(1-p^{-s})^{-m_p}.
  \label{eq:label-product}
\end{equation}
Its formal logarithm is
\begin{equation}
  \log Z_{\lab,p}(s)
  =m_p\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{p^{-rs}}r,
  \label{eq:label-logarithm}
\end{equation}
so the coefficient at repetition \(r\) is exactly \(m_p/r\).  Dividing a
raw orbit sum by its primitive period can change \(|\gamma|\log p\) into
\(\log p\), but it does not remove any of the \(m_p\) primitive factors.
Period normalization changes the label; it does not quotient the orbit set.

The label product in \eqref{eq:label-product} is therefore not silently
identified with \eqref{eq:raw-product}, with the finite-lattice Euler
products of \citet{BaakeRobertsWeiss2008}, or with the ordinary toral zeta
function studied by \citet{BaakeLauPaskunas2010}.  The point of the audit is
precisely to keep these constructions separate.

\section{Scalar obstruction and exact normalization boundary}
\label{sec:scalar}

Write \(z=p^{-s}\) as a formal local variable.  The next theorem concerns
only finite pure-denominator products with fixed scalar coefficients.  This
scope is part of the statement, not a later qualification.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed nonzero scalar denominator obstruction]
\label{thm:scalar-obstruction}
Let \(p\) be an odd prime.  For each \(\gamma\in\Gamma_p\), let
\(w_\gamma\in\C\setminus\{0\}\) be fixed and independent of \(z\).  Then
\begin{equation}
  \prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}(1-w_\gamma z)^{-1}
  \ne (1-z)^{-1}
  \label{eq:scalar-no-go}
\end{equation}
as rational functions, equivalently as formal power series at \(z=0\).
If zero coefficients are admitted, equality holds if and only if the
multiset of weights is \(\{1,0,\ldots,0\}\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An equality in \eqref{eq:scalar-no-go} would imply, after clearing
denominators,
\begin{equation}
  \prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}(1-w_\gamma z)=1-z.
  \label{eq:cleared-denominators}
\end{equation}
If every \(w_\gamma\) is nonzero, the polynomial on the left has degree
\(m_p\) and nonzero leading coefficient.  The polynomial on the right has
degree one.  Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-shell} gives \(m_p\ge2\) for every odd
prime, so equality is impossible.

If zeros are allowed, omit their unit factors.  The degree comparison shows
that exactly one nonzero weight can remain.  Comparing the coefficient of
\(z\) then forces that weight to equal one.  Conversely the multiset
\(\{1,0,\ldots,0\}\) plainly gives \((1-z)^{-1}\).
\end{proof}

For a finite scalar potential \(\phi\), an orbit weight of the form
\(w_\gamma=\exp(S_\gamma\phi)\) is nonzero.  Thus
Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar-obstruction} applies when those weights are placed in
the same finite pure-denominator product.  The theorem is not a theorem
about the full weighted-zeta or transfer-operator frameworks of
\citet{Ruelle1976} and \citet{ParryPollicott1990}.  It does not address
matrix weights, vector bundles, \(z\)-dependent weights, numerator
cancellation, alternating products, Fredholm determinants, or cohomological
superdeterminants.

\subsection{Repetition detects the equal-weight failure}

Taking formal logarithms in a hypothetical scalar identity shows that the
necessary and sufficient power-sum conditions are
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}w_\gamma^r=1
  \qquad (r\ge1).
  \label{eq:power-sum-condition}
\end{equation}
The tempting equal choice \(w_\gamma=1/m_p\) repairs the first coefficient,
but gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}w_\gamma^r
  =m_p^{\,1-r}.
  \label{eq:equal-weight-sums}
\end{equation}
When \(m_p>1\), the value differs from one for every \(r\ge2\).  The
registered repetitions \(r=1,2,3\) are exact examples of this formal
identity, not a numerical approximation to an infinite product.

\subsection{Fractional outer exponents succeed globally}

The primitive cycles partition \(V_p\), so
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}|\gamma|=|V_p|=p^2-1.
  \label{eq:shell-partition}
\end{equation}
Interpret fractional powers as formal germs at \(z=0\), using
\((1-z)^{-a}=\exp(-a\log(1-z))\).  Equation
\eqref{eq:shell-partition} then gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
  \prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma_p}
    (1-p^{-s})^{-|\gamma|/(p^2-1)}
  =(1-p^{-s})^{-1}.
  \label{eq:fractional-prime-identity}
\end{equation}
This is a successful repair.  It is nevertheless a different construction
from Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar-obstruction}: the exponent is applied outside
the common factor and depends on the complete shell cardinality and complete
cycle partition.  We call it \emph{global normalized counting}.

The same argument is not specific to primes.  For an integer \(q\ge2\), let
\(E_q\subset\Ttwo\) be the points of exact additive order \(q\).  The
integral automorphism \(A\) permutes \(E_q\), whose cardinality is the Jordan
totient
\begin{equation}
  |E_q|=J_2(q)
  =q^2\prod_{\substack{\ell\mid q\\ \ell\ \mathrm{prime}}}
     (1-\ell^{-2}).
  \label{eq:jordan-totient}
\end{equation}
If \(\Gamma(E_q)\) denotes its cycle set, then symbolically
\begin{equation}
  \prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma(E_q)}
    (1-q^{-s})^{-|\gamma|/J_2(q)}
  =(1-q^{-s})^{-1}.
  \label{eq:fractional-composite-identity}
\end{equation}
No value of \(q\) is selected or enumerated here.  Equation
\eqref{eq:fractional-composite-identity} records only that normalized cycle
mass works for every finite permutation of an exact-order shell, so the
mechanism has no intrinsic prime specificity.

\subsection{A selector has an exact discard cost}

Keeping one orbit produces one factor by definition.  On the complete shell
it also discards exactly \(m_p-1\) primitive cycles and adds a global
symmetry-breaking choice.  The frozen construction supplies no canonical
selector.  We record this cost without claiming that no enriched or
canonical selector can exist.

\section{Global bounds and the escape boundary}
\label{sec:global}

The one-time label factors define the formal prime-indexed product
\begin{equation}
  Z_{\lab}(s)=\prod_p(1-p^{-s})^{-m_p}.
  \label{eq:global-label-product}
\end{equation}
The finite audit in Section~\ref{sec:audit} supplies no analytic evidence
for this product.  The only global conclusions used here come from the
all-prime bounds in Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-shell}.

\begin{proposition}[Safe convergence strips]
\label{prop:convergence}
Let \(s=\sigma+it\).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For real \(1<s\le2\), the positive logarithmic series for
        \(Z_{\lab}(s)\) diverges.
  \item For complex \(s\) with \(1<\sigma\le2\), the logarithmic series is
        not absolutely convergent.
  \item For \(\sigma>3\), the logarithmic series is absolutely convergent.
\end{enumerate}
No statement is made for \(2<\sigma\le3\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For \(1<\sigma\le2\), the first repetition and the odd-prime lower bound
give
\begin{equation}
  \sum_p m_pp^{-\sigma}
  \ge \sum_{p\ \mathrm{odd}}(p-1)p^{-\sigma}.
  \label{eq:convergence-lower}
\end{equation}
For odd \(p\), the summand on the right is at least
\(\tfrac12p^{1-\sigma}\), which is at least \(1/(2p)\) when
\(\sigma\le2\).  Euler's divergence of \(\sum_p1/p\) proves divergence.
When \(s\) is real, every term in
\[
  \sum_p m_p\sum_{r\ge1}\frac{p^{-rs}}r
\]
is positive.  For complex \(s\), the same first-repetition estimate proves
failure of absolute convergence.

For \(\sigma>3\), use \(m_p\le|V_p|=p^2-1\).  For all sufficiently large
\(p\),
\[
  \sum_{r\ge1}\frac{|p^{-rs}|}{r}
  =-\log(1-p^{-\sigma})
  \le C_\sigma p^{-\sigma}.
\]
The absolute logarithmic series is therefore bounded, up to finitely many
terms, by a constant multiple of
\[
  \sum_p p^{2-\sigma}
  \le \sum_{n\ge2}n^{2-\sigma}<\infty.
\]
This proves the stated upper strip and nothing sharper.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:convergence} does not determine an exact abscissa.  It
does not address conditional convergence in the gap, meromorphic
continuation, a functional equation, or zeros.  These omissions are
substantive boundaries rather than missing numerical experiments.

\subsection{Mechanisms not closed by the theorem}

The degree proof uses commutative scalar factors with no numerator.  A
matrix determinant can combine eigenchannels; an alternating or
cohomological product can place factors in a numerator; and a Fredholm or
transfer determinant can encode cancellations not visible in a finite
product of scalar linear denominators.  Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar-obstruction}
excludes none of these possibilities.  The classical weighted and
operator-theoretic literature is precisely why the scope must remain narrow
\citep{Ruelle1976,ParryPollicott1990}.

A centralizer quotient is another genuine escape.  In the inert case, the
finite-field centralizer can be identified with \(\F_{p^2}^{\times}\),
which acts transitively on the nonzero shell.  In the split case, the two
nonzero eigenlines and their complement form three natural centralizer
strata.  At \(p=5\), the Jordan kernel minus zero and its complement form
two natural strata.  Such symmetry structure is standard context for
rational-lattice orbit analysis \citep{BaakeNeumaerkerRoberts2013}.
The present audit neither constructs nor rules out such a quotient.  A
successful quotient would still depend on the \(p\)-shell centralizer, and
the label \(p\) would still come from the externally specified
additive-order shell.  The centralizer route remains follow-up work.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The strongest conclusion of this audit is a scoped incompatibility, not a
new dynamical system.  The complete nonzero \(p\)-torsion shell has
\(m_p>1\) primitive orbits for every odd prime.  A point-potential return
retains their lengths; an external one-time label retains their count.
Fixed nonzero scalar denominator weights cannot collapse that count to one.
Equal weights fail under repetition.  Fractional orbit masses succeed
exactly, but only by using the complete shell as a normalized counting
space, a mechanism that also works symbolically for composite order.

Most ingredients have direct prior collisions.  Prime-lattice common
periods and orbit decompositions are classical \citep{Gaspari1994}; the
binary and ramified cycle boundaries and their symmetry context are already
explicit in the rational-lattice literature
\citep{BaakeNeumaerkerRoberts2013}; finite-lattice Euler products and
primitive/repetition formalisms are also established
\citep{BaakeRobertsWeiss2008,ArtinMazur1965,Ruelle1976,
ParryPollicott1990}.  The value of the note is therefore a transparent
semantic and mechanism audit, not classification, zeta, determinant, or
priority novelty.

The exact terminal decision is
\begin{center}
\small
\classification\\[2pt]
\routea\\[2pt]
\routeb.
\end{center}
The first label certifies only the frozen prime-shell multiplicity and pure
scalar obstruction.  The second admits the exact fractional identity and
rejects it only as global, tautological, and non-prime-specific.  The third
records that no spectral or quantization route was opened.  The next bounded
question is whether a centralizer-enriched quotient can compress shell
multiplicity without merely hiding a global selector.  This note neither
answers nor experimentally investigates that question.
