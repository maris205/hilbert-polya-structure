\section{The actual spectral Jacobian}
\label{spec:section}

Fix a field $k$, units $s,t$ with $s$ of exact order $r$, and
$K=k(c)$, where $c$ is transcendental.  In this section put
$T=t^r$, $\varepsilon=(-1)^{r+1}$, and $n=2r$.
Let $X/K$ be the generic fiber of the original pencil in
Theorem~\ref{geom:pencil}.  We will identify its Jacobian, including
the torsor structure when $X(K)$ has no chosen point.  General
spectral-line-bundle and cyclic-Picard constructions provide the
background \cite{Beauville1990,InoueVanhaeckeYamazaki2015}.
We give the rank-two construction and its inverse here, since the
identity of the original state curve is essential.

\subsection{A finite critical algebra before the spectral construction}

Consider the projective cubic family $W\to\mathbb A^1_c$ with affine
equation
\begin{equation}
F(u,v,c)=v^2+cuv-\varepsilon Tv-u^3+Tu^2=0,
\qquad O_W=[0:1:0].
\label{spec:weierstrass}
\end{equation}
Its role at this point is purely algebraic; no identity with the
original state curve is assumed.

\begin{lemma}\label{spec:finite-critical}
The family $W$ is projective and flat, its total space is
geometrically regular, and each finite geometric fiber is integral
and reduced.  Its relative critical scheme is finite, with coordinate
algebra
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\Gamma(Z_W,\mathcal O_{Z_W})
 =k[z]/\bigl((T-z^2)^2-\varepsilon Tz\bigr),\\
c=\varepsilon-z-z^3/T.
\end{gathered}
\label{spec:critical-algebra}
\end{equation}
In particular $W_K$ is geometrically smooth of genus one.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The homogeneous equation is
$v^2w+cuvw-\varepsilon Tvw^2-u^3+Tu^2w=0$.
At infinity it has only $O_W$, where the derivative with respect to
$w$ is $1$.  Thus $O_W$ is relatively smooth at every value of $c$.
Every positive-degree component of a projective plane curve meets
the line at infinity.  For these cubics all such intersections would
have to occur at $O_W$.  Two components, or a repeated component,
would make that point nonsmooth.  Hence each geometric fiber has
one component of multiplicity one.  A plane hypersurface has no
embedded components, so the fiber is integral and reduced.  The
cubic Hilbert polynomial is constant, giving flatness, and the
projective equation gives properness.  Each fiber has arithmetic
genus one.

The relative derivatives and the derivative in the base direction are
\begin{equation}
F_u=cv-3u^2+2Tu,\qquad
F_v=2v+cu-\varepsilon T,\qquad F_c=uv.
\label{spec:derivatives}
\end{equation}
A singular point of a fiber cannot have $u=0$: its possible values
of $v$ are $0,\varepsilon T$, and $F_v$ is respectively
$-\varepsilon T,\varepsilon T$.  If $v=0$, the remaining possibility
is $u=T$, where $F_u=-T^2\ne0$.  Thus every fiber singularity has
$uv\ne0$, and $F_c\ne0$ there.  The total space is smooth at those
points by the absolute Jacobian criterion; it is smooth at all
remaining points because it is relatively smooth there.  These
arguments also apply after an algebraic closure of $k$.

The relative differential presentation on the affine chart is
\[
\Omega^1_{W/\mathbb A^1}
=(\mathcal O_W\,du\oplus\mathcal O_W\,dv)/(F_u\,du+F_v\,dv).
\]
The critical scheme is therefore given by $(F_u,F_v)$, and it has
no point at infinity.  The preceding calculation places its entire
support in $uv\ne0$.  This contains the scheme itself, including
nilpotents: the closed complement would otherwise be a nonempty
finite-type scheme and would have a geometric closed point.
Eliminate $c$ by $c=(\varepsilon T-2v)/u$.  Substitution into $F=0$
and $uF_u=0$ gives
\[
v^2=u^2(T-u),\qquad \varepsilon Tv=u^3.
\]
Indeed the first substituted equation is $-v^2-u^3+Tu^2=0$;
the second, after using it, reduces to $\varepsilon Tv-u^3=0$.
Writing $z=v/u$ now gives
\[
u=T-z^2,\qquad v=z(T-z^2),\qquad
(T-z^2)^2=\varepsilon Tz.
\]
Conversely, in the quotient by the displayed quartic, $z$ is a
unit because its constant term is $T^2$.  The equality
$(T-z^2)^2=\varepsilon Tz$ makes $T-z^2$, and then $v$, units.
The preceding formulas therefore define the inverse map and satisfy
$F=F_u=F_v=0$.  They also give
\[
c=T/z-3z=\varepsilon-z-z^3/T,
\]
where the last equality is the quartic relation divided by $Tz$.
This proves \eqref{spec:critical-algebra} scheme-theoretically.
The quartic is monic, so the algebra is finite-dimensional even when
its roots repeat.  Consequently its generic fiber over $k[c]$ is
empty.  The same calculation over an algebraic closure proves that
$W_K$ is geometrically smooth; its integral cubic fibers have
arithmetic genus one.  No step divided by two or three.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The two spectral charts and their four endpoints}

Define $E/K$ by gluing the two monic quadratic charts
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(Z,\lambda)&=\lambda^2-(T+cZ+Z^2)\lambda+\varepsilon Z^3=0,\\
\mu^2-(1+cV+TV^2)\mu+\varepsilon V&=0,
\qquad V=Z^{-1},\quad\mu=\lambda Z^{-2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{spec:quotient-charts}
\end{equation}
On $Z\lambda\ne0$ the substitutions
\begin{equation}
u=\frac{\varepsilon TZ}{\lambda},\quad
v=\frac{\varepsilon T^2}{\lambda},\qquad
Z=\frac{Tu}{v},\quad \lambda=\frac{\varepsilon T^2}{v}
\label{spec:birational-change}
\end{equation}
are mutually inverse, since
\[
\frac{v^3}{T^3}\Phi(Tu/v,\varepsilon T^2/v)=-\varepsilon F(u,v,c).
\]
They identify the locus $Z\ne0,\infty$ with an open subset of the
smooth $W_K$: here the spectral equation forces $\lambda\ne0$.
The two points over $Z=0$ have $\lambda=0,T$, with quadratic
derivatives $-T,T$.  The two points over $V=0$ have $\mu=0,1$,
with derivatives $-1,1$.  Thus every endpoint is smooth in every
characteristic.  This proves smoothness of the whole $E$, without
a characteristic-dependent discriminant argument.

For the original spectral variable $z$ define $C/K$ by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\lambda^2-(T+cz^r+z^{2r})\lambda+\varepsilon z^{3r}&=0,\\
\mu^2-(1+cv^r+Tv^{2r})\mu+\varepsilon v^r&=0,
\qquad v=z^{-1},\quad\mu=\lambda z^{-2r}.
\end{aligned}
\label{spec:original-charts}
\end{equation}
Write $P_0,P_T$ for $(z,\lambda)=(0,0),(0,T)$, and $Q_2,Q_1$ for
the points at $v=0$ with $\mu=1,0$.  The same derivatives show that
$z$ or $v$ is a local parameter at each of these four points.
Away from them, $C$ is the pullback of $E$ by $Z=z^r$, a finite
\'{e}tale map on the punctured line because $rz^{r-1}$ is a unit.
Consequently $C$ is geometrically smooth as well.

The two-chart descriptions give finite flat degree-two maps to their
respective projective lines.  The bases $1,\lambda$ and $1,\mu$
have the transition prescribed in the equations, so
\begin{equation}
(\tau_C)_*\mathcal O_C=\mathcal O\oplus\mathcal O(-2r),
\qquad
(\tau_E)_*\mathcal O_E=\mathcal O\oplus\mathcal O(-2).
\label{spec:finite-pushdown}
\end{equation}
These curves are projective and have $h^0(\mathcal O)=1$ after
algebraic closure.  Smoothness and geometric connectedness imply
geometric integrality, and their Euler characteristics yield
$g(C)=2r-1$ and $g(E)=1$.  In particular, the genus of $C$ is not
the genus of the original state curve $X$.

Let $G=\langle\sigma\rangle$, with
$\sigma(z,\lambda)=(sz,\lambda)$.  On either monic quadratic chart
every element is uniquely a sum of a polynomial and a polynomial
times $\lambda$, or times $\mu$ at infinity.  Taking invariants
retains exactly the powers of $z^r$ or $v^r$.  Thus the quotient is
the specified $\pi:C\to E$, $Z=z^r$, not merely a curve with the
same function field.  All four endpoints are fixed by the full
group; their parameter maps are $z\mapsto z^r$ or $v\mapsto v^r$.
For $r=1$ the quotient is the identity.  Finally,
\eqref{spec:birational-change} extends uniquely to a smooth
projective-curve isomorphism $E\simeq W_K$.  At
$O=(Z,\lambda)=(0,0)$ the orders of $Z,\lambda$ are $1,3$, so
$u,v$ have pole orders $2,3$; hence $O$ maps to $O_W$.  We use
$O$ as the origin on $E$.

\begin{theorem}\label{spec:jacobian}
The original generic curve $X/K$ is an $E$-torsor over $K$, and
$\operatorname{Jac}(X)\simeq E$ over that same field.  More precisely,
the actual matrix $M_{r,s}$ defines a morphism
\[
\alpha:X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}^{2r}(C)
\]
which identifies $X$ with a geometric translate of
$\pi^*\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)$.  The pullback
$\pi^*:\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)\to\operatorname{Pic}^0(C)$ is a
closed immersion with trivial scheme-theoretic kernel.
No rational point on $X$ is assumed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We separate the construction of the actual map from the group
calculation which identifies its image.

\emph{The actual spectral module in families.}
On the nonempty torus open $X^\circ\subset X$, put
$M(z)=A(s^{r-1}z)\cdots A(z)$ in the fixed gauge of
\eqref{main:matrix}.  The original trace and determinant give
\[
\det(\lambda I-M(z))
=\lambda^2-(T+cz^r+z^{2r})\lambda+\varepsilon z^{3r}.
\]
Let the generator $\lambda$ of the spectral algebra act by $M(z)$
on $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1_z\times X^\circ}^{\oplus2}$.
Cayley--Hamilton verifies its defining relation; at infinity use
$v^nM(v^{-1})$.  The finite-map equivalence between sheaves and
modules defines a sheaf $\mathcal L$ on $C\times X^\circ$ with
\begin{equation}
(\tau_C)_*\mathcal L=\mathcal O^{\oplus2}.
\label{spec:module-pushdown}
\end{equation}
It is a line bundle.  To check this also in the parameter direction,
suppose that at a geometric parameter value and a finite $z=a$ the
matrix were scalar, $M(a)=bI$.  At $(a,b)$ the adjugate of
$\lambda I-M(z)$ is zero, so both partial derivatives of its
determinant vanish.  This would be a singular point of the already
smooth $C$.  At infinity the matrix is
$P=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$, also nonscalar.  A nonscalar
two-by-two matrix over a field has a cyclic vector: some vector $e$
makes $e,M(a)e$ a basis.  The same determinant remains a unit in a
neighborhood in the family.  Mapping the spectral-algebra basis
$1,\lambda$ to $e,M(z)e$ there exhibits the module as free of
rank one over that algebra.  These neighborhoods prove the claimed
line-bundle property of $\mathcal L$.

For every fiber $L$ of this family,
$\chi(L)=\chi(\mathcal O^{\oplus2}_{\mathbb P^1})=2$.
Since $g(C)=n-1$, Riemann--Roch gives $\deg L=n$.  The family
therefore defines $\alpha$ on $X^\circ$.  Properness of the Picard
component and the DVR valuative criterion extend it uniquely over
every omitted point of the smooth projective curve $X$.

\emph{Recovery of the constant-conjugacy class.}
For a line bundle with pushdown \eqref{spec:module-pushdown},
multiplication by the spectral coordinate is a map
\[
\lambda:(\tau_C)_*L\longrightarrow(\tau_C)_*L(n).
\]
A trivialization of the pushdown recovers a matrix polynomial of
degree at most $n$.  Two trivializations differ by a constant
$\operatorname{GL}_2$ matrix, not a rational $z$-dependent gauge.
Conversely a line-bundle isomorphism pushes down to such a constant
matrix intertwining the $\lambda$ actions.  This recovery works
in families on the open locus where
$H^0(C,L(-1))=H^1(C,L(-1))=0$.  There the pushdown is trivial,
$H^0(C,L)$ has rank two, and its evaluation map identifies
$H^0(C,L)\otimes\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}$ with the pushdown.
These conditions hold on our image, because its twist pushes down
to $\mathcal O(-1)^{\oplus2}$.  The rational point $P_0\in C(K)$
allows rigidification of the Picard functor, so a universal line
bundle and this family recovery are available on the relevant
Picard open set.  This is the particular spectral-correspondence
argument needed here; it has not used a characteristic-zero
version of a general moduli theorem.

\emph{A rational inverse to the original states.}
Write $M_j=[z^j]M$.  The original matrix gives the three identities
\begin{equation}
M_n=P,\qquad (M_{n-1})_{12}=1,\qquad
M_0=t^{r-1}A_0.
\label{spec:endpoint-coefficients}
\end{equation}
For the first, the highest coefficients multiply to
$s^{2\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}i}P^r=P$.
For the second, a term of total degree $2r-1$ contains one $A_1$
and otherwise $P$'s.  Its $(1,2)$ entry is zero unless $A_1$ is
the rightmost factor, since a $P$ on its right kills the second
column.  The remaining term has scalar multiplier
$s^{2\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}i}=1$ and $(A_1)_{12}=1$.
Finally $\operatorname{tr}A_0=t$, $\det A_0=0$, so
$A_0^2=tA_0$ and $M_0=A_0^r=t^{r-1}A_0$.
These proofs include $r=1$ with empty products interpreted as one.
Since $(A_0)_{12}=-x$ and $(A_0)_{22}=x(y-1)$, recovery is
\begin{equation}
x=-\frac{(M_0)_{12}}{t^{r-1}(M_{n-1})_{12}},\qquad
y=1+\frac{(M_0)_{22}}{t^{r-1}x}.
\label{spec:state-inverse}
\end{equation}
The denominators are nonzero on $X^\circ$.  A constant conjugacy
preserving $M_n=P$ is diagonal; it scales the two $(1,2)$ entries
together and preserves the $(2,2)$ entry.  Thus these formulas are
independent of the remaining gauge.

They are rational functions on the Picard image, not just an
algorithm on geometric points.  Indeed on $H^0(C,L)$ the leading
coefficient $P$ splits the rank-two bundle into
$\operatorname{im}P\oplus\ker P$.
The maps $PM_0(1-P)$ and $PM_{n-1}(1-P)$ between its two line
bundles have scalar ratio $-t^{r-1}x$, and
$\operatorname{tr}((1-P)M_0)=t^{r-1}x(y-1)$.
These are precisely the family-invariant ratios in
\eqref{spec:state-inverse}.  As $x,y$ generate $K(X)$, they prove
\begin{equation}
K(\alpha(X))\xrightarrow{\alpha^*}K(X)
\quad\text{is an isomorphism}.
\label{spec:function-field-inverse}
\end{equation}
In particular $\alpha$ is nonconstant and birational onto its image,
with total degree one.  This excludes a hidden purely inseparable
degree, which geometric generic injectivity alone would not exclude.

\emph{The fixed Picard difference.}
Cyclically rotating the original product gives
\[
M(sz)A(z)=A(z)M(z),
\]
since $s^r=1$.
Hence $A(z)$ induces a rational homomorphism
$a_L:L\dashrightarrow\sigma^*L$.
It is invertible away from $z=0,\infty$, because $\det A=z^3$.
Its full cyclic composite is multiplication by the spectral
coordinate:
\begin{equation}
(\sigma^{r-1})^*a_L\circ\cdots\circ\sigma^*a_L\circ a_L
=M(z)|_L=\lambda.
\label{spec:cyclic-composite}
\end{equation}
Thus this map is not itself an ordinary $G$-linearization of $L$.
The two spectral charts give
\[
\operatorname{div}_C(\lambda)=3rP_0-2rQ_2-rQ_1.
\]
At $P_0$, the other root is a unit tending to $T$, and the product
of roots is $\varepsilon z^{3r}$, giving order $3r$; at $P_T$ the
root is a unit.  At $Q_2$ the coordinate $\mu$ tends to $1$, giving
pole order $2r$ for $\lambda$; at $Q_1$, $\mu$ has order $r$,
giving pole order $r$.  The divisor of $a_L$ is supported at these
four fixed points.  Taking orders in \eqref{spec:cyclic-composite}
therefore gives the integer divisor identity
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{div}(a_L)=D_A:=3P_0-2Q_2-Q_1,
\qquad \sigma^*L\otimes L^{-1}\simeq\mathcal O_C(D_A).
\label{spec:fixed-difference}
\end{equation}
This is independent of the state.  By the extension of $\alpha$
and separation of the Picard functor it holds for every point of
$X$, not just $X^\circ$.  Consequently, for any geometric base
point $P_*$, all differences
$\alpha(P)\otimes\alpha(P_*)^{-1}$ lie in
\[
B_C=\ker(\sigma^*-1:\operatorname{Pic}^0(C)\to
                              \operatorname{Pic}^0(C)).
\]

\emph{The invariant component and the absence of a kernel.}
Write $J_E=\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)$ and $J_C=\operatorname{Pic}^0(C)$.
The norm gives $\operatorname{Nm}_{\pi}\pi^*=[r]$.  Thus the kernel
of $\pi^*:J_E\to J_C$ is a subgroup scheme of the finite \'{e}tale
group $J_E[r]$, since $r$ is invertible.  It suffices to exclude
nontrivial geometric points.  Over an algebraic closure of $K$,
suppose $N\in J_E$ has trivial pullback.  The pullback carries its
natural $G$-action.  In a trivialization, this action differs from
function pullback by constants, since the global units on the
projective integral curve $C$ are constant.  On the fiber at the
full-group fixed point $P_0$, the natural action is the identity.
Those constants must all be one.  The resulting nowhere-zero
section is $G$-invariant, and the quotient and projection formula
give
\[
H^0(C,\pi^*N)^G=H^0(E,N).
\]
It descends to a nowhere-zero section of $N$: a zero would pull
back to a zero.  Thus $N$ is trivial.  The whole scheme-theoretic
kernel is trivial, and the proper group homomorphism $\pi^*$ is a
closed immersion.  Let $H_C=\pi^*J_E$.

The inclusion $H_C\subset B_C$ has dimension one.  Finite-map
cohomology and the averaging projector $r^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}g$
give
\[
\operatorname{Lie}(B_C)=H^1(C,\mathcal O_C)^G
                       =H^1(E,\mathcal O_E),
\qquad \dim\operatorname{Lie}(B_C)=1.
\]
Averaging is an exact direct-summand projector, so taking these
invariants commutes with cohomology.  The local dimension of $B_C$
at the identity is at least one because it contains $H_C$, while
its tangent dimension is one.  Its local ring is therefore regular;
translation gives smoothness of its identity component.
The closed connected subgroup $H_C$ of the same dimension is
that component:
\begin{equation}
B_C^0=H_C\simeq J_E.
\label{spec:invariant-component}
\end{equation}
For $r=1$ the reasoning reduces to $C=E$ and $B_C=J_C$.

\emph{The full coset and descent over the original field.}
After choosing $P_*$ geometrically, the difference morphism from
$X$ has connected image containing the identity, so it lies in
$B_C^0=H_C$.  It is nonconstant by
\eqref{spec:function-field-inverse}.  Its projective image is
therefore the whole one-dimensional group $H_C$.
Hence $\alpha(X)$ is a complete geometric translate of $H_C$.
It is smooth, and \eqref{spec:function-field-inverse} makes the
map birational to it.  A birational morphism between smooth
projective curves is an isomorphism, proving the asserted image
description without a residual isogeny.

All the original surfaces, matrices, two spectral charts, and
morphisms are defined over $K=k(c)$.  If $k$ is not algebraically
closed, their geometric prerequisites are checked after the
faithfully flat field extension $k(c)\subset\bar k(c)$ by the same
equations and Theorem~\ref{geom:pencil}.  No separability of this
constant-field extension is required.  Smoothness, geometric
integrality, and the genus calculations descend; thus the module
construction and the rational inverse were indeed over $K$.
Let $Y=\alpha(X)$ with its $K$-structure.  The $K$-defined action
of $J_E$ on $\operatorname{Pic}^n(C)$ by tensoring with $\pi^*N$
preserves $Y$, as can be checked after faithful flat geometric
extension where it is a coset.  The action map
\[
J_E\times Y\longrightarrow Y\times Y,
\qquad(N,L)\longmapsto(L,L\otimes\pi^*N)
\]
is an isomorphism after that extension, hence is an isomorphism
over $K$.  The closed immersion $\alpha:X\to Y$ is likewise an
isomorphism over $K$.  This gives $X$ its $J_E$-torsor structure,
and the rational origin $O\in E(K)$ identifies $E$ with $J_E$.
The torsor group of a genus-one curve is its Jacobian: with a
geometric base point the identification is
$P\mapsto\mathcal O_X(P-P_*)$, and changing the base point changes
only a translation, not this group identification.  It therefore
descends to $K$, giving $\operatorname{Jac}(X)\simeq E$.
The argument does not descend a geometric choice of $P_*$ and does
not assert that the torsor is trivial over $K$.
\end{proof}
