## Claim

Let $r\ge3$, let $2\le m<n$ with $\gcd(m,n)=1$, and put
$$
K=\mathbb C(c_1,\ldots,c_{r-1}),\qquad c_r=0,\qquad
h(x)=\prod_{i=1}^r(x+c_i),
$$
where $c_1,\ldots,c_{r-1}$ are algebraically independent. On
$$D_h=\operatorname{Spec}K[Q,P,x]/(QP-h(x))$$
define
$$
\sigma_m(Q,P,x)=
\left(Q,P+\frac{h(x+mQ^m)-h(x)}Q,x+mQ^m\right),
$$
$$
\tau_n(Q,P,x)=
\left(Q+\frac{h(x+nP^n)-h(x)}P,P,x+nP^n\right),
\qquad\phi=\tau_n\sigma_m.
$$
Then for every integer $N\ge1$,
$$
\boxed{C_{\operatorname{Aut}_K(D_h)}(\phi^N)=\langle\phi\rangle.}
\tag{1}
$$
The same equality holds after extension to $\overline K$, with
$\operatorname{Aut}_{\overline K}(D_{h,\overline K})$ on the left.
These are full algebraic surface-automorphism groups, not merely the
subgroup generated by the two displayed shears and not merely their
symplectic subgroup.

In particular, passing to an iterate creates no new fixed-base quotient
centralizer, and $\phi$ has no algebraic root of order greater than one.
More precisely, if $\psi^\ell=\phi^j$ with $\ell\ge1$ and $j\ne0$, then
$\ell$ divides $j$ and $\psi=\phi^{j/\ell}$.

## Status

**PROVABLE AS STATED for (1), with the proof below.**

The full-phase-space centralizer follows by combining (1) with the
separate ambient lemma package explicitly identified in Section 8.
That combination is not being represented as a theorem already supplied

There is a useful near-counterexample: extra finite **conformally

symplectic conjecture, but show why semilinear and Poisson conditions must
not be omitted.

## Assumptions and notation

- All centralizers in (1) fix the indicated coefficient field pointwise.
  A map acting nontrivially on the $c_i$ is semilinear and is not covered
  by that notation.
- The generic polynomial $h$ has distinct roots and no nonidentity affine
  automorphism of its root set. Both facts are checked below.
- Composition is composition of maps, with the rightmost map applied
  first. The two positive shear increments are exactly $mQ^m$ and
  $nP^n$, as induced by the original Hamiltonians $Q^m,P^n$.
- For a field $k$, write $s_A$ for the $Q$-shear whose $x$ increment is
  $A(Q)\in Qk[Q]$, and $t_B$ for the $P$-shear whose increment is
  $B(P)\in Pk[P]$. The complementary coordinate is determined by
  $QP=h(x)$, as in the displayed formulas.
- Put $U_Q=\{s_A\}$, $U_P=\{t_B\}$,
  $H_a(Q,P,x)=(aQ,a^{-1}P,x)$, and $I(Q,P,x)=(P,Q,x)$.

## Proof strategy and dependency map

1. Check that the generic root configuration has no affine symmetry.
2. Use the established Danielewski automorphism-group decomposition over
   $\mathbb C$.
3. Build its free-product tree. The two-syllable word $\phi$ translates
   an explicit axis by two edges.
4. Compute the pointwise-axis stabilizer directly: its scalars satisfy
   $a^m=a^n=1$, so it is trivial.
5. A possible translation by one edge would square to $\phi$. Its two
   shear exponents would both be $n$, contradicting $m\ne n$.
6. Transport any finite set of coefficients into $\mathbb C$ to apply
   the result to $K$ and $\overline K$ without silently enlarging the
   scope of a complex-field citation.
7. Keep semilinear base actions and the ambient lifting kernel separate.

## Proof

### 1. The generic root configuration has no affine symmetry

Write the roots as $t_i=-c_i$ for $i<r$ and $t_r=0$. They are distinct.
If an affine map $z\mapsto az+b$ permutes them by a permutation $\pi$,
its values at two distinct roots determine $a,b$ as rational functions
of the $t_i$. Thus allowing $a,b\in\overline K$ gives no additional
possibilities beyond $a,b\in K$.

For a fixed permutation $\pi$, the condition that this affine map sends
every $t_i$ to $t_{\pi(i)}$ is a finite list of polynomial identities in
the $t_i$, after multiplication by the nonzero difference used to solve
for $a$. A nonidentity permutation cannot satisfy these identities at
the generic point. Indeed, specialize the distinct roots to
$$\{0,1,2,\ldots,r-2,r\}.$$
An affine map preserving this real set has real coefficients, since two
distinct real points have distinct real images. A finite permutation
forces a finite-order affine map. A nonidentity real finite-order affine
map is a reflection. Preservation of the minimum and maximum would make
it $z\mapsto r-z$, but this sends $1$ to the absent point $r-1$.
There is no such reflection. The case $r=3$ is the set $\{0,1,3\}$.

Consequently no nonidentity permutation satisfies the generic
identities. The only affine root symmetry is the identity.

### 2. The precise established group input

For a complex polynomial $h$ of degree at least three with simple roots
and no nonidentity affine root symmetry, the algebraic automorphism group
has the decomposition
$$
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathbb C}(D_h)
=(U_Q*U_P)\rtimes\bigl(\mathbb C^*\rtimes\langle I\rangle\bigr).
\tag{2}
$$
Here the two shear groups form a free product, the multiplicative factor
consists of the $H_a$, and $IH_aI=H_{a^{-1}}$. This is the input of
Kutzschebauch--Leuenberger, Theorem 2.7, whose proof uses the generator
theorem and a strict-degree normal-form argument. It is not a new result
of this check. The published source is *Ann. Sc. Norm. Super. Pisa*,
XV (2016), 183--207, Theorem 2.7 and proof on pp.189--190.
[Published primary text](https://journals.sns.it/index.php/annaliscienze/article/download/420/410/639);
[author arXiv version, Section 2.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1311.1075).

The actions needed here are direct substitutions:
$$
H_as_AH_a^{-1}=s_{A(a^{-1}Q)},\qquad
H_at_BH_a^{-1}=t_{B(aP)},\qquad Is_AI=t_{A(P)}.
\tag{3}
$$
In particular, torus conjugation preserves the exponent of each monomial
shear. No dynamical-degree inference is used in (2) or (3).

### 3. The tree and the axis of the two-syllable word

For this section work over $\mathbb C$, and put
$$W=U_Q*U_P,\qquad E=\mathbb C^*\rtimes\langle I\rangle.$$
The free-product tree has vertices the left cosets $W/U_Q$ and $W/U_P$.
For every $w\in W$ an edge labeled $w$ joins $wU_Q$ to $wU_P$.
It is a tree: connectedness follows by reading a word one syllable at a
time, while a nonbacktracking cycle would give a nonempty reduced word
equal to the identity, contradicting the free-product normal form.

The group $W$ acts by left multiplication. The factor $E$ acts by its
conjugation action on $W$; $H_a$ preserves the two vertex types, and $I$
exchanges them. Thus (2) acts on this tree, allowing $I$ to invert an
edge. Let $e$ be the edge labeled by the identity. Its setwise stabilizer
in the full group is $E$, and its pointwise stabilizer is exactly
$\{H_a\}$. These assertions follow from the unique expression $we_0$
with $w\in W,e_0\in E$ and the stated action on vertex types.

Set
$$a_0=\sigma_m\in U_Q\setminus\{1\},\qquad
b_0=\tau_n\in U_P\setminus\{1\},\qquad\phi=b_0a_0.$$
The following indexed edges form a nonbacktracking bi-infinite path:
$$
e_{2j}=\phi^j e,\qquad e_{2j+1}=\phi^j b_0e,
\qquad j\in\mathbb Z.
\tag{4}
$$
Adjacent edges share alternately a $U_P$-type and a $U_Q$-type vertex;
the nontrivial alternating syllables prevent backtracking by normal form.
Denote this line by $\mathcal A$. The element $\phi$ translates it by
two edges, and $\phi^N$ translates it by $2N$.

This is the unique translation axis of $\phi^N$. For completeness, the
geodesic from a vertex off $\mathcal A$ to its image consists of the
segment to its closest vertex on $\mathcal A$, the translated segment
along $\mathcal A$, and the translated off-axis segment. Its length is
$2N+2\operatorname{dist}(v,\mathcal A)$. Thus $\mathcal A$ is precisely
the minimal-displacement line.

An element commuting with $\phi^N$ preserves this line. Its restriction
cannot be a reflection, since a reflection conjugates a nonzero
translation to its inverse. It therefore translates the indexed edges
by an integer, giving a homomorphism
$$
\ell:C_{\operatorname{Aut}(D_h)}(\phi^N)\longrightarrow\mathbb Z,
\qquad\ell(\phi)=2.
\tag{5}
$$

### 4. The pointwise-axis kernel is trivial, including all root scalars

Let $g$ lie in the kernel of (5). It fixes $e$ pointwise, so $g=H_a$.
It also fixes $b_0e$, giving $b_0^{-1}H_ab_0\in E$. Since $W\cap E=1$,
the normal form of this element forces
$$H_ab_0H_a^{-1}=b_0.$$
Equation (3), applied to the increment $nP^n$, gives $a^n=1$.

The element also fixes $b_0a_0e$. Since it now commutes with $b_0$,
the same normal-form argument gives
$$H_aa_0H_a^{-1}=a_0,$$
and hence $a^m=1$. The coprimality assumption forces $a=1$.
Thus (5) is injective. This calculation excludes the full finite scalar
kernel, not just its identity component.

### 5. A half-step translation, and hence a proper root, is impossible

The image of (5) is a subgroup of $\mathbb Z$ containing $2$, so it is
either $2\mathbb Z$ or $\mathbb Z$. Suppose it were $\mathbb Z$, and
choose $g$ with $\ell(g)=1$. Injectivity and $\ell(g^2)=\ell(\phi)=2$
give
$$g^2=\phi.$$
By (4), $ge=b_0e$, so $g=b_0e_0$ for some $e_0\in E$.
Translation by one edge exchanges the two vertex types; therefore
$e_0=H_aI$ for some $a\ne0$. Since $(H_aI)^2=1$, (3) gives
$$
g^2=b_0\bigl(H_aI\,b_0\,IH_a^{-1}\bigr)
    =b_0s_{n a^{-n}Q^n}.
$$
Equality with $\phi=b_0s_{mQ^m}$ would imply
$$n a^{-n}Q^n=mQ^m.$$
This contradicts $m\ne n$. Hence the image of (5) is $2\mathbb Z$.
Its injectivity now gives $C(\phi^N)=\langle\phi\rangle$ for every
$N\ge1$ over $\mathbb C$.

If $\psi^\ell=\phi^j$ with $j\ne0$, then $\psi$ commutes with that
power, so $\psi=\phi^k$. The infinite order of the translating element
gives $\ell k=j$. This proves the root statement without assuming in
advance that a root belongs to the subgroup generated by the two shears.

### 6. The generic coefficient field and its algebraic closure

The cited theorem was stated over $\mathbb C$. Its use for the generic
coefficient field can be justified without asserting an uncited
field-general version.

Take any $G\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\overline K}(D_{h,\overline K})$
commuting with $\phi^N$. Polynomial representatives for $G,G^{-1}$ and
$h$ involve finitely many coefficients. Let $E_0\subset\overline K$ be
the field generated over $\mathbb Q$ by those coefficients and the $c_i$.
It is a finitely generated characteristic-zero field and admits an
embedding $\iota:E_0\hookrightarrow\mathbb C$: send a transcendence
basis to algebraically independent complex numbers and extend over its
finite algebraic extension inside the algebraically closed field
$\mathbb C$. This embedding need not fix the original complex constants.
No such requirement is needed for the algebraic identities being tested.

The images of the roots are still distinct and have no affine symmetry.
Indeed, an affine permutation of a finite root set is determined by two
roots, and its finitely many defining identities can be pulled back
through the injective field map. It would contradict Section 1.

Applying $\iota$ to the coefficients therefore yields a complex
automorphism $G_\iota$ commuting with $\phi_\iota^N$ on a surface to
which Sections 2--5 apply. Thus $G_\iota=\phi_\iota^k$ for an integer
$k$. This equality pulls back to $G=\phi^k$: the coordinate ring has the
unique normal form consisting of polynomials in $x$, positive powers of
$Q$ times polynomials in $x$, and positive powers of $P$ times
polynomials in $x$. Injectivity of $\iota$ detects equality of every
coefficient in that normal form.

This proves (1) over $\overline K$, and consequently over $K$.
$\square$

## 7. Explicit finite-symmetry and semilinear checks

### 7.1 Symplectic diagonal maps add nothing beyond the product-one torus

In the full phase space let
$$D_{\boldsymbol a}(q_i,p_i)=(a_iq_i,a_i^{-1}p_i),
\qquad A=\prod_i a_i.$$
Direct conjugation gives
$$
D_{\boldsymbol a}S_{Q^m}D_{\boldsymbol a}^{-1}
=S_{A^{-m}Q^m},\qquad
D_{\boldsymbol a}T_{P^n}D_{\boldsymbol a}^{-1}
=T_{A^nP^n}.
$$
The induced quotient map is $H_A$. If the diagonal map commutes with
$F^N$, its quotient commutes with $\phi^N$. Equivalently, comparison of
the two alternating reduced words for the $N$-th powers forces
$A^m=A^n=1$. Thus $A=1$. Conversely $A=1$ makes it commute with each
shear. There is no missing root-unit component when $m,n$ are coprime.

### 7.2 Non-symplectic finite scalings really exist

Put
$$d=rmn-m-n>0.$$
For $t\in\mathbb C^*$ with $t^d=1$, choose constants $a_i$ with
$\prod_i a_i=t^n$, set $s=t^{mn}$, and define
$$D_t(q_i,p_i)=(a_iq_i,(s/a_i)p_i).$$
Then
$$
A=\prod_i a_i=t^n,\qquad
B=\prod_i(s/a_i)=t^{rmn-n}=t^m,
\qquad A^m=B^n=s.
$$
For a diagonal map with $a_ib_i=s$, conjugation multiplies the first
shear increment by $sA^{-m}$ and the second by $sB^{-n}$. Both factors
are one here. Thus $D_t$ commutes separately with $S_{Q^m}$ and
$T_{P^n}$, and hence with every $F^N$.

However,
$$D_t^*\omega=s\omega.$$
Since
$$\gcd(d,mn)=\gcd(m+n,mn)=1,$$
the conditions $t^d=1$ and $s=t^{mn}=1$ force $t=1$.
Every nontrivial such scaling is therefore **not** symplectic.

For the suggested test case $(r,m,n)=(3,2,3)$, take a nontrivial
thirteenth root $t$. One example is
$$
(q_1,q_2,q_3,p_1,p_2,p_3)
\longmapsto
(t^3q_1,q_2,q_3,t^3p_1,t^6p_2,t^6p_3).
$$
It centralizes the map but multiplies $\omega$ by $t^6$.
This is a counterexample to omitting the word “symplectic”, not to the
actual candidate. No finite computation is a proof dependency.

### 7.3 Base permutations are semilinear, not fixed-base exceptions

A simultaneous index permutation induces on the quotient coordinate
algebra
$$
c_i\longmapsto c_{\sigma(i)}-c_{\sigma(r)},\qquad
x\longmapsto x+c_{\sigma(r)},\qquad Q\longmapsto Q,\quad P\longmapsto P.
$$
It commutes with the induced dynamics because it is induced by the
simultaneous permutation of the original shears. It generally does not
fix $K$ pointwise and therefore does not contradict (1).

The scalings in Section 7.2 also change the base, by $c_i\mapsto sc_i$.
This illustrates why a fixed-$K$ centralizer calculation by itself cannot
classify all base-changing automorphisms. In the symplectic ambient
problem the separate normalization argument is what reduces the base
action to simultaneous permutations.

### 7.4 Why the exponent assumptions matter to this calculation

If $\gcd(m,n)>1$, every $H_a$ with
$a^{\gcd(m,n)}=1$ fixes the full axis and commutes with both shears.
The claimed trivial scalar kernel would then be false.

If the distinct-exponent condition is dropped and $m=n$, then
$$(\tau_m I)^2=\tau_m\sigma_m.$$
Thus a square root exists in the full surface automorphism group.
The involution $I$ reverses the usual surface symplectic form, so this
example does not by itself supply a symplectic square root. Its role is
to check exactly where the full-group root exclusion uses $m\ne n$.
No reversor classification is claimed.
