\section{The boundedness and compactness wall}
\label{sec:bounded}

The absolute row sums simultaneously give a Schur bound and a compactness
criterion.  For $\sigma>0$, let
\begin{equation}
  R_m=\sum_{n\ge1}\abs{h_s(m,n)}.
  \label{eq:row-sum}
\end{equation}
Let $A=A(m)$ be the least integer for which $2^A>m$.  The neighbors of $m$
are exactly $n_j=2^{A+j}-m$, $j\ge0$.  The first neighbor is a positive
integer, while for $j\ge1$, $n_j\ge2^{A+j-1}$.  Consequently the row sum
satisfies
\begin{align}
 R_m
 &=m^{-\sigma/2}\sum_{j\ge0}(2^{A+j}-m)^{-\sigma/2} \notag\\
 &\le m^{-\sigma/2}
   +\frac{m^{-\sigma/2}2^{-A\sigma/2}}
          {1-2^{-\sigma/2}}
 \le m^{-\sigma/2}+C_\sigma m^{-\sigma}.
 \label{eq:row-decay}
\end{align}
In particular, $\sup_mR_m<\infty$ and $R_m\to0$.

\begin{theorem}[Sharp boundedness and compactness]
\label{thm:bounded}
The coefficient array $h_s$ admits a bounded operator realization on
$\ell^2(\N)$ if and only if $\sigma>0$.  Whenever it does, the realization
$H_s$ is compact.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $\sigma>0$, the modulus array is symmetric, so the Schur test and
\cref{eq:row-decay} give a bounded realization.  To prove compactness, fix
$\varepsilon>0$.  Choose $M$ so that $R_m<\varepsilon$ for $m>M$.  Each of
the finitely many rows $m\le M$ is absolutely summable, so some $N\ge M$
satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{n>N}\abs{h_s(m,n)}<\varepsilon,
  \qquad 1\le m\le M.
\end{equation*}
Every absolute row sum of $H_s-P_NH_sP_N$ is then below $\varepsilon$:
for $m\le M$ it is the selected row tail, and for $m>M$ it is at most
$R_m$.  The same holds for columns by symmetry of the modulus array.  A
second Schur estimate yields
$\norm{H_s-P_NH_sP_N}\le\varepsilon$.  Hence finite-rank compressions
converge in norm.

Suppose now that $\sigma\le0$ and that a bounded operator had the declared
matrix coefficients.  In its row indexed by $1$, the entries at
$n=2^a-1$, $a\ge1$, have squared moduli $(2^a-1)^{-\sigma}$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{a\ge1}\abs{h_s(1,2^a-1)}^2
  =\sum_{a\ge1}(2^a-1)^{-\sigma}=\infty.
  \label{eq:row-one-obstruction}
\end{equation}
But a row of a bounded operator is the coordinate sequence of its adjoint
applied to a basis vector and must lie in $\ell^2$.  This contradiction
excludes every bounded realization on $\sigma\le0$.
\end{proof}

The proof is genuinely infinite at the boundary: no finite singular-value
calculation can witness the divergent row in
\cref{eq:row-one-obstruction}.
