\begin{abstract}
Let $R$ be a finite commutative chain ring with maximal ideal
$\mathfrak m=(\pi)$, residue-field size $q$, and
$\mathfrak m^{a+1}=0\ne\mathfrak m^a$.  We study the nearest-neighbor shift
whose allowed pairs satisfy
$xy\in\Soc(R)\setminus\{0\}$.  Valuation turns this condition into the exact
boundary equation $\val(x)+\val(y)=a$.  The adjacency graph therefore splits
into complete bipartite components and, when $a$ is even, one full-shift
component.  If
\[
 \rho=(q-1)q^{a/2},
\]
then every component has Perron value $\rho$, the adjacency rank is $a+1$,
and the topological entropy is $\log\rho$.  There are
$\lfloor a/2\rfloor+1$ ergodic maximal-entropy measures.  For odd $a$ all
of them have period two; for even $a$ exactly one is mixing.  We compute
every fixed-point count and the full dynamical zeta function.  The first four
fixed-point counts recover $(q,a)$, whereas the shift forgets all finer ring
structure: any two chain rings with the same $(q,a)$ give one-block conjugate
shifts.  The relation is a union of nonzero-socle product fibres, rather than
the classical zero-product graph or a single fixed-product matrix; those
ownership distinctions are kept explicit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Finite commutative chain rings have a single ideal filtration, but rings
with the same residue-field size and filtration length need not be
isomorphic.  Their structure and classification are classical
\cite{ClarkLiang1973,Hou2001}.  This makes them a useful test for a symbolic
question: which parts of a ring survive after multiplication is reduced to a
nearest-neighbor constraint?

Fix a chain ring $R$ and allow $x$ to be followed by $y$ precisely when
their product is a nonzero element of the socle.  The answer is unusually
rigid at the dynamical level and unusually coarse at the ring level.  The
ring valuation decomposes the shift into finitely many irreducible components
of exactly equal entropy.  The parity of the filtration length decides
whether one of these components is mixing.  At the same time, the entire
adjacency matrix has only $a+1$ nonzero eigenvalues, regardless of the size
$q^{a+1}-1$ of its alphabet.

There is an important ownership boundary.  Anderson and Livingston's
zero-divisor graph joins distinct nonzero zero divisors when their product is
zero \cite{AndersonLivingston1999}.  Spectra and ranks of that graph over
finite chain rings and principal ideal rings have already been studied by
Rattanakangwanwong and Meemark \cite{RattanakangwanwongMeemark2022}.  Our
graph instead includes units, retains loops, and selects the single valuation
boundary on which the product is in $\Soc(R)\setminus\{0\}$.  More directly,
Dol\v{z}an defines the fixed-product matrix
$A_u(R)=(\mathbf 1_{\{xy=u\}})_{x,y\in R}$ and determines characteristic
polynomials in broad finite-local-ring regimes \cite{Dolzan2026}.  After
deleting its zero row and column, our adjacency matrix is exactly
\[
 A_R=\sum_{u\in\Soc(R)\setminus\{0\}}A_u(R).
\]
Thus fixed-product matrices and their spectral study are prior art.  We claim
only the residual union-of-socle-values relation and its symbolic consequences;
we do not claim the zero-divisor graph, either prior spectral framework, the
finite-type zeta determinant \cite{BowenLanford1970}, or the Parry construction
\cite{Parry1964}.

The residual calculation has four parts.  First, valuation gives an exact
complete-bipartite normal form and the adjacency rank.  Second, the normal
form gives the complete maximal-measure simplex and an odd/even mixing
transition.  Third, it gives all periodic counts and the rational zeta
function.  Finally, four fixed-point counts recover the two surviving
parameters, while an explicit layerwise conjugacy proves that no finer ring
invariant survives.

\section{The valuation boundary}
\label{sec:valuation}

Let $R$ be a finite commutative chain ring.  Its unique maximal ideal is
principal, say $\mathfrak m=(\pi)$, and for some $a\geq1$ its ideal chain is
\[
 R=\mathfrak m^0\supsetneq\mathfrak m^1\supsetneq\cdots
 \supsetneq\mathfrak m^a\supsetneq\mathfrak m^{a+1}=0.
\]
We call $a+1$ the length and write $|R/\mathfrak m|=q$.  For $x\ne0$ define
\[
 \val(x)=i\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 x\in\mathfrak m^i\setminus\mathfrak m^{i+1},
 \qquad 0\leq i\leq a,
\]
and put $V_i=\{x\in R:\val(x)=i\}$.  We use $R^\bullet=R\setminus\{0\}$.
Here $\Soc(R)=\operatorname{Ann}_R(\mathfrak m)$ is the socle of the regular
$R$-module.

\begin{lemma}[Layer arithmetic]
\label{lem:valuation}
For $0\leq i\leq a$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
 |\mathfrak m^i|=q^{a+1-i},
 \qquad
 w_i:=|V_i|=(q-1)q^{a-i}.
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\[
 \Soc(R)=\mathfrak m^a,
 \qquad
 xy\in\Soc(R)\setminus\{0\}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \val(x)+\val(y)=a.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Multiplication by $\pi^i$ identifies
$R/\mathfrak m$ with $\mathfrak m^i/\mathfrak m^{i+1}$ as additive groups.
Every successive quotient therefore has $q$ elements.  Counting upward from
$\mathfrak m^{a+1}=0$ gives \eqref{eq:weights}.

Every nonzero $x\in V_i$ has the form $x=\pi^i u$ for a unit $u$.  Hence, if
$i+j\leq a$, the product of elements in $V_i$ and $V_j$ lies in
$V_{i+j}$; if $i+j\geq a+1$, it is zero.  Also
$\mathfrak m\mathfrak m^a=0$, whereas an element in $V_i$ with $i<a$ is not
annihilated by $\pi$.  Thus $\Soc(R)=\mathfrak m^a$, and the final
equivalence follows.
\end{proof}

Define the socle-product shift
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shift}
 X_R=\left\{x\in(R^\bullet)^\Z:
 x_nx_{n+1}\in\Soc(R)\setminus\{0\}
 \text{ for every }n\in\Z\right\},
\end{equation}
with the left shift $\sigma$.  Let $A_R$ be its zero-one adjacency matrix,
indexed by $R^\bullet$.  The same shift results if the alphabet is declared
to be all of $R$, because zero has no allowed predecessor or successor.

\section{Normal form, rank, and entropy}
\label{sec:normal}

Write $J_{r,s}$ for the $r\times s$ all-ones matrix and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho}
 N=q^{a+1}-1,
 \qquad
 \rho=(q-1)q^{a/2}.
\end{equation}
Although $\rho$ can be irrational when $a$ is odd, its square
$\rho^2=(q-1)^2q^a$ is always an integer.

\begin{theorem}[Complete-bipartite normal form]
\label{thm:normal}
After the alphabet is ordered by valuation, $A_R$ is the direct sum, over
$0\leq i<a-i\leq a$, of the blocks
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bipartite-block}
 B_i=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0&J_{w_i,w_{a-i}}\\
 J_{w_{a-i},w_i}&0
 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and, when $a=2b$ is even, the additional block $J_{w_b,w_b}$.
Consequently
\[
 \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb R}A_R=a+1,
 \qquad
 \htop(X_R)=\log\rho.
\]
More precisely, if $a=2b$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:char-even}
 \det(tI-A_R)=
 t^{N-a-1}(t-\rho)^{b+1}(t+\rho)^b,
\end{equation}
whereas, if $a=2b+1$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:char-odd}
 \det(tI-A_R)=
 t^{N-a-1}(t^2-\rho^2)^{b+1}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:valuation}, every vertex of $V_i$ is joined to every vertex of
$V_{a-i}$ and to no other vertex.  This gives the displayed direct sum.
For $i<a-i$, the block $B_i$ has rank two and its only nonzero eigenvalues
are
\[
 \pm\sqrt{w_iw_{a-i}}=\pm(q-1)q^{a/2}=\pm\rho.
\]
If $a=2b$, the central all-ones block has rank one and sole nonzero
eigenvalue $w_b=(q-1)q^b=\rho$.  Summing the ranks and multiplicities gives
\eqref{eq:char-even}--\eqref{eq:char-odd}.  Every irreducible block has
spectral radius $\rho$, so the entropy of their finite disjoint union is
$\log\rho$.
\end{proof}

The compression can also be recorded without the full matrix.  On functions
constant on valuation layers, $A_R$ acts through the $(a+1)\times(a+1)$
matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quotient}
 Q_{ij}=w_j\,\mathbf 1_{\{i+j=a\}}.
\end{equation}
All other directions are killed.  The matrix $Q$ is anti-diagonal with no
zero entry, so it is nonsingular; this gives a second proof of the exact
rank $a+1$.

\section{Equal-entropy components and maximal measures}
\label{sec:mme}

For $i<a-i$, let $C_i$ be the shift supported on
$V_i\cup V_{a-i}$.  It is an irreducible complete-bipartite shift of exact
period two.  When $a=2b$, let $C_b$ be the full shift on $V_b$; it is mixing.
These are precisely the irreducible components of $X_R$.

\begin{theorem}[Parity transition and the MME simplex]
\label{thm:mme}
The shift $X_R$ has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mme-count}
 c(a)=\left\lfloor\frac a2\right\rfloor+1
\end{equation}
ergodic measures of maximal entropy, one on each irreducible component.
Their convex hull is the full simplex of maximal-entropy measures.

If $a$ is odd, every ergodic maximal-entropy measure has period two and none
is mixing.  If $a$ is even, exactly one ergodic maximal-entropy measure is
mixing: the uniform Bernoulli measure on the central full shift $C_{a/2}$.
The other ergodic maximal-entropy measures still have period two.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each complete-bipartite component has Perron value
$\sqrt{w_iw_{a-i}}=\rho$, and the central full shift, when present, has
Perron value $w_{a/2}=\rho$.  Thus every component has entropy
$\log\rho$.  An irreducible finite-type shift has a unique Parry measure
\cite{Parry1964}; hence every component supplies one ergodic
maximal-entropy measure.  There are $c(a)$ components.  Since they are
pairwise disjoint invariant clopen sets, every invariant measure decomposes
over them, and entropy is the corresponding affine average.  This proves
the simplex statement.  The period and mixing assertions follow from the
complete-bipartite blocks and the looped all-ones central block.
\end{proof}

The component measures are explicit.  On $C_i$ with $i<a-i$, each layer has
stationary mass $1/2$; conditional on the current layer, the next symbol is
uniform on the opposite layer.  On $C_{a/2}$ the coordinates are independent
and uniform on $V_{a/2}$.  Thus the odd/even statement concerns the existence
of a mixing maximal component.  The full shift $X_R$ itself is reducible, and
hence not topologically transitive, for every $a\geq2$.  At the boundary case
$a=1$ there is just one complete-bipartite component, so $X_R$ is irreducible
of exact period two (and is still not mixing).

\section{All periods and the zeta function}
\label{sec:periods}

Put
\[
 F_n(R)=\#\Fix_{X_R}(\sigma^n)=\operatorname{tr}(A_R^n),
 \qquad n\geq1.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Complete periodic ledger]
\label{thm:periods}
If $a=2b$ is even, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-even}
 F_n(R)=\rho^n\bigl((b+1)+b(-1)^n\bigr)
 =
 \begin{cases}
  \rho^n,&n\text{ odd},\\
  (a+1)\rho^n,&n\text{ even}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
If $a=2b+1$ is odd, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-odd}
 F_n(R)=(b+1)\bigl(1+(-1)^n\bigr)\rho^n
 =
 \begin{cases}
  0,&n\text{ odd},\\
  (a+1)\rho^n,&n\text{ even}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The Artin--Mazur zeta function is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta}
 \zeta_{X_R}(z)
 =\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{F_n(R)}n z^n\right)
 =
 \begin{cases}
  \displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\rho z)(1-\rho^2z^2)^b},&a=2b,\\[8pt]
  \displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\rho^2z^2)^{b+1}},&a=2b+1.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Sum the $n$th powers of the nonzero eigenvalues in
\eqref{eq:char-even} and \eqref{eq:char-odd}.  This gives
\eqref{eq:fixed-even} and \eqref{eq:fixed-odd}.  The Bowen--Lanford
determinant identity $\zeta_{X_R}(z)=\det(I-zA_R)^{-1}$
\cite{BowenLanford1970}, followed by the same factorization, gives
\eqref{eq:zeta}.
\end{proof}

For completeness, the number $E_n(R)$ of points of least period $n$ and the
number $O_n(R)$ of length-$n$ orbits are therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:least-period}
 E_n(R)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)F_d(R),
 \qquad
 O_n(R)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)F_d(R),
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the M\"obius function.  Thus \cref{thm:periods} determines
the complete orbit census, not only the fixed-point sequence.

\section{Ring collapse and four-period rigidity}
\label{sec:rigidity}

The normal form proves that the shift remembers less than its ring, while
the first four periods prove that it remembers exactly two parameters.

\begin{theorem}[Classification inside the family]
\label{thm:rigidity}
Let $R$ and $S$ be finite commutative chain rings, with parameter pairs
$(q_R,a_R)$ and $(q_S,a_S)$.  Then
\[
 X_R\text{ and }X_S\text{ are topologically conjugate}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 (q_R,a_R)=(q_S,a_S).
\]
When the parameters agree, the conjugacy can be chosen as a one-block code
induced by arbitrary bijections between corresponding valuation layers.

More explicitly, $(q,a)$ is recovered from $F_1,F_2,F_3,F_4$ as follows.
If $F_1>0$, then $a$ is even and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recover-even}
 a=\frac{F_2}{F_1^2}-1,
 \qquad
 (q-1)^2q^a=F_1^2.
\end{equation}
If $F_1=0$, then $a$ is odd and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recover-odd}
 a=\frac{F_2^2}{F_4}-1,
 \qquad
 (q-1)^2q^a=\frac{F_4}{F_2}.
\end{equation}
In the odd case $F_3=0$; in the even case $F_3=F_1^3$, providing internal
consistency checks.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $(q_R,a_R)=(q_S,a_S)=(q,a)$.  By
\eqref{eq:weights}, $|V_i(R)|=|V_i(S)|$ for every $i$.  Choose bijections
$\phi_i:V_i(R)\to V_i(S)$ and let $\phi$ be their union.  The valuation
boundary in \cref{lem:valuation} shows
\[
 xy\in\Soc(R)\setminus\{0\}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \phi(x)\phi(y)\in\Soc(S)\setminus\{0\}.
\]
Thus $\phi$ is an adjacency-graph isomorphism and its coordinatewise
extension is a one-block conjugacy.

Conversely, conjugacy preserves all fixed-point counts.  If $F_1>0$, the
even formula \eqref{eq:fixed-even} gives \eqref{eq:recover-even}.  If
$F_1=0$, \eqref{eq:fixed-odd} gives
$F_2=(a+1)\rho^2$ and $F_4=(a+1)\rho^4$, hence
\eqref{eq:recover-odd}.  For a fixed recovered $a$, the function
$(t-1)^2t^a$ is strictly increasing on integers $t\geq2$, so the displayed
value recovers $q$ uniquely.  Therefore conjugate members have the same
parameter pair.
\end{proof}

This collapse is genuine.  For every prime $p$ and $a\geq1$, the rings
\[
 \Z/p^{a+1}\Z
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \F_p[t]/(t^{a+1})
\]
have the same $(q,a)=(p,a)$ and therefore conjugate socle-product shifts,
but the rings are not isomorphic: their characteristics are $p^{a+1}$ and
$p$, respectively.

\section{Exact controls and ownership boundary}

The accompanying standard-library program checks the valuation layers,
component count, rank quotient, zeta determinant, fixed counts through period
ten, and four-period recovery for $q\in\{2,3,4,5\}$ and $1\leq a\leq5$.
It separately realizes the valuation law in both
$\Z/p^{a+1}\Z$ and $\F_p[t]/(t^{a+1})$ for $p=2,3,5$, verifies an explicit
layerwise collapse, and checks $\F_4[t]/(t^{a+1})$ for the nonprime residue
field.  These finite controls guard indexing and implementation; the proofs
above establish the full parameter range.

The zero-divisor firewall remains substantive.  In a chain ring the
classical zero-product condition is a valuation inequality at or beyond the
nilpotence threshold, while \eqref{eq:shift} selects the preceding equality
boundary and allows units and loops.  The cited zero-divisor graph literature
therefore owns the surrounding graph framework and the closest zero-product
spectral calculations.  Dol\v{z}an's fixed-product matrices are a still closer
owner: the displayed sum identity exhibits our matrix as their sum over the nonzero
socle.  That work does not, by itself, give the complete-bipartite union,
equal-entropy component simplex, periodic ledger, four-period recovery, or
layerwise conjugacy proved here.  A bounded search through 28 August 2026
found no exact collision for that owner-subtracted symbolic package.  This
dated statement is not an absolute priority claim; public posting, submission,
and specialist priority clearance remain on hold.
