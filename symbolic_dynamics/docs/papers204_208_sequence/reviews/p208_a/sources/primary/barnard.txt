                                                 Abstract. The pop-stack operator of a finite lattice L is the map pop↓L : L → L that sends each
                                                 element x ∈ L to the meet of {x} ∪ covL (x), where covL (x) is the set of elements covered by x in
                                                 L. We study several properties of the pop-stack operator of tors Λ, the lattice of torsion classes of
                                                 a τ -tilting finite algebra Λ over a field K. We describe the pop-stack operator in terms of certain
                                                 mutations of 2-term simple-minded collections. This allows us to describe preimages of a given
                                                 torsion class under the pop-stack operator.
                                                    We then specialize our attention to Cambrian lattices of a finite irreducible Coxeter group W .
                                                 Using tools from representation theory, we provide simple Coxeter-theoretic and lattice-theoretic
                                                 descriptions of the image of the pop-stack operator of a Cambrian lattice (which can be stated
                                                 without representation theory). When specialized to a bipartite Cambrian lattice of type A, this
                                                 result settles a conjecture of Choi and Sun. We also settle a related enumerative conjecture of
                                                 Defant and Williams. When L is an arbitrary lattice quotient of the weak order on W , we prove
                                                 that the maximum size of a forward orbit under the pop-stack operator of L is at most the Coxeter
                                                 number of W ; when L is a Cambrian lattice, we provide an explicit construction to show that this
                                                 maximum forward orbit size is actually equal to the Coxeter number.

images of pop↓ and pop↑ are also naturally in bijection with the set of facets of a certain simplicial
complex called the canonical join complex of L.

1.2. Lattices of torsion classes. In this article, we take a representation-theoretic perspective
and consider a finite-dimensional basic algebra Λ over a field K. The set of torsion classes (see
Section 3.2 for the definition) of finitely-generated (right) Λ-modules forms a complete lattice
[IRTT15] that we denote by tors Λ. Examples of lattices arising in this way include the weak order
and Cambrian lattices associated to any finite crystallographic Coxeter group [AHI+ , Miz14, IT09].
This paper continues a rich tradition of using lattices of torsion classes as a tool for proving new
results in both representation theory and in algebraic combinatorics; see, e.g., [DIR+ 23, Eno23,
IRRT18, RST21, TW19b, TW19a] and many others.
   We focus our attention on the case when Λ is τ -tilting finite (in the sense of [DIJ17]); this is
equivalent to assuming tors Λ is finite. Our goal is to study the image and the dynamical properties
of the pop-stack operator of tors Λ. While pop↓ has already appeared (sometimes under different
names) in the theory of lattices of torsion classes [AP22, BH, Eno23, ES22, Hana, Sak], it has
primarily been used as a tool rather than a dynamical operator worthy of its own investigation.
Let us remark that the article [BTZ21] initiated the study of dynamical combinatorics of torsion
classes by considering rowmotion.
   In Section 4, we consider certain pairs of sets of modules called 2-term simple-minded collections
and semibrick pairs. 2-term simple-minded collections were first introduced in the special case
when Λ is a symmetric algebra in [Ric02]; they are special cases of the more general simple-minded
collections introduced in [AN09] (under the name spherical collections). Semibrick pairs are a
generalization of 2-term simple-minded collections that were introduced in [HI21b] as a tool for
studying a generalization of the picture group of [ITW]. As we recall in Section 3.3, there is
a bijection between tors Λ (with Λ τ -tilting finite) and the set 2-smc Λ of 2-term simple-minded
collections [Asa20]. By the results of [BCZ19], this bijection encodes information about cover
relations and canonical join representations in tors Λ.

   The set 2-smc Λ also comes equipped with a set of mutation operators that categorify the mutation
theory of cluster algebras. See [BY13, Section 3.7] and [KY14, Section 7.2] (special cases also
appeared earlier in [KS, Section 8.1] and [KQ15, Section 3]). These mutation formulas can also
be applied to many of the more general semibrick pairs; see [BH22, HI21a]. Moreover, under
the bijection between 2-smc Λ and tors Λ, the mutation operators give a representation-theoretic
formula for how the corresponding 2-term simple-minded collection changes when one traverses a
cover relation in tors Λ.
   For each semibrick pair (X , Y), there is classically one mutation operator for each module in
X ∪Y. In the present paper, we more generally define a mutation operator for each nonempty subset
X ′ ⊆ X or Y ′ ⊆ Y. Our first main result (Theorem 5.1) states that, under the bijection between
tors Λ and 2-smc Λ, applying the pop-stack operator and its dual to a torsion class corresponds to
performing certain mutations on the associated 2-term simple-minded collections.
   In Section 5.2, we characterize the preimages of a prescribed torsion class under pop↓tors Λ and
pop↑tors Λ . As corollaries, we obtain descriptions of the 1-pop-stack sortable and the 2-pop-stack
sortable elements of tors Λ (Corollaries 5.4 and 5.5).

1.3. Cambrian lattices. Let W be a finite irreducible Coxeter group, and let Weak(W ) denote
the (right) weak order on W . Given a Coxeter element c of W , one can construct the c-Cambrian
lattice Cambc , which is the sublattice of Weak(W ) consisting of Reading’s c-sortable elements
[Rea06, Rea07]. (The c-Cambrian lattice is also a lattice quotient of Weak(W ).) When W is
crystallographic, we can realize Cambc as the lattice of torsion classes of the tensor algebra KQc
associated to the weighted Dynkin quiver associated to c [IT09].
   Two very special Cambrian lattices are the Tamari lattice and the type-B Tamari lattice; the
images of the pop-stack operators on these lattices were characterized and enumerated in [Hon22]
and [CS], respectively. On the other hand, analogous results for the Cambrian lattice Cambc× asso-
ciated to a type-A bipartite Coxeter element c× have remained elusive: a conjectural enumeration
of the image was formulated by Defant and Williams [DW23], while a characterization of the image
was conjectured by Choi and Sun [CS].
   It turns out that our representation-theoretic perspective is quite useful for understanding the
image of pop↓Cambc . In Theorem 5.10, we provide a representation-theoretic characterization of the
image of pop↓tors Λ whenever Λ is hereditary. When the Coxeter group W is crystallographic, the
tensor algebra KQc is hereditary, and we can reformulate our description of the image of the pop-
stack operator in purely lattice-theoretic and Coxeter-theoretic terms. We then check directly (by
hand and by computer) that these lattice-theoretic and Coxeter-theoretic descriptions still holds
when W is not crystallographic.
   Our Coxeter-theoretic description of the image of pop↓Cambc is surprisingly simple: we will prove
(Theorem 7.8) that a c-sortable element w ∈ Cambc is in the image of pop↓Cambc if and only if the
right descents of w all commute and w has no left inversions in common with c−1 .
   To state our purely lattice-theoretic description of the image of pop↓Cambc , let us write s1 , . . . , sn
for the simple reflections of W ; these are the atoms of Cambc . For 1 ≤ i ≤ n, let pi be the unique
maximal element of the set

                        {x ∈ Cambc | si ≤ x and sj ̸≤ x for all sj ∈ S \ {si }}.

We will prove that an element w ∈ Cambc is in the image of pop↓Cambc if and only if the interval
[pop↓Cambc (w), w] (in Cambc ) is Boolean and pi ̸≤ w for all 1 ≤ i ≤ n. When W is crystallographic,
the elements p1 , . . . , pn correspond in a natural way to the projective indecomposable modules of
the tensor algebra KQc ; this is one reason why our representation-theoretic perspective was so
useful for discovering and proving our description of the image of pop↓Cambc .

1.4. Organization. Sections 2 and 3 provide necessary background on posets and representation
theory, respectively. In Section 4, we discuss the theory of mutation of semibrick pairs, and we
extend the theory to allow mutation at multiple bricks simultaneously; the proof of one of the
main results of this section (Theorem 4.6) is postponed until Appendix A. Section 5 provides a
representation-theoretic description of the pop-stack operator on tors Λ, describes preimages of a
torsion class under pop↓tors Λ , and characterizes the image of pop↓tors Λ . Beginning in Section 6, we
fixate on Cambrian lattices. Section 6 provides background on Coxeter groups, root systems, and
Cambrian lattices (including their realizations as lattices of torsion classes). Section 7 is devoted to
characterizing the image of the pop-stack operator on an arbitrary Cambrian lattice and deducing
Equation (1.1). In Section 8, we provide explicit combinatorial characterizations and enumerations
of the images of pop-stack operators on Cambrian lattices of type A. In Section 9, we prove our
results concerning the maximum sizes of forward orbits under the pop-stack operators on lattice
quotients of the weak order. Section 10 collects several ideas for future work.

                                      2. Posets and Lattices
   Let P be a finite poset. For x, y ∈ P with x ≤ y, the interval between x and y is the induced
subposet [x, y] = {z ∈ P | x ≤ z ≤ y} of P . We say y covers x and write x ⋖ y if x < y and
[x, y] = {x, y}. The Hasse diagram of P is the graph with vertex set P in which x and y are
adjacent whenever x ⋖ y or y ⋖ x. We say P is connected if its Hasse diagram is connected. A rank
function on P is a function rank : P → Z such that rank(y) = rank(x) + 1 whenever x ⋖ y. We
say P is ranked if there exists a rank function on P . An antichain is a poset P in which any two
distinct elements are incomparable (that is, if x, y ∈ P are such that x ≤ y, then x = y). The dual
of P is the poset P ∗ with the same underlying set as P defined so that x ≤ y in P ∗ if and only if
y ≤ x in P . A subset X of P is called convex if for all x, y ∈ X and all z ∈ P with x ≤ z ≤ y, we
have z ∈ X. An order ideal is a subset I of P such that if x ∈ P and y ∈ I satisfy x ≤ y, then
x ∈ I. An upper set of P is a subset U ⊆ P such that P \ U is an order ideal. We write J(P ) for
the set of order ideals of P , ordered by inclusion.

    A lattice is a poset L such that any two elements x, y ∈ L have a greatest lower bound, which
is called their meet and denoted by x ∧ y, and a least upper bound, which is called their join and
denoted by x ∨ y. A finite lattice is distributive if it is isomorphic to J(P ) for some finite poset P .
A lattice is Boolean if it is isomorphic to the power set of a set, ordered by inclusion. Hence, if P
is an antichain, then J(P ) is Boolean.

   The Galois graph of a finite semidistributive lattice L is the loopless directed graph with vertex
set JIrrL in which there is an arrow j → j ′ if and only if j ̸= j ′ and j ̸≤ κ(j ′ ). For each edge
x ⋖ y in the Hasse diagram of L, there is a unique join-irreducible element jx,y ∈ JIrrL such that
jx,y ≤ y and κ(jx,y ) ≥ x (see [Bar19, Proposition 2.2 & Lemma 3.3]). We call jx,y the shard label of
the edge x ⋖ y. The canonical join representation and canonical meet representation of an element
v ∈ L are given by D(v) = {jx,v : x ⋖ v} and U(v) = {κ(jv,y ) : v ⋖ y}. Moreover, the canonical join
complex of L is just the collection of independent sets of the Galois graph of L.
   Note that intervals in semidistributive lattices are semidistributive. The following lemma was
stated in [DW23] for semidistrim lattices, which are more general than semidistributive lattices,
but we only need to consider semidistributive lattices. (See also [RST21, Section 4].)
Lemma 2.1 ([DW23, Corollary 7.10]). Let L be a finite semidistributive lattice, and let [u, v] be
an interval in L. There is a bijection from {j ∈ JIrrL | j ≤ v and κ(j) ≥ u} to JIrr[u,v] given by
j 7→ u ∨ j. This bijection is an isomorphism from an induced subgraph of the Galois graph of L to
the Galois graph of [u, v]. If x ⋖ y is an edge in [u, v] whose shard label in L is jx,y , then the shard
label of x ⋖ y in [u, v] is u ∨ jx,y .

of) a given object as a subcategory.
   Now let C ⊆ mod Λ be an arbitrary subcategory and M ∈ mod Λ. A minimal left C-approximation
of M is a morphism gM,C : M → C with C ∈ C such that
      (i) the induced map (gM,C )∗ : HomΛ (C, D) → HomΛ (M, D) is surjective for all D ∈ C and
     (ii) if f : C → C is such that f ◦ gM,C = gM,C , then f is an isomorphism.
Dually, a minimal right C-approximation of M is a morphism gC,M : C → M with C ∈ C such that
    (i*) the induced map (gC,M )∗ : HomΛ (D, C) → HomΛ (D, M ) is surjective for all D ∈ C and
   (ii*) if f : C → C is such that gC,M ◦ f = gC,M , then f is an isomorphism.
It is well known that minimal left and right C-approximations are unique up to isomorphism when-
ever they exist. If every object of mod Λ admits both a minimal left C-approximation and a minimal
right C-approximation, then C is said to be functorially finite. Almost all of the subcategories we
consider in this paper will be functorially finite.
3.2. Torsion classes. A pair (T , F) of subcategories of mod Λ is called a torsion pair if
     (i) HomΛ (T, F ) = 0 for all T ∈ T and F ∈ F and
    (ii) every M ∈ mod Λ is the middle term of an exact sequence tT M ,→ M ↠ fF M with
         tT M ∈ T and fF M ∈ F.
In this case, we say that T is a torsion class and that F is a torsion-free class. We denote by tors Λ
and torsf Λ the sets of torsion classes and torsion-free classes in mod Λ, respectively. Torsion pairs
were introduced by Dickson in [Dic66] as a generalization of the torsion and torsion-free abelian
groups. We refer to the expository article [Tho21] for background information on torsion pairs,
including formal statements for the facts mentioned in this section.
   It is well known that a subcategory T ⊆ mod Λ (resp. F ⊆ mod Λ) is a torsion class (resp.
torsion-free class) if and only if it is closed under extensions and quotients (resp. extensions and
submodules). Moreover, every torsion class determines a unique torsion pair and vice versa via the
associations T 7→ (T , T ⊥ ) and (T , F) 7→ T . Similarly, every torsion-free class determines a unique
torsion pair and vice versa via the associations F 7→ (⊥ F, F) and (T , F) 7→ F.
   For C ⊆ mod Λ an arbitrary subcategory, we have that T(C) is the smallest torsion class containing
C; the corresponding torsion pair is (T(C), C ⊥ ). Dually, Filt(Cogen(C)) is the smallest torsion-free
class containing C, and the corresponding torsion pair is (⊥ C, Filt(Cogen(C))).
   We consider tors Λ and torsf Λ as posets under the containment relation. Each of these is a
complete    lattice whose meet

   In this paper, we restrict our attention to algebras Λ for which the lattice tors Λ is finite. These are

indecomposable modules (up to isomorphism). If Λ is hereditary, then it is τ -tilting finite if and
only if it is representation-finite. For Λ not hereditary, representation-finiteness implies τ -tilting

3.5. Wide and Serre subcategories. A subcategory W ⊆ mod Λ is called wide if it is closed
under extensions, kernels, and cokernels. Alternatively, a subcategory is wide if it is an exact-
embedded abelian subcategory. We denote by wide Λ the set of wide subcategories of mod Λ.
   It is a classical result of [Rin76] that there is a bijection sbrick Λ → wide Λ given by X 7→ Filt(X ).
The inverse sends a wide subcategory W to the set of objects that are simple in W (i.e., those X ∈ W
that admit no proper submodules in W). Combining this with Theorem 3.7, we see that, under
the τ -tilting finite hypothesis, there are two bijections tors Λ → wide Λ given by T 7→ Filt(D(T ))
and T 7→ Filt(U(T )). The inverses of these bijections are given by W 7→ T(W) and W 7→ ⊥ W,
respectively. Moreover, these bijections correspond to the so-called Ingalls–Thomas bijections of
[IT09, MŠ17]; that is, we have the following. (See also [Eno23, Remark 4.20], which relates this
result to the rowmotion operators.)
Proposition 3.8 ([Asa20, Theorem 2.3]). Let T ∈ tors Λ.
  (1) We have Filt(D(T )) = {M ∈ T | kerf ∈ T for all N ∈ T and f : N → M }.
               POP-STACK OPERATORS FOR TORSION CLASSES AND CAMBRIAN LATTICES                              11


   (2) We have Filt(U(T )) = {M ∈ T ⊥ | cokerf ∈ T ⊥ for all N ∈ T ⊥ and f : M → N }.
Remark 3.9. Since Λ is τ -tilting finite, every wide subcategory of mod Λ is functorially finite by
[MŠ17, Corollary 3.11]. Equivalently (see [Eno22, Proposition 4.12]), for every W ∈ wide Λ, there
exists a finite-dimensional algebra Λ′ such that W is exact-equivalent to mod Λ′ .
   We conclude this section with a brief discussion of Serre subcategories. A subcategory W ⊆ mod Λ
is called Serre if it is closed under extensions, submodules, and quotient modules. The following is
well known.
Proposition 3.10. Let W ⊆ mod Λ. Then the following are equivalent.
  (1) The subcategory W is Serre.
  (2) The subcategory W is at least two of the following: a wide subcategory, a torsion class, and
      a torsion-free class.
  (3) The subcategory W is a wide subcategory, a torsion class, and a torsion-free class.
  (4) We have W = Filt(S) for some set S of modules that are simple in mod Λ.
  (5) There exists a projective module P ∈ mod Λ such that W = P ⊥ .
  (6) There exists an injective module I ∈ mod Λ such that W = ⊥ I.
  (7) The subcategory W is wide, and every object that is simple in W is also simple in mod Λ.
   Given a wide subcategory W, we will also say that a wide subcategory W ′ ⊆ W is Serre in W
if every object that is simple in W ′ is also simple in W.

6.1. Coxeter groups. A standard reference for much of the material in this subsection is [BB05].
   Let (W, S) be a finite Coxeter system. This means that S is a finite set and that W is a finite
group with a presentation of the form
                                                 ′
                                  ⟨S | (ss′ )m(s,s ) = e for all s, s′ ∈ S⟩,

                            m(s, s) = 1     and    m(s, s′ ) = m(s′ , s) ∈ {2, 3, . . .}
for all distinct s, s′ ∈ S. (We often refer only to the Coxeter group, tacitly assuming S is part of
the data of W .)
   The elements of S are called the simple reflections. A reflection is an element of the form wsw−1
for w ∈ W and s ∈ S. The Coxeter graph of W is the graph ΓW with vertex set S in which two
simple reflections s and s′ are connected by an edge whenever m(s, s′ ) ≥ 3; this edge is labeled
with the number m(s, s′ ) if m(s, s′ ) ≥ 4. We will assume that W is irreducible, which means that

characterization of finite irreducible Coxeter groups (see [BB05, Appendix A1]).
   We will use sans serif font when we write words over the alphabet S; this allows us to distinguish
a word s1 · · · sk from the element s1 · · · sk ∈ W that it represents.

as short as possible. The number of letters in a reduced word for w is called the length of w and is
denoted ℓ(w). A right inversion (resp. left inversion) of w is a reflection t such that ℓ(wt) < ℓ(w)
(resp. ℓ(tw) < ℓ(w)). The (right) weak order is the partial order ≤ on W defined so that u ≤ v if
and only if there exists a reduced word for v that has a reduced word for u as a prefix. Equivalently,
u ≤ v if and only if every left inversion of u is a left inversion of v. Let Weak(W ) denote the poset
(W, ≤). It is well known that Weak(W ) is a ranked semidistributive lattice (ℓ is a rank function).
In fact, if W is crystallographic, then Weak(W ) is isomorphic to the lattice of torsion classes of the
preprojective algebra of type W (see Remark 7.7).
   A cover reflection of an element w ∈ W is a reflection t of W such that ℓ(tw) = ℓ(w) − 1 and
tw = ws for some s ∈ S. Thus, the cover reflections of w corresponds bijectively to the elements
covered by w in Weak(W ). Because Weak(W ) is a semidistributive lattice, each element w ∈ W

   In addition to the hereditary algebras KQc , one can also associate a (non-hereditary) prepro-

specialization of Items (1) to (4) in Lemma 5.12 to the cases Λ = KQc and Λ = Π(W ).

8.3. Arc diagrams. Arrange n + 1 points along a horizontal line, and identify them with the
numbers 1, . . . , n + 1 from left to right. An arc is a curve that moves monotonically rightward
from a point i to another point j (for some i < j), passing above or below each of the points
i + 1, . . . , j − 1. Two arcs are considered to be the same if they have the same endpoints and they
pass above the same collection of numbered points. A noncrossing arc diagram (of type An ) is a
collection of arcs that can be drawn so that no two arcs have the same left endpoint or have the
same right endpoint or cross in their interiors. We write |δ| for the number of arcs in a noncrossing
arc diagram δ. Let ADn be the set of noncrossing arc diagrams of type An .
    Given a permutation w ∈ An , form a noncrossing arc diagram ∆(w) ∈ ADn as follows. For
each descent i of w, draw an arc from w(i + 1) to w(i) such that for each integer k satisfy-
ing w(i + 1) < k < w(i), the arc passes above (resp. below) the point k if w−1 (k) > i + 1 (resp.
w−1 (k) < i). This defines a map ∆ : An → ADn , and it is straightforward to check that ∆ is a
bijection. (See [Rea15].)
    Given a Coxeter element c of An , say an arc a with left endpoint i and right endpoint j is
c-sortable if for every k ∈ {i + 1, . . . , j − 1}, a passes above (resp. below) k if νc (k) = A (resp.
νc (k) = B). Note that for all 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n + 1, there is a unique c-sortable arc from i to j.
Let AD(c) = ∆(Cambc ) be the set of noncrossing arc diagrams of c-sortable elements of An . It is
immediate from Theorem 8.2 that a noncrossing arc diagram is in AD(c) if and only if all of its
arcs are c-sortable. Hence, AD(c) is a simplicial complex whose vertices are the c-sortable arcs.

Proof. First assume that a ⋖ x in L, and let a′ = π↓≡ (a). Since π↓≡ : L → L is order-preserving, we

because a′ is the largest element of π↓≡ (L) lying below a. Moreover, we cannot have a′ < a < b
because a <· x. Therefore, a and b are incomparable. This implies that a ∨ b = x (because a <· x
and b < x). Since a ≡ a′ , we have x = a ∨ b ≡ a′ ∨ b = b. But this contradicts the fact that b and
x are distinct elements of π↓≡ (L). From this contradiction, we deduce that a′ <· x in π↓≡ (L).

set of elements covered by x in π↓≡ (L); we must show that it is bijective. To show it is injective,
suppose instead that there exist distinct elements a1 , a2 that are covered by x in L such that
π↓≡ (a1 ) = π↓≡ (a2 ). Then a1 ≡ a2 , so a1 ≡ a1 ∨ a2 = x, contradicting the fact that x is the unique
minimal element of [x]≡ . To prove surjectivity, suppose d <· x in π↓≡ (L). Then there exists d′ ∈ L
such that d ≤ d′ <· x. Since π↓≡ is order-preserving, we have d ≤ π↓≡ (d′ ) < x. Thus, d = π↓≡ (d′ ). □

   It will be convenient to think of the letters in a word over the alphabet S as being distinct entities
even if they represent the same simple reflection. For example, the two occurrences of s2 in the
word s2 s1 s2 s3 are considered to be different. If s and s′ are letters representing simple reflections
s and s′ , then we let m(s, s′ ) = m(s, s′ ) (recall that m(s, s′ ) is the order of ss′ ). We can apply a
commutation move to a word by swapping two consecutive letters s and s′ if m(s, s′ ) = 2 (this move
is not allowed if m(s, s′ ) = 1). The commutation class of a word Q over S is the set of words that
can be obtained from Q via a sequence of commutation moves. We write Q ≡ Q′ if Q and Q′ are in
the same commutation class.
   Let Q be a word over S. The heap of Q is the poset Heap(Q) whose elements are the letters
in Q (which we view as distinct from one another) and whose order relation is defined so that if s
and s′ are two letters (which could represent the same simple reflection), then s < s′ if and only if
s appears to the left of s′ in every word in the commutation class of Q. A word Q′ is in the same
commutation class as Q if and only if Heap(Q) = Heap(Q′ ). More generally, Heap(Q′ ) is an order
ideal of Heap(Q) if and only if there exists a word Q′′ such that Q′ Q′′ ≡ Q.
