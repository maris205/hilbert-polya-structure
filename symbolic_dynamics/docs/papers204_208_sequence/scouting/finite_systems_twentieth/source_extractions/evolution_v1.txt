L36: ## 1. Introduction
L37: 
L38: An evolution algebra over a field is an algebra with a basis on which multiplication is defined by the product of distinct basis terms being zero and the square of each basis element being a linear form in basis elements [cite12†10 ].
L39: 
L40: In study of any class of algebras, it is important to describe up to isomorphism at least algebras of lower dimensions. In [cite13†8 ] and [cite14†11 ], the classifications of associative and nilpotent Lie algebras of low dimensions were given.
L41: About classifications of evolution algebras the following results are known:
L42: 
L43: In [cite15†3 ] (see also [cite16†4 ]) two-dimensional evolution algebras over the complex numbers were classified. For the classification of two-dimensional evolution algebras over the real numbers see [cite17†9 ].
L44: Recently, [cite18†2 ] the authors classified three dimensional evolution algebras over a field having characteristic different from 2 and in which there are roots of orders 2, 3 and 7. It is proved that there are 116 types of three-dimensional evolution algebras.
L45: Very recently, in [cite19†6 ] the authors studied the distribution of finite-dimensional evolution algebras over any base field into isotopism^{1}^{1} 1 The concept of isotopism of algebras was introduced in [cite20†1 ] as a generalization of isomorphism. Two $n$-dimensional algebras $A$ and $B$ defined over a field $K$ are isotopic if there exist three non-singular linear transformations $f,g$ and $h$ from $A$ to $B$ such that $f(u)g(v)=h(uv)$, for all $u,v\in A$.
L46: classes according to their structure tuples and to the dimension of their annihilators. It is shown the existence of four isotopism classes of two-dimensional evolution algebras, whatever the base field is. For the three-dimensional case it is shown how to deal with the distribution into isotopism classes of evolution algebras of higher dimensions.
L47: In [cite21†5 ] (see also [cite22†7 ]) a classification of indecomposable nilpotent evolution algebras up to dimension five over algebraically closed fields of characteristic not two is given. To do this in [cite21†5 ] the type and several invariant subspaces related to the upper annihilating series of finite-dimensional nilpotent evolution algebras are introduced.
L48: A class of nilpotent evolution algebras, defined in terms of a nondegenerate, symmetric, bilinear form and some commuting, symmetric, diagonalizable endomorphisms relative to the form, are constructed.
L49: In this paper we develop the methods of [cite21†5 ] for high dimensional evolution algebras. We construct nilpotent evolution algebras of any type. We show that, except the cases considered by Elduque and Labra, this construction of nilpotent evolution algebras does not give all possible nilpotent evolution algebras.
L50: ## 2. Basic definitions and facts
L51: 
L52: Evolution algebras. Let $(E,\cdot)$ be an algebra over a field $K$. If it admits a basis $\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots\}$, such that
L53: 
L54:  | $$e_{i}\cdot e_{j}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if \ $i\neq j$;}\\
L55: \displaystyle\sum_{k}a_{ik}e_{k},&\text{if \ $i=j$,}\end{cases}$$  |
L56: 
L57: then this algebra is called an evolution algebra [cite12†10 ]. The basis is called a natural basis. We denote by $A=(a_{ij})$ the matrix of the structural constants of the evolution algebra $E$.
L58: It is known that an evolution algebra is commutative but not associative, in general. For basic properties of the evolution algebra see [cite12†10 ].
L59: 
L60: For an evolution algebra $E$ and $k\geq 1$ we introduce the following sequence
L61: 
L62:  | $$E^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}E^{i}E^{k-i}.$$  |  | (2.1)
L63: 
L64: Since $E$ is a commutative algebra we obtain
L65: 
L66:  | $$E^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}E^{i}E^{k-i},$$  |
L67: 
L68: where $\lfloor x\rfloor$ denotes the integer part of $x$.
L69: ###### Definition 1.
L70: 
L71: An evolution algebra $E$ is called nilpotent if there exists some $n\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $E^{n}=0$. The smallest $n$ such that $E^{n}=0$ is called the index of nilpotency.
L72: 
L73: The following theorem is known (see [cite15†3 ]).
L74: ###### Theorem 1.
L75: 
L76: An $n$-dimensional evolution algebra $E$ is nilpotent iff the matrix of the structural constants corresponding to $E$ can be written as
L77: 
L78:  | $$\widehat{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&a_{12}&a_{13}&\dots&a_{1n}\\[4.2679pt]
L79: 0&0&a_{23}&\dots&a_{2n}\\[4.2679pt]
L80: 0&0&0&\dots&a_{3n}\\[4.2679pt]
L81: \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\[4.2679pt]
L82: 0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
L83: \end{array}\right).$$  |  | (2.2)
L84: 
L85: Upper annihilating series. Following [cite21†5 ] we introduce the following definitions:
L86: Let $\mathcal{E}$ be an evolution algebra with a natural basis $B=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}$ and matrix of structural constants $A=(a_{ij})$. The graph $\Gamma(\mathcal{E},B)=(V,E)$, with $V=\{1,\dots,n\}$ and $E=\{(i,j)\in V\times V:a_{ij}\neq 0\}$, is called the graph attached to the evolution algebra $\mathcal{E}$ relative to the natural basis $B$.
L87: ###### Definition 2.
L88: 
L89: For an algebra $\mathcal{A}$ define the chain $\operatorname{ann}^{i}(\mathcal{A})$, $i\geq 1$ by
L90: 
L91:  | $$\begin{array}[]{ll}\operatorname{ann}^{1}(\mathcal{A}):=\operatorname{ann}(\mathcal{A}):=\{x\in\mathcal{A}:x\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}x=0\},\\[8.53581pt]
L92: \operatorname{ann}^{i}(\mathcal{A})/\operatorname{ann}^{i-1}(\mathcal{A}):=\operatorname{ann}(\mathcal{A}/\operatorname{ann}^{i-1}(\mathcal{A})).\end{array}$$  |
L93: ###### Definition 3.
L94: 
L95: The following series is called the upper annihilating series:
L96: 
L97:  | $$0=\operatorname{ann}^{0}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\operatorname{ann}^{1}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\dots\subseteq\operatorname{ann}^{r}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\dots$$  |
L98: 
L99: It is known that a non-associative algebra (in particular an evolution algebra) is nilpotent if and only if its upper annihilating series riches $\mathcal{A}$, i.e., $\operatorname{ann}^{r}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}$, for some $r\geq 1$.
L100: ###### Definition 4.
L101: 
L102: Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite-dimensional nilpotent non-associative algebra over a field $\mathbb{F}$, and let $r$ be the lowest natural number such that $\operatorname{ann}^{r}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}$. The type of $\mathcal{A}$ is the sequence $[n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{r}]$ such that $n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{i}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\operatorname{ann}^{i}(\mathcal{A}))$, for all $i=1,2,\dots,r$. Thus
L103:  | $$n_{i}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\operatorname{ann}^{i}(\mathcal{A}))-\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\operatorname{ann}^{i-1}(\mathcal{A})),\ \ i=1,2,\dots,r.$$  |
L104: ## 3. Nilpotent evolution algebras
L105: 
L106: Consider a field $\mathbb{F}$ of characteristic not equal to 2. Let $\mathcal{U}$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{F}$ with $\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{U}=n$.
L107: ###### Definition 5.
L108: 
L109: Let $b:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{F}$ be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form and let $f_{i}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}$, $i=1,2,\dots,k-1$ be pairwise commuting, symmetric (relative to $b$), diagonalizable endomorphisms. We define the algebra $E(\mathcal{U},b,f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1}):=\mathcal{U}\times\underbrace{\mathbb{F}\times\dots\times\mathbb{F}}_{k}$ with multiplication
L110:  | $$\left(u,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\right)\left(v,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right)=$$  |
L111:  | $$\left(0,b(u,v),b(f_{1}(u),v)+\alpha_{1}\beta_{1},b(f_{2}(u),v)+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2},\dots,b(f_{k-1}(u),v)+\alpha_{k-1}\beta_{k-1}\right),$$  |  | (3.1)
L112: 
L113: for any $u,v\in\mathcal{U}$ and $\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{F}$.
L114: ###### Proposition 1.
L115: 
L116: $E(\mathcal{U},b,f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1})$ is a nilpotent evolution algebra of type $[\underbrace{1,1,\dots 1}_{k},n]$.
L117: ###### Proof.
L118: 
L119: By assumptions there is an orthogonal basis $\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\}$ of $\mathcal{U}$, relative to $b$, consisting of common eigenvalues for $f_{i}$, $i=1,\dots,k-1$. Then
L120: 
L121:  | $$e_{1}=(u_{1},0,0,\dots,0),\dots,e_{n}=(u_{n},0,0,\dots,0),$$  |
L122:  | $$e_{n+1}=(0,1,0,0,\dots,0),\dots,e_{n+k}=(0,0,\dots,0,1)$$  |
L123: 
L124: is a natural basis of $E=E(\mathcal{U},b,f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1})$. Moreover, $e_{i}e_{j}=0$ if $i\neq j$ and
L125:  | $$e_{i}^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllll}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{ij}e_{n+j},\ \ \mbox{if}\ \ i=1,\dots,n\\[5.69054pt]
L126: e_{i+1},\ \ \mbox{if}\ \ i=n+1,\dots,n+k-1\\[5.69054pt]
L127: 0,\ \ \mbox{if}\ \ i=n+k,\end{array}\right.$$  |  | (3.2)
L128: 
L129: where
L130: 
L131:  | $$\lambda_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}b(u_{i},u_{i}),\ \ \mbox{if}\ \ j=1\\[5.69054pt]
L132: b(f_{j-1}(u_{i}),u_{i}),\ \ \mbox{if}\ \ j=2,\dots,k.\end{array}\right.$$  |
L133: 
L134: Now using multiplication (cite23†3.1 ) we calculate $\operatorname{ann}^{j}(E)$. We have
L135:  | $$\operatorname{ann}(E)=\{\left(u,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\right)\in E:\ \ \left(u,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\right)\left(v,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right)=0,\forall\left(v,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right)\in E\}$$  |
L136:  | $$=\underbrace{0\times 0\times\dots\times 0}_{k}\times\mathbb{F}.$$  |
L137: 
L138: Note that the first zero in the RHS of this formula is $n$-dimensional zero-vector.
L139:  | $$\operatorname{ann}^{2}(E)=\{\left(u,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\right)\in E:$$  |
L140:  | $$\left(u,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\right)\left(v,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right)\in\operatorname{ann}(E),\forall\left(v,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right)\in E\}$$  |
L141:  | $$=\underbrace{0\times 0\times\dots\times 0}_{k-1}\times\mathbb{F}\times\mathbb{F}.$$  |
L142: 
L143: Using induction one can prove that
L144:  | $$\operatorname{ann}^{j}(E)=\underbrace{0\times 0\times\dots\times 0}_{k-j+1}\times\underbrace{\mathbb{F}\times\dots\times\mathbb{F}}_{j},\ \ j=1,\dots,k.$$  |
L145: 
L146: and $\operatorname{ann}^{k+1}(E)=E$. Thus we have
L147: 
L148:  | $$n_{i}=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\operatorname{ann}^{i}(E))-\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\operatorname{ann}^{i-1}(E))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,\ \ \mbox{if}\ \ i=1,\dots k\\[5.69054pt]
L149: n,\ \ \mbox{if}\ \ i=k+1.\end{array}\right.$$  |
