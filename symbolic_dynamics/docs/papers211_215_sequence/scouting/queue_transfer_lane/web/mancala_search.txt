## Abstract

We determine the optimal bound for the maximum number of moves required to reach a periodic configuration of open mancala (also called open owari), inspired by a popular African game. A mancala move can be interpreted as a map from the set of compositions of a given integer in itself, thus relating our result to the study of the corresponding finite dynamical system.

## 1. Introduction

Mancala is a family of traditional African games played in many versions and under many names. It comprises a circular list of holes containing zero or more seeds. A move consists of selecting a nonempty hole, taking all its seeds and sowing them in the subsequent holes, one seed per hole.

Here we study an idealized version of this called open mancala (see  [1], where the game is called open owari). We assume there is an infinite sequence of holes. Further we assume that the nonempty holes are consecutive; such a configuration is described by a sequence of positive integers 𝜆𝑖, 𝑖 =1,…,ℓ, giving the number of seeds in each nonempty hole, starting from the leftmost one. We assume a move always selects the leftmost nonempty hole, sowing to the right. Thus the leftmost nonempty hole always advances of one position. It is clear that the sequence of configurations becomes periodic in a finite time. The periodic configurations have been completely classified [1], [3], [2]: see Section  2.

This game is related to a card game called Bulgarian Solitaire, discussed by Gardner  [5] in a 1983 Scientific American column. Indeed, that game is isomorphic to the case of open mancala where 𝜆𝑖 ≥𝜆𝑗 for 𝑖 ≤𝑗 (monotone mancala), see Section  6. Gardner mentioned a conjecture on the maximum number of moves before the onset of periodicity when the number of cards is of the form 𝑘⁢(𝑘+1)/2. The conjecture was proved independently a few years later by Igusa  [7] and Etienne  [4]. In 1998, Griggs & Ho  [6] revisited the result and proposed a new conjecture in the case of a generic number of cards. Due to the isomorphism between monotone mancala and Bulgarian solitaire, our result is strongly related to this conjecture. However, our results apply to open mancala, not to the monotone variant, hence the conjecture about the Bulgarian Solitaire remains unproven.

The main result of the paper is the optimal bound for the maximum number of moves, before open mancala reaches periodicity, as a function of 𝑛, the number of seeds. In Section  3 we prove the lower bound by producing, for any 𝑛, a configuration that requires exactly that number of moves. In Section  4 we introduce the main tool in proving the upper bound, namely 𝑠-monotonicity. In Section  5 we prove that every configuration reaches periodicity within that number of moves.

## 2. Notations and setting

We denote by ℕ the set of nonnegative integers and by ℕ∗ the set of strictly positive integers. By 𝑇𝑘, 𝑘 ∈ℕ, we denote the 𝑘 th triangular number 𝑇𝑘=𝑘∑𝑖=0𝑖=𝑘⁢(𝑘+1)2, where in particular 𝑇0 =0.

The following special configurations play an important role.

Definition 2.8 Marching Group

For a given 𝑘 ∈ℕ we define the special mancala configuration 𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘 with mass 𝑇𝑘 and length 𝑘, called marching group^{1} of order 𝑘, as follows: 𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘𝑖={𝑘−𝑖+1if 𝑖≤𝑘,0.

Definition 2.9 Augmented Marching Group

A mancala configuration 𝜆 such that 𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘 ≤𝜆 <𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘+1 for some 𝑘 ∈ℕ is called an augmented marching group of order 𝑘. It satisfies 𝑇𝑘 ≤|𝜆| <𝑇𝑘+1.

Notice that a marching group is a particular case of an augmented marching group. The following important theorem about augmented marching groups is readily proved. See  [1, Theorems 1 and 2].

Theorem 2.1

Augmented marching groups are the only periodic configurations for ℳ and, if 𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘 ≤𝜆 <𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘+1, then the period of 𝜆 is a divisor of 𝑘 +1 . Marching groups are the only fixed points for ℳ.

A remarkable fact about open mancala is that every configuration becomes an augmented marching group, and hence periodic, after a finite number of moves. The paper is devoted to find an optimal bound on such a number of moves which depends only on the mass of the initial configuration.

Definition 2.10 Depth and Diameter

The depth of a configuration 𝜆 is its distance from the periodic configuration, i.e. the number of moves needed to reach periodicity. For 𝑛 ≥0 we call the depth of 𝛬𝑛, denoted by 𝐷⁡(𝑛), the maximal depth of all configurations 𝜆 with |𝜆| =𝑛.

The diameter of a configuration is the number of moves before the first repetition. It is equal to its depth plus the length of the period minus one. The maximal diameter of all configurations of mass 𝑛 is called the diameter of 𝛬𝑛.

Clearly if 𝑛 =𝑇𝑘 the diameter of 𝛬𝑛 equals its depth 𝐷⁡(𝑛), whereas if 𝑛 =𝑇𝑘 +𝑟, 1 ≤𝑟 ≤𝑘 the diameter of 𝛬𝑛 is bounded above by 𝐷⁡(𝑛) +𝑘.^{2}

The number 𝐷⁡(𝑛) can be seen also as the depth of a graph. Indeed, for a given 𝑛 ≥1 we can construct the directed graph 𝒢𝑛 having the configurations in 𝛬𝑛 as nodes and an arc from 𝜆 to 𝜇 whenever 𝜇 =ℳ⁡(𝜆). The graph 𝒢𝑛 contains exactly 2𝑛−1 nodes. A cycle of 𝒢𝑛 corresponds to sequences of moves that repeat periodically. A configuration is periodic if it belongs to a cycle.

In the case 𝑛 ≥2, if we remove all arcs connecting two periodic configurations (arcs that belong to a cycle), the remaining graph 𝒢𝑜𝑛 is a disjoint union of trees rooted at a periodic configuration, and in each tree all arcs point toward the root. In this setting, 𝐷⁡(𝑛) represents the depth of 𝒢𝑛, i.e. the maximal depth of the trees of 𝒢𝑜𝑛.

### 5.2. Optimal upper bound

The following lemma will be fundamental in proving the optimal upper bound.

Lemma 5.5

Let 𝜆 ∈𝛬𝑛 with 𝑛 =𝑇𝑘 +𝑟, 0 ≤𝑟 ≤𝑘, and denote by 𝜎𝑞 the number of 𝑞-canonical configurations encountered during the evolution of 𝜆, with 𝑞 =2,…,𝑘.

Then the total number 𝑠 =∑𝑞𝜎𝑞 of canonical configurations encountered, and hence the least level of monotonicity of the first configuration that becomes larger than (or equal to) 𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘, is bounded by 𝑠≤𝑊⁡(𝑘−1,𝑟+1)+1.In particular, for 𝑟 <𝑘2 we have(5.2)𝑠≤𝑘−𝑟−1 and for 𝑘−32 ≤𝑟 ≤𝑘 we have(5.3)𝑠≤⌊2⁢𝑘−𝑟3⌋,where we used the special values of function 𝑊 computed in   Definition  5.1.

Proof

If 𝜎𝑞 =0 for all 𝑞, then we can resort to Corollary 5.2 and conclude immediately. Otherwise, set 𝑠𝑞 =∑𝑖≤𝑞𝜎𝑖 and let ̄𝑞 be the largest value of 𝑞 such that 𝜎𝑞 >0. ## Depth of 𝛬𝑛

Let 𝑛 =𝑇𝑘 +𝑟 with 𝑘 ≥2 and 0 ≤𝑟 ≤𝑘 . Then 𝐷⁡(𝑛) =𝐷∗⁡(𝑛) . The case 𝑘 ≤1 corresponds to 𝑛 ≤2, easily studied manually.

Proof

In view of the results of Section  3 it suffices to prove the upper bound 𝐷⁡(𝑛) ≤𝐷∗⁡(𝑛). Let 𝜆 ∈𝛬𝑛. We invoke Lemma 5.3 so that there exists 𝑠 satisfying the constraints (5.4) such that after at most 𝑇𝑘−1 +𝑘⁢𝑠 moves the resulting configuration is 𝑠-monotone and ≥𝔪⁢𝔤𝑘. Now we enforce Lemma 5.8 and distinguish three cases.

Case   𝑟 <𝑘2. If 𝑠 ≥𝑟, we use the first inequality of (5.4) in (5.5) to conclude 𝑡†≤𝑇𝑘−1+𝑘⁢(𝑘−𝑟−1)=𝐷∗⁡(𝑛).

If 𝑠 <𝑟, using 𝑠 ≥0, and 2⁢𝑟 ≤𝑘 −1, i.e.  𝑟 ≤𝑘 −𝑟 −1 we have 𝑡†≤𝑇𝑘−1+(𝑟−1)⁢(𝑘+2)≤𝑇𝑘−1+𝑟⁢𝑘+2⁢𝑟−𝑘−2<𝑇𝑘−1+𝑘⁢(𝑘−𝑟−1)−2 so that in any case 𝑡† ≤𝐷∗⁡(𝑛).

Case   𝑟 =𝑘2. Again we distinguish the case 𝑠 ≥𝑟, together with the third inequality of (5.4) to obtain 𝑡†≤𝑇𝑘−1+𝑘⁢𝑟=3⁢𝑇𝑘−1−𝑘⁢(𝑘−𝑟−1)=𝐷∗⁡(𝑛).

Whereas if 𝑠 <𝑟, again using 𝑠 ≥0 we have 𝑡†≤𝑇𝑘−1+(𝑟−1)⁢(𝑘+2)=3⁢𝑇𝑘−1−𝑘⁢(𝑟−1)−2<𝐷∗⁡(𝑛).

Case   𝑟 >𝑘2. We start with the case 𝑠 <𝑟 and again use 𝑠 ≥0 to get 𝑡†≤𝑇𝑘−1+(𝑟−1)⁢(𝑘+2)=𝐷∗⁡(𝑛).

Finally, if 𝑠 ≥𝑟, enforcing the second inequality in (5.4), using 2⁢𝑟 −𝑘 ≥1 we obtain

𝑡†≤𝑇𝑘−1+𝑘⁢2⁢𝑘−𝑟3=𝑇𝑘−1+(𝑟−1)⁢(𝑘+2)−(2⁢𝑟−𝑘)⁢(23⁢𝑘+1)+2<𝐷∗⁡(𝑛).

We have covered all the possible cases and the proof is complete. □

The previous Theorem 5.9, and in particular the upper bound 𝐷⁡(𝑛) ≤𝐷∗⁡(𝑛) also allows to compute the maximal diameter of 𝛬𝑛:

Corollary 5.10

## Diameter of 𝛬𝑛

The diameter of 𝛬𝑛 (see   Definition  2.10) is given by 𝐷∗⁡(𝑛) +𝑃⁡(𝑛) −1 where 𝑃⁡(𝑛) is the maximal period of an augmented marching group, i.e. 𝑃⁡(𝑛) =1 if 𝑛 =𝑇𝑘 is a triangular number, otherwise 𝑃⁡(𝑛) =𝑘 if 𝑛 =𝑇𝑘 +𝑟, with 0 <𝑟 ≤𝑘, is not a triangular number.

Proof

That the diameter cannot exceed 𝐷∗⁡(𝑛) +𝑃⁡(𝑛) −1 immediately follows from Theorem 5.9. The reverse inequality follows from the constructions of Section  3, in particular we make use of Proposition 3.5, Corollary 3.7, Proposition 3.8 and the discussion about the period of the resulting augmented marching groups following each of these results. □
