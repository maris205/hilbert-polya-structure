\section{A fixed endpoint and the complete periodic census}
Fix
\begin{equation}
T(x)=\begin{cases}x+2x^2,&0\le x\le1/2,\\2x-1,&1/2<x\le1.\end{cases}
\qquad
g(z)=\frac{\sqrt{1+8z}-1}{4},\quad h(z)=\frac{1+z}{2}.
\label{eq:map}
\end{equation}
The endpoint $1/2$ belongs to the left branch, so $T(1/2)=1$.
Its assignment changes a countable boundary orbit set relative to the other
common convention and will matter for literal return intervals. The complex
square root is chosen with positive real part on $\Rea z>0$.
The neutral point is zero. Original time counts one application of $T$.

\begin{theorem}[All periodic words, with the neutral orbit separated]
For every integer $n\ge1$, $T^n$ has exactly $2^n$ fixed points. Binary
length-$n$ words give a bijection with these based points. The number of
primitive cycles of period $n$ is
\begin{equation}
P_n=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)2^{n/d}.
\label{eq:necklaces}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu$ is the Möbius function.
Only the all-zero orbit is neutral. Every other periodic orbit has positive
multiplier at least two. Least word period and least orbit period coincide.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On $[0,1]$, the maps $g,h$ are increasing, $0<g'\le1$, and $h'=1/2$.
Any inverse composition containing $h$ is a contraction with Lipschitz
constant at most $1/2$, so it has exactly one fixed point. The all-$g$
composition has only zero because $g(x)<x$ for positive $x$; the all-$h$
composition has fixed point one.

Before reading forward itineraries, exclude boundary ambiguity. If an
intermediate point of a mixed inverse cycle were zero, the inverse step
producing it would be $g$ with zero input. Iterating this implication around
the cycle forces every branch to be $g$. If it were one, the step would
be $h$ with input one, forcing the all-$h$ cycle. Thus mixed cycles avoid
zero and one. They also avoid $1/2$, which can arise only as $g(1)$ or
$h(0)$. Their forward itineraries are therefore unambiguous and agree with
the prescribed branch composition. Two different based words cannot produce
the same point because the deterministic forward orbit has only one
itinerary. Conversely every fixed point solves the equation for its inverse
word. This proves the complete count and identifies least periods.

Möbius inversion of the primitive-power decomposition gives
\eqref{eq:necklaces}. Every nonzero periodic word visits the right branch,
where $T'=2$, while each left derivative is $1+4x\ge1$. Multiplying these
positive derivatives gives the multiplier statement. Under $a$ repetitions,
the period is multiplied by $a$ and the multiplier becomes $\Lambda^a$.
\end{proof}
Each branch preserves orientation. No time-reversal symmetry is declared
for this noninvertible interval map.

\section{Every first-return branch and the sharp tail}
Set $Y=(1/2,1]$, let $\tau$ be the first positive return time to $Y$, and
write $R=T^\tau$. Define
\begin{equation}
h_n(z)=\frac{1+g^{n-1}(z)}2\quad(n\ge1),\qquad
a_m=g^m(1/2)\quad(m\ge0).
\label{eq:branches}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Complete return partition and reference-measure tail]
Every point of $Y$ has a finite first return, and
\begin{equation}
\{y\in Y:\tau(y)=n\}=h_n(Y),\qquad R\circ h_n=\mathrm{id}_Y.
\label{eq:return}
\end{equation}
These left-open, right-closed intervals partition $Y$. For $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
\Leb\{y\in Y:\tau(y)>n\}=\frac{a_{n-1}}2\sim\frac1{4n}.
\label{eq:tail}
\end{equation}
Under normalized Lebesgue measure on $Y$, the tail is asymptotic to
$1/(2n)$ and its mean is infinite.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
After a point of $Y$ takes the right branch it is positive. While it remains
in $(0,1/2]$, the left branch increases it strictly. If it never escaped,
it would converge to a positive fixed point of $x+2x^2$, which is impossible.
The inverse of a return of length $n$ first reverses $n-1$ left steps and
then one right step, giving \eqref{eq:branches}. The preceding inverse
left iterates lie in $(0,1/2]$, which proves the first-return assertion.
Moreover $g(1)=1/2$ gives $h_{n+1}(1)=h_n(1/2)$. The left-branch assignment
at $1/2$ makes the shared upper endpoints return on the claimed iterate.
These adjacent intervals exhaust $Y$ as their left endpoints tend to $1/2$.

Their endpoints give the exact tail in \eqref{eq:tail}; for $n=1$ it is
$(1/2,3/4]$, of length $1/4$. The recurrence
$a_{m-1}=a_m+2a_m^2$ gives
\begin{equation}
\frac1{a_m}-\frac1{a_{m-1}}=\frac2{1+2a_m},\qquad
\frac1{2m+2}\le a_m\le\frac1{m+2}.
\label{eq:reciprocal}
\end{equation}
The increment lies between one and two. Using the upper bound in
$2/(1+2a_m)=2-4a_m/(1+2a_m)$ and summing yields
$a_m^{-1}=2m+O(\log(m+2))$. Therefore
$a_m=(2m)^{-1}+O(\log m/m^2)$ and \eqref{eq:tail} follows.
Normalization doubles the tail, and the harmonic-series comparison gives
the infinite mean. This is a reference-Lebesgue statement; no stationary
induced density has been assumed.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Primitive induction preserves the original clock]
Nonneutral primitive $T$ cycles correspond bijectively to primitive $R$
cycles. If an induced cycle has return period $r_p$, return times
$n_1,\ldots,n_{r_p}$, and total multiplier $\Lambda_p$, its original period
is $N_p=\sum_j n_j$ and its multiplier remains $\Lambda_p$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Every nonneutral periodic word contains a right symbol and hence visits $Y$.
Successive gaps between such visits determine the induced itinerary.
Conversely \eqref{eq:branches} reconstructs the original cycle. Changing the
chosen visit rotates the induced word. Thus no additional multiplicity equal
to the number of returns is introduced. A smaller original or induced
period would force a smaller period on the other side.
\end{proof}
The unweighted zeta and its neutral-point removal are consequently
\begin{equation}
Z_T(z)=\frac1{1-2z},\qquad
Z_{T,\mathrm{nonneutral}}(z)=\frac{1-z}{1-2z},\qquad |z|<1/2.
\label{eq:unweighted}
\end{equation}
Indeed the first is the exponential series of the exact fixed counts; the
second removes the single primitive fixed point. The elementary renewal
identity $1-\sum_{n\ge1}z^n=(1-2z)/(1-z)$ is consistent with the same
original clock. These statements concern unweighted counting.

\section{One complex disk for countably many branches}
Fix once and for all
\begin{equation}
\Omega=\{z:|z-1|<3/4\},\qquad
e_k(z)=\left(\frac{z-1}{3/4}\right)^k\quad(k\ge0),
\label{eq:domain}
\end{equation}
where the $e_k$ are the normalized monomial basis of $H^2(\Omega)$.
The notation $D(c,r)$ denotes the open disk of center $c$ and radius $r$.

\begin{lemma}[Strict common range and reciprocal growth]
Every $h_n$ is holomorphic on a neighborhood of $\overline\Omega$ and
\begin{equation}
h_n(\Omega)\subset D(1,1/2)\Subset\Omega,\qquad
\sup_\Omega|h_n'|\le1/2.
\label{eq:range}
\end{equation}
For $w_m=g^m(z)$, uniformly on $\Omega$,
\begin{equation}
|w_m|\le\frac1{4/7+2m/25}\le\frac{25}{2(m+1)}.
\label{eq:complexgrowth}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $\Rea z>0$, then $\Rea\sqrt{1+8z}>1$, so $w=g(z)$ lies in the right
half-plane. The identity $z=w(1+2w)$ yields
\begin{equation}
\frac1w=\frac1z+\frac2{1+2w}.
\label{eq:complexreciprocal}
\end{equation}
The added term has positive real part. The disk $D(1,1)$ is exactly the set
of nonzero $z$ with $\Rea(1/z)>1/2$; hence $g$ preserves it.
Starting from $\Omega$, every $w_m$ stays in that disk. For $n\ge2$ this
gives $|h_n(z)-1|<1/2$, while for $n=1$ the bound is $3/8$.
The closed initial disk stays inside the right half-plane, where all iterated
branches are holomorphic. The estimate $|g'|=|1+8z|^{-1/2}\le1$ there gives
the derivative bound in \eqref{eq:range}; convexity also gives a Lipschitz
constant $1/2$ on the closed disk.

For $w\in D(1,1)$, $|w|\le2$ and $\Rea w>0$. Thus
$\Rea(2/(1+2w))\ge2/25$. The initial disk has
$\Rea(1/z)\ge4/7$. Iteration of \eqref{eq:complexreciprocal} proves
\eqref{eq:complexgrowth}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[All-branch derivative decay]
An explicit uniform constant is
\begin{equation}
C_*=1250\exp(625\pi^2/6),\qquad
\sup_\Omega|h_n'|\le\frac{C_*}{n^2}\quad(n\ge1).
\label{eq:derivativebound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate $w_{k-1}=w_k(1+2w_k)$ and telescope to obtain
\begin{align}
(g^m)'(z)
&=\left(\frac{w_m}{z}\right)^2
\prod_{k=1}^m\frac{(1+2w_k)^2}{1+4w_k}\notag\\
&=\left(\frac{w_m}{z}\right)^2
\prod_{k=1}^m\left(1+\frac{4w_k^2}{1+4w_k}\right).
\label{eq:derivativeproduct}
\end{align}
Since $|1+4w_k|\ge1$, the absolute product is at most
$\exp(4\sum_{k\ge1}|w_k|^2)$. Equation \eqref{eq:complexgrowth} bounds
its exponent by $625\pi^2/6$. Also $|z|\ge1/4$, and with $m=n-1$ the
leading square is at most $2500/n^2$. The factor $1/2$ in $h_n$ gives
\eqref{eq:derivativebound}. Its intentionally large constant proves
convergence; it is not advertised as a useful numerical cutoff certificate.
\end{proof}

\section{A trace-class operator with two explicit clocks}
Let $u$ count returns to $Y$, and let $\zeta$ count original applications of
$T$. Define
\begin{equation}
A_nf=h_n'f\circ h_n,\qquad
\LL_\zeta=\sum_{n\ge1}\zeta^n A_n,\qquad
\Delta(u,\zeta)=\det(I-u\LL_\zeta).
\label{eq:operator}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Nuclear inducing on the closed original-clock disk]
The branch series converges absolutely in trace norm on $H^2(\Omega)$ for
every $|\zeta|\le1$. It is trace-norm continuous on that closed disk and
holomorphic inside it. For fixed such $\zeta$, $\Delta$ is entire in $u$;
it is jointly holomorphic for $u\in\mathbb C$, $|\zeta|<1$.
The displayed branch series fails to converge for $|\zeta|>1$ even on the
constant function. This last assertion does not exclude separately proved
analytic continuation.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $f=\sum c_ke_k$. The coefficient functional has norm one and strict
containment in \eqref{eq:range} gives
\begin{equation}
\|h_n'e_k\circ h_n\|_{H^2}\le\sup_\Omega|h_n'|(2/3)^k.
\label{eq:rankone}
\end{equation}
Thus the explicit rank-one expansion has summable norms and
$\|A_n\|_1\le3\sup|h_n'|\le3C_*/n^2$. Hilbert-space nuclearity here is
trace class. Summing proves the norm convergence and boundary continuity;
locally uniform trace-norm convergence proves interior holomorphy. The
ordinary trace-class Fredholm determinant gives the asserted parameter
regularity and entire dependence on $u$.

For any real $x\in[1/2,1]$, the real reciprocal increment is at most two,
so $g^{n-1}(x)\ge1/(2n)$. Every real factor in
\eqref{eq:derivativeproduct} is at least one, and $x\le1$, yielding
$h_n'(x)\ge1/(8n^2)$. If $|\zeta|>1$, the terms
$\zeta^n h_n'(x)$ do not tend to zero. The branch series therefore fails
the necessary term test already for $f=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Primitive determinant with the original and induced times]
For a primitive induced cycle $p$, let $r_p$ be its number of returns,
$N_p$ its total original time, and $\Lambda_p>1$ its forward multiplier.
For $|\zeta|\le1$, set
\begin{equation}
K(\zeta)=\sum_{n\ge1}|\zeta|^n\sup_\Omega|h_n'|<\infty.
\label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
For $|u|K(\zeta)<1$, the absolutely regrouped primitive product is
\begin{equation}
\Delta(u,\zeta)=
\prod_{p\;\text{primitive induced}}\prod_{k\ge1}
\left(1-\frac{u^{r_p}\zeta^{N_p}}{\Lambda_p^k}\right).
\label{eq:primitive}
\end{equation}
At $u=1$ this identity holds initially for sufficiently small $|\zeta|$.
Entire continuation in $u$ of the determinant does not imply unrestricted
convergence of its primitive product.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every $r$-branch composition $\chi$ maps the closed disk into the common
interior disk and has Lipschitz constant at most $2^{-r}$. It has exactly one
fixed point $x_\chi$, and invariance of $[1/2,1]$ makes that point real.
Write $C_\chi f=f\circ\chi$ for composition by this branch.
Summing diagonal Hardy coefficients as a geometric series and applying the
residue theorem gives
\begin{equation}
\tr(\chi'C_\chi)=\frac1{2\pi\ii}\int_{|z-1|=3/4}
\frac{\chi'(z)}{z-\chi(z)}\dd z
=\frac{\chi'(x_\chi)}{1-\chi'(x_\chi)}.
\label{eq:trace}
\end{equation}
Strict containment justifies summation under the contour. Contraction gives
the unique zero of $z-\chi(z)$ and makes it simple. Summing the absolute
trace contributions over all compositions gives the bound $2K(\zeta)^r$.

First use the trace-log determinant identity on the smaller domain
$|u|\|\LL_\zeta\|<1$. The absolute coefficient bound extends its scalar
logarithmic series to $|u|K(\zeta)<1$. The identity theorem then identifies
its exponential with the entire determinant throughout that disk.
This argument does not identify $K$ with the Hardy operator norm.

For an $a$-fold repeat of $p$, the inverse multiplier is $\Lambda_p^{-a}$,
so its trace weight is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Lambda_p^{-a}}{1-\Lambda_p^{-a}}
=\sum_{k\ge1}\Lambda_p^{-ak},
\label{eq:repetitions}
\end{equation}
and its two clocks are $(ar_p,aN_p)$. Grouping $r_p$ based phases and
dividing by $ar_p$ in the logarithm leaves $1/a$. Absolute convergence now
permits the primitive and repetition regrouping, giving
\eqref{eq:primitive}. Finally $K(\zeta)\to0$ as $\zeta\to0$, which gives
the stated $u=1$ specialization.
\end{proof}
The neutral fixed point has multiplier one and would make the flat-trace
denominator vanish. It cannot be inserted as another ordinary trace-class
branch contribution. Equation \eqref{eq:unweighted} and the derivative-weighted
determinant \eqref{eq:primitive} are distinct objects even though both retain
the original iteration clock.

\section{The original Perron operator on the specified \texorpdfstring{$L^1$}{L1} space}
On Lebesgue $L^1([0,1])$, define
\begin{equation}
\PP f(x)=g'(x)f(g(x))+\frac12 f((x+1)/2).
\label{eq:perron}
\end{equation}
Change of variables on the two monotone branches proves positivity,
preservation of integral, and $L^1$ contraction. Endpoint values do not
matter in this space.

\begin{theorem}[Noncompactness and a neutral approximate eigenvalue]
The operator \eqref{eq:perron} is not compact, hence not nuclear, on this
$L^1$ space. On its closed zero-integral subspace, one is an approximate
eigenvalue, and the exact power norm is
\begin{equation}
\|\PP^n|_{\int f=0}\|_{L^1\to L^1}=1
\quad\text{for every integer }n\ge0.
\label{eq:powernorm}
\end{equation}
There are no constants $0<C<\infty$ and $0<\rho<1$ for which
\begin{equation}
\|\PP^n|_{\int f=0}\|_{L^1\to L^1}\le C\rho^n
\quad\text{for all }n\ge0.
\label{eq:nogap}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose disjoint intervals $I_j\subset(0,1/2)$ and set
$f_j=\mathbf1_{I_j}/|I_j|$. Monotonicity makes the images $T(I_j)$
disjoint. Their Perron images have norm one and these disjoint supports,
so their pairwise distance is two. The image of the unit ball is therefore
not relatively compact. This noncompactness argument alone is not specific
to intermittency.

The neutral point yields the stronger assertion. For $0<\epsilon<1/4$, let
$f_\epsilon=\epsilon^{-1}\mathbf1_{[0,\epsilon]}$. Direct integration over
the common support and the extra interval up to
$T(\epsilon)=\epsilon+2\epsilon^2$ gives
\begin{equation}
\|\PP f_\epsilon-f_\epsilon\|_1
=2\left(1-\frac{g(\epsilon)}\epsilon\right)\le4\epsilon.
\label{eq:approx}
\end{equation}
For the equality, $g'\le1$ on the common support, and the extra mass is
$(\epsilon-g(\epsilon))/\epsilon$. The inequality follows from
$\epsilon=g(\epsilon)+2g(\epsilon)^2$.
Now $v_\epsilon=f_\epsilon-f_{2\epsilon}$ has integral zero and norm one,
and
\begin{equation}
\|(\PP-I)v_\epsilon\|_1\le12\epsilon\longrightarrow0.
\label{eq:zerointegral}
\end{equation}
The subspace is invariant because the operator preserves integral.
For every fixed integer $n\ge1$, the contraction property and telescoping
give
\begin{equation}
\|\PP^n v_\epsilon-v_\epsilon\|_1
\le\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|\PP^j(\PP-I)v_\epsilon\|_1
\le12n\epsilon.
\label{eq:telescoping}
\end{equation}
Hence the restricted power norm is at least $1-12n\epsilon$ for every
$\epsilon$, and letting $\epsilon\to0$ gives a lower bound one.
Contraction gives the matching upper bound; $n=0$ is the identity.
This proves \eqref{eq:powernorm}. The right side of \eqref{eq:nogap} tends
to zero, contradicting the exact norm for large $n$.
\end{proof}
This theorem is confined to the named Lebesgue $L^1$ space. It does not
transfer to an unstated bounded-variation, anisotropic, or analytic space,
and it does not contradict a separately defined regularized determinant.
