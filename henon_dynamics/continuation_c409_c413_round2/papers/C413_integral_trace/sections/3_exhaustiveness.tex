\section{Exhaustiveness by a maximal coordinate}\label{sec:exhaustiveness}

Let $(x_i)$ be a periodic integral solution of
\eqref{eq:recurrence}, and set $M=\max_i|x_i|$. If $M=0$,
the point is the origin. We first handle every $M\geq2$ uniformly,
and then resolve $M=1$ by a complete count within the unit cube.

Choose the index so that $x_0=u$ with $|u|=M$, and write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:neighbours}
 a=x_{-1},\qquad b=x_1.
\end{equation}
Two instances of the recurrence give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maximum-equations}
 au=x_{-2}+b,\qquad ub=a+x_2.
\end{equation}
No assumption has been made on the sign of $u$.

\begin{lemma}[Neighbour bound and equality case]\label{lem:maximum}
For $M\geq2$, the neighbours satisfy $|a|,|b|\leq2$.
If either has modulus two, the orbit belongs to $E\sqcup D$.
Every other periodic orbit of height at least two therefore has
$a,b\in\{-1,0,1\}$ at a coordinate of maximal modulus.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The triangle inequality in \eqref{eq:maximum-equations} gives
$M|a|\leq2M$ and $M|b|\leq2M$, proving the bound.
Suppose first that $b=2\eta$ with $\eta\in\{-1,1\}$.
The equality $2\eta u=a+x_2$, together with
$|a|,|x_2|\leq M=|u|$, forces equality in the triangle
inequality. Both terms have the sign of $\eta u$ and modulus $M$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:equal-neighbour}
 a=x_2=\eta u.
\end{equation}
The first equation in \eqref{eq:maximum-equations} now implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mtwo}
 M^2=|au|\leq |x_{-2}|+|b|\leq M+2.
\end{equation}
Since $M$ is an integer at least two, this forces $M=2$.
Writing $u=2\varepsilon$, the consecutive triple at the maximum is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:special-signed}
 (a,u,b)=(2\eta\varepsilon,2\varepsilon,2\eta),
 \qquad\varepsilon,\eta\in\{-1,1\}.
\end{equation}
Its entries have modulus two and positive product. The four choices
are exactly $E\sqcup D$, already verified in
Proposition~\ref{prop:itineraries}. Three consecutive entries
determine the entire two-sided orbit, so there is no further
continuation in this case.

If $|a|=2$, apply the preceding argument to the reversed scalar
sequence of Lemma~\ref{lem:reversal}. Its right neighbour is $a$.
The resulting triple is a permutation of a point of $E\sqcup D$,
and hence again lies in that same four-point set. In all other cases,
integrality and the neighbour bound give the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Zero-neighbour alternatives]\label{lem:zeros}
Suppose $M\geq2$ and $a,b\in\{-1,0,1\}$. If both
neighbours vanish, the orbit is $A_M$. Exactly one vanishing
neighbour is impossible.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $a=b=0$, the triple is $(0,u,0)$, with $u=\pm M$.
Both signs occur in the already verified axis word
\eqref{eq:wordA}, so uniqueness of the scalar sequence gives $A_M$.

Suppose that $a=0$ and $b=t\in\{-1,1\}$. Starting from
$(x_{-1},x_0,x_1)=(0,u,t)$, the recurrence gives
\begin{align}
 x_2&=ut,& x_3&=0,& x_4&=-t,\nonumber\\
 x_5&=-ut,& x_6&=u,& x_7&=t(1-u^2).
 \label{eq:zero-contradiction}
\end{align}
In the final step we used $t^2=1$. It follows that
$|x_7|=M^2-1>M$ for every integer $M\geq2$, contradicting
the definition of $M$. This includes the endpoint $M=2$.
The case $b=0$, $a\in\{-1,1\}$ reduces to the one just
treated by Lemma~\ref{lem:reversal}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Nonzero-neighbour alternatives]\label{lem:signs}
Suppose $M\geq2$ and $a,b\in\{-1,1\}$. Then the orbit
is $B_M$ or $C_M$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The second equation of \eqref{eq:maximum-equations} reads
$x_2=bu-a$. If $abu<0$, the terms $bu$ and $a$ have
opposite signs, so $|x_2|=M+1$, a contradiction. Thus $abu>0$,
and therefore $u=abM$. This leaves precisely the four triples
in Table~\ref{tab:signs}.

\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The unit-cube remainder]\label{prop:unit-cube}
Every periodic orbit of height at most one belongs to
$O\sqcup A_1\sqcup B_1\sqcup C_1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Consider triples in $\{-1,0,1\}^3$. If a triple has no zero
entry and negative coordinate product, then $yz=-x$ and the next
coordinate is $yz-x=-2x$, of modulus two. The four triples of
this type cannot occur in an orbit of height at most one.

The remaining $23$ triples consist of the origin, the six triples
with exactly one nonzero entry, the twelve with exactly two nonzero
entries, and the four with three nonzero entries and positive product.
The first two groups are $O$ and $A_1$. Equations
\eqref{eq:B1} and \eqref{eq:wordC}, or
Table~\ref{tab:twelve}, show that $B_1$ and $C_1$ lie in
the union of the last two groups. They are disjoint and have total
size $4+12=16$, by Proposition~\ref{prop:itineraries}. That
union has exactly sixteen elements, so they exhaust it. This proves
the claim without a search or an assumed numerical period bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the periodic classification in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
If $M\geq2$, Lemma~\ref{lem:maximum} either identifies the
orbit as $E$ or $D$, or reduces its neighbours to
$\{-1,0,1\}$. Lemmas~\ref{lem:zeros} and~\ref{lem:signs}
then identify it as $A_M$, $B_M$, or $C_M$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:unit-cube} handles $M\leq1$.
This proves inclusion in the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:classification}; the reverse inclusion, disjointness, and
least periods were proved in Proposition~\ref{prop:itineraries}.
\end{proof}

As a finite consequence, the cube $[-2,2]^3$ contains exactly
49 points whose entire orbit remains in that cube: the five special
points and the $22$ points contributed by each of $m=1,2$.
For every other one of its 76 initial states, each time direction
must leave the cube in at most 125 iterates. Indeed, 126 successive
states inside a set of size 125 would repeat; invertibility would
then make the initial point periodic. The classification just proved
would force it into the listed 49 points. This finite certificate is
a consequence of the uniform proof, not an input to it.
