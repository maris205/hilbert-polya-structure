\section{The cubic normal form and local tools}\label{sec:normal}

We first record the explicit field description used at every vertex.
Its constants matter because the arithmetic conclusion is over the
original field $k$.

\begin{lemma}[Cubic splitting field]\label{lem:normal}
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic three containing $a\neq0$ and
$\alpha\neq0$. Choose $\beta,z$ in an algebraic closure with
\[
 \beta^2=\alpha/a^3,\qquad z^3-z=\beta.
\]
Then the splitting field of $f(X)-\alpha$ over $F$ is $F(\beta,z)$.
Its three roots are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:roots}
 a(z^2-1),\qquad a(z^2+z),\qquad a(z^2-z).
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discproduct}
 \disc_X(f(X)-\alpha)=a^3\alpha,
 \qquad \prod_{f(\gamma)=\alpha}\gamma=\alpha.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Direct expansion in characteristic three gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalidentity}
 f\bigl(a(z^2-1)\bigr)=a^3(z^3-z)^2.
\end{equation}
The three substitutions $z,z+1,z-1$ give exactly the expressions
in \eqref{eq:roots}. Since $\beta\neq0$, the equation
$z^3-z=\beta$ implies $z\notin\F_3$. The pairwise differences
of the displayed expressions are $a(z+1)$, $a(1-z)$ and $az$,
all nonzero. Hence these are the three distinct roots, and the
splitting field of $f(X)-\alpha$ is contained in $F(\beta,z)$.
Conversely, subtracting the second expression in \eqref{eq:roots}
from the third gives $az$. Hence the field generated by the three
roots contains $z$ and then $\beta=z^3-z$. This proves equality of
fields, not just an embedding into a radical extension.

The product formula follows from the constant coefficient $-\alpha$.
For the discriminant, the resultant with the derivative is
$(-a)^3\prod\gamma=-a^3\alpha$. The degree-three discriminant sign
is negative, giving the first formula in \eqref{eq:discproduct}.
\end{proof}

In particular, if $\Delta$ is a Vandermonde product of the three
roots, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:betavandermonde}
 (\Delta/a^3)^2=\alpha/a^3.
\end{equation}
Thus the quadratic radical is already in the cubic splitting field,
with no need to adjoin a square root of $a$.

\begin{lemma}[Generic roots and the first height]\label{lem:first}
For every $n\geq1$, the polynomial $f^{\circ n}(X)-t$ is separable
and irreducible over $k(t)$. At height one,
\[
 L_1=k(z),\qquad t=a^3(z^3-z)^2,
\]
and its Galois group is the group of six transformations
$z\mapsto\varepsilon z+j$, with $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$ and
$j\in\F_3$. In particular $G_1=\sym_3$, $L_1/k$ is regular, and
the geometric ramification index at infinity is six.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The polynomial $f^{\circ n}(X)-t$ is irreducible in $k[X,t]$:
its quotient ring is $k[X]$ under $t=f^{\circ n}(X)$. It is
primitive as a polynomial in $X$ over $k[t]$, so Gauss's lemma gives
irreducibility over $k(t)$. The chain rule gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:iterate-derivative}
 (f^{\circ n})'(X)=(-a)^n\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{\circ j}(X).
\end{equation}
If a zero of this derivative also satisfied $f^{\circ n}(X)=t$,
some iterate would be zero and all later iterates would be zero,
contradicting the transcendence of $t$. This proves separability.

For $n=1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:normal} identifies the splitting field
with $k(z)$ and the displayed parameter $t$. This rational function
has degree six, so $[k(z):k(t)]=6$. Each of the six indicated
transformations fixes $t$, and they form the full Galois group,
isomorphic to $\sym_3$. The field $k(z)$ is a rational function
field over $k$, so it has no additional constants and is regular.
The unique pole of $t$ has order six; this gives the geometric
infinity index.
\end{proof}

For reference, we give the radical-degree fact used below. This also
specifies precisely what is meant by the rank of a collection of
classes.

\begin{lemma}[Classical radical degrees]\label{lem:radicaldegree}
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic different from two. For
$b_1,\ldots,b_s\in F^*$, the degree of
$F(\sqrt{b_1},\ldots,\sqrt{b_s})/F$ is $2^r$, where $r$ is the
dimension of the span of $[b_1],\ldots,[b_s]$ in $F^*/F^{*2}$.
In characteristic three, if $z_i^3-z_i=c_i\in F$, then
$[F(z_1,\ldots,z_s):F]=3^r$, where $r$ is the dimension of the
span of $[c_i]$ in $F/\wpmap(F)$ over $\F_3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In the quadratic case the compositum is Galois, with group a subgroup
$H\subseteq\F_2^s$ recording the signs of the square roots.
An exponent vector $e$ annihilates $H$ precisely when
$\prod_i\sqrt{b_i}^{\,e_i}$ is fixed by $H$, hence lies in $F$.
This is equivalent to $\prod_i b_i^{e_i}$ being a square in $F$;
the converse uses that the two square roots of a square are in $F$.
The annihilator of $H$ is therefore the relation space of the square
classes. The perfect coordinate pairing on $\F_2^s$ gives
$\dim H=r$.

For Artin--Schreier equations, the compositum is again Galois, now
with $H\subseteq\F_3^s$ recording the translations of the $z_i$.
A vector $e$ annihilates $H$ if and only if $\sum_i e_i z_i\in F$.
Such a vector gives $\sum_i e_i c_i\in\wpmap(F)$. Conversely,
if $\sum_i e_i c_i=u^3-u$ for $u\in F$, then
$\sum_i e_i z_i-u$ is a root of $X^3-X$ and belongs to $\F_3$.
Thus $e$ annihilates $H$. The same pairing argument proves the claim.
\end{proof}

All completions used in the geometric calculation are one-variable
complete discretely valued fields with algebraically closed constant
and residue field. The following local facts will be applied in that
setting.

\begin{lemma}[Geometric local facts]\label{lem:local}
Let $K=\kappa((s))$, where $\kappa$ is algebraically closed of
characteristic three, and let $v_K(s)=1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every unit of $\kappa[[s]]$ is a square in $K$. Every element
of $\kappa[[s]]$ belongs to $\wpmap(K)$.
\item If $v_K(b)=-1$, then $b\notin\wpmap(K)$, and the extension
defined by $z^3-z=b$ is totally ramified of degree three.
\item The compositum of finitely many tame extensions of $K$, of
ramification indices $e_1,\ldots,e_r$, is tame of index
$\operatorname{lcm}(e_1,\ldots,e_r)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For the first statement, solve the square-root or Artin--Schreier
equation in the algebraically closed residue field. Hensel's lemma
lifts the root: the derivatives are respectively a nonzero multiple
of the residue square root and $-1$.

If $u$ has a pole, $v_K(u^3-u)=3v_K(u)$; if $u$ is integral,
$u^3-u$ has no pole. Thus an element of pole order one is not an
Artin--Schreier image. The equation gives a degree-three extension.
In that extension, $z$ must have negative valuation and
$3v(z)=-e$, where $e$ is the ramification index over $K$. Hence
$3\mid e$, and the degree bound forces $e=3$.

For the last statement the residue extension is trivial. A tame
extension of index $e$ can be written $K(s^{1/e})$: for a
uniformizer $\pi$ upstairs, write $s=u\pi^e$, take an $e$th root
of the unit $u$ by Hensel's lemma, and adjust $\pi$.
Its degree is $e$, and $\kappa$ already contains the $e$th roots
of unity. The asserted compositum and its index follow.
\end{proof}

We also use the standard local different formula: if a finite
separable geometric extension is written
$\kappa((s))\subseteq\kappa((u))$ with $s=H(u)$, its different
exponent is $\ord_u H'(u)$. This follows from the presentation of
relative differentials, generated by $du$ with relation
$H'(u)du=0$, or equivalently from the monogenic different formula.
For a tower the exponents satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:different-transitivity}
 d(L/K)=d(L/F)+e(L/F)d(F/K).
\end{equation}
These local facts, Kummer and Artin--Schreier theory, and the
Riemann--Hurwitz formula are classical function-field tools; see,
for example, \cite[Chapter~3]{stichtenoth2009function}.
