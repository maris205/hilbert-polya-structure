\section{Primitive rays and the full interior polar divisor}
\label{sec:poles}

Assume \(a=c^r,b=c^s\) with \(\gcd(r,s)=1\).
Every positive lattice point has a unique decomposition
\((n,m)=k(i,j)\), \(k\geq1\), \((i,j)\in\prim\).
With \(h_{i,j}=\gcd(ri,sj)\), summation on one ray gives
\[
\sum_{k\geq1}(c^{k h_{i,j}}-1)x^{ki}y^{kj}
=\frac{c^{h_{i,j}}x^iy^j}{1-c^{h_{i,j}}x^iy^j}
-\frac{x^iy^j}{1-x^iy^j}.
\]
Absolute convergence on \(\Omega_{r,s,c}\) justifies rearrangement.
Moreover,
\[
\sum_{(i,j)\in\prim}\frac{x^iy^j}{1-x^iy^j}
=\sum_{n,m\geq1}x^ny^m
=\frac{xy}{(1-x)(1-y)}.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:meromorphic-expansion} on the original domain.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bounded-h}
For every \((i,j)\in\prim\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h-factor}
h_{i,j}=\gcd(r,j)\gcd(s,i)\mid rs.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a prime \(\ell\). Write
\(\alpha=v_\ell(r),\beta=v_\ell(s)\) and
\(\gamma=v_\ell(i),\delta=v_\ell(j)\).
The coprimality assumptions give
\(\min(\alpha,\beta)=\min(\gamma,\delta)=0\).
If \(\alpha>0\), then \(\beta=0\), and
\(\min(\alpha+\gamma,\delta)=\min(\alpha,\delta)\), since either
\(\gamma=0\) or \(\delta=0\). The case \(\beta>0\) is symmetric,
and if \(\alpha=\beta=0\), the minimum is zero.
Hence the valuation of \(h_{i,j}\) equals that of the right-hand
side of \eqref{eq:h-factor}, proving both assertions.
\end{proof}

On a compact sub-bidisc \(|x|\leq u<1,|y|\leq v<1\), the numerator
monomials satisfy
\[
|c^{h_{i,j}}x^iy^j|\leq c^{rs}u^iv^j.
\]
Only finitely many pairs have the right-hand side greater than
\(1/2\). For all remaining pairs, the corresponding term in
\eqref{eq:meromorphic-expansion} is holomorphic and bounded by
\(2c^{rs}u^iv^j\). This is a summable family.
Thus the expansion is locally a finite meromorphic sum plus a
normally convergent holomorphic tail. It defines a meromorphic
function on all of \(\D^2\), with a locally finite family of possible
polar hypersurfaces \(P_{i,j}\).

We now check that these are actual distinct components.
For a primitive vector \((i,j)\), choose a second integer vector
so that the two form a unimodular matrix. The resulting invertible
monomial coordinate change on \((\C^\times)^2\) makes
\(x^iy^j\) one coordinate. Hence its nonzero level sets are smooth
and irreducible in the algebraic torus. The portion of a level set
in \(\D^2\) is connected: after choosing a point \((\alpha,\beta)\)
on it, it has the parametrization
\[
x=\alpha t^j,\qquad y=\beta t^{-i},\qquad t\in\C^\times,
\]
and the bidisc inequalities specify an annulus in \(t\).
The portion is nonempty, for example by taking positive
\(x=c^{-h_{i,j}/(2i)}\) and \(y=c^{-h_{i,j}/(2j)}\).
Distinct primitive exponent vectors give distinct hypersurfaces.

At a point of one component away from all the others, its own
summand has a simple pole and all other summands are holomorphic.
Such points exist because the family is locally finite and distinct
components have discrete intersections on each component.
Thus every component is genuine and has divisor order one;
intersections do not erase any part of the polar divisor.

There is also a direct slice calculation that rules out cancellation
at an intersection. Fix \(y_0\in\D\) and a pole \(x_0\in\D\).
Neither coordinate is zero. For each summand with
\(c^{h_{i,j}}x_0^iy_0^j=1\), differentiation of its denominator gives
\[
\Res_{x=x_0}
\frac{c^{h_{i,j}}x^iy_0^j}{1-c^{h_{i,j}}x^iy_0^j}
=-\frac{x_0}{i}.
\]
Only finitely many such summands meet a compact neighborhood of
\((x_0,y_0)\), and the remaining terms are holomorphic there.
Their residues sum to \eqref{eq:residue}, which is nonzero because
\(x_0\ne0\) and every \(1/i\) is positive.
This completes the continuation and polar-divisor assertions of
Theorem~\ref{thm:classification}.
