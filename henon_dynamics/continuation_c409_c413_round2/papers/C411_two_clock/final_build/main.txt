                              Joint meromorphic boundaries
                               of two-clock common returns



                                                    Abstract
         For integers a, b ≥ 2, we study the ordinary two-variable series whose coefficient at xn y m is
     the number of common fixed points of the n-th iterate of circle multiplication by a and the m-th
     iterate of circle multiplication by b. These coefficients are gcd(an − 1, bm − 1). We classify the
     open domain of absolute convergence and the joint meromorphic continuation for every pair of
     bases. For multiplicatively independent bases the convergence domain is the unit bidisc. For
     a = cr , b = cs with coprime positive r, s, it is the portion of that bidisc satisfying crs |x|s |y|r < 1.
     In the dependent case a primitive-ray expansion continues the series meromorphically to the
     whole bidisc and identifies every polar hypersurface, with noncancelling simple slice residues.
     In both cases no point of the bidisc boundary admits joint meromorphic continuation. The
     boundary proof combines a dense positive atomic measure on real parameter slices with a classical
     subharmonic propagation argument and, where necessary, interior pole grids. The conclusion is
     genuinely joint: it does not require every individual complex slice to have a natural boundary.


1    Introduction
Two commuting circle endomorphisms have common periodic points at two independent times.
For multiplication by a and by b, the common-return count at (n, m) is the elementary arithmetic
quantity gcd(an − 1, bm − 1). We retain both times in the ordinary series
                                        X
                       Ra,b (x, y) =           gcd(an − 1, bm − 1)xn y m ,        a, b ≥ 2.                       (1)
                                       n,m≥1

Our question is the full complex-analytic geometry of this series: where it converges absolutely,
which poles appear after continuation, and which points obstruct joint meromorphic continuation.
    Theorem 2.1 answers these questions for every pair of integer bases. Multiplicative dependence
changes the initial convergence domain and creates an explicit locally finite polar divisor inside the
bidisc. It does not change the terminal joint boundary: the entire boundary of the bidisc obstructs
meromorphic continuation in both branches. In particular, the curved part of the dependent
convergence boundary must not be confused with the final joint natural boundary.
    Neither the dynamics nor the use of rectangular return times is new. Ward’s thesis already
defines a rectangular zeta function, aggregating rectangles by volume into one variable [7, Chapter
IV, Definition IV.1.2]. Ward’s later paper explicitly treats the two independent times of the
multiplication by 2 and 3 circle action and its solenoid extension [6, Example 3.1]. These are direct
precedents for the native count and the two clocks in (1). Merely keeping a second variable would
not constitute the analytic classification proved here.
    There are also close one-variable invariants for commuting systems. Miles studies weak and
strong synchronization through equalities between corresponding iterates at a common time, together
with generating functions and arithmetic growth bounds [3]. His natural-boundary theorem for

6     Propagation and the joint meromorphic boundary
6.1    A classical subharmonic propagation argument
We give the precise local statement needed to pass from Proposition 5.1 to a joint boundary.
Its ingredients are the standard upper-envelope and decreasing-limit principles for subharmonic
functions, Cauchy’s inequality and the maximum principle; see [2, Section 4.8 and Properties 8.4.3].
The lemma is an explicit application of these classical tools.

6.2   The independent branch
For multiplicatively independent bases, Section 3 proves holomorphy of Ra,b on D2 . Choose
t ∈ (0, 1/b), apply Proposition 5.1, and then apply Lemma 6.1 with V = D. This excludes every
joint meromorphic cap on T × D. Since Ra,b (x, y) = Rb,a (y, x), the same argument excludes D × T.
A cap at a corner in T2 would contain a nearby point of one of these faces and restrict to a cap
there. Thus every point of ∂(D2 ) is excluded in this branch.

7    Consequences and limits
For a = b = 2, the initial domain is

                                       {(x, y) ∈ D2 : 2|xy| < 1},

and the continued function has the polar components 2xi y j = 1 for (i, j) ∈ P. This specialization
illustrates both distinctions in the theorem: the diagonal series is rational, but the joint bidisc
boundary is impenetrable; and the curved boundary
                                              √     √ of initial convergence is not everywhere a barrier
to continuation. For example, (x, y) = (i/ 2, i/ 2) lies on 2|xy| = 1 but on no polar component.
The pair (i, j) = (1, 1) gives 2xy = −1, while every other positive primitive pair has i + j > 2 and
hence |2xi y j | < 1. The continued function is therefore holomorphic near this point. This is a direct
illustration of the classified divisor, not an additional independent result.
    The arithmetic and analytic inputs have separate roles. Corvaja–Zannier supplies the subexpo-
nential two-index bound in the independent branch. Elementary common-base algebra determines
the dependent convergence domain and ray exponents. Positive atomic measures give one family of
natural-boundary slices for both branches. Classical subharmonic propagation turns those slices into
joint boundary obstructions where a holomorphic product is available, and the actual dependent
pole grids settle the remaining parameter range.
    The result does not assert that every fixed complex slice has a natural boundary; the zero axes
show why that would be false. Nor does it give an effective exceptional threshold for the S-unit
theorem, a new diagonal recurrence classification, or a new definition of rectangular returns. It
concerns the ordinary two-clock series (1) and its native common fixed counts, without replacing
the two integer times by volume, subgroup index, or another arithmetic clock.
