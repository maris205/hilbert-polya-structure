\section{Integrality and normal forms}
\label{sec:normalization}

The inverse of~\eqref{eq:family} is
\[
 H_{a,b}^{-1}(x,y)=(x^2+bx+a-y,x).
\]
Thus every periodic orbit gives a cyclic rational coordinate sequence
\((x_i)_{i\in\Z/n\Z}\) satisfying
\begin{equation}
 x_{i-1}+x_{i+1}=x_i^2+bx_i+a.
 \label{eq:general_recurrence}
\end{equation}
All arguments below allow repeated coordinates and an arbitrary
positive period \(n\).

\begin{lemma}[Integrality]
\label{lem:integrality}
If \(a,b\in\Z\), every rational periodic point of \(H_{a,b}\) belongs
to \(\Z^2\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix a prime \(p\) and let \(M=\max_i|x_i|_p\). If \(M>1\), choose
\(j\) with \(|x_j|_p=M\). The term \(x_j^2\) has norm \(M^2\),
whereas \(|bx_j|_p\le M\) and \(|a|_p\le1\). The ultrametric
inequality, with its unique dominant term, gives
\[
 |x_j^2+bx_j+a|_p=M^2.
\]
The left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:general_recurrence} has norm at most
\(M\), a contradiction. Hence \(|x_i|_p\le1\) for every prime \(p\)
and every \(i\). A rational number integral at every prime is an
integer.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Integer translation]
\label{lem:translation}
With \(q,e,A\) as in Theorem~\ref{thm:classification}, the map
\(T_q(x,y)=(x+q,y+q)\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
 T_q\circ H_{a,b}\circ T_q^{-1}=H_{A,e}.
 \label{eq:translation}
\end{equation}
It preserves rationality, integrality, and exact periods. The original
coordinates are obtained from normalized coordinates by subtracting
\(q\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put \(x=u-q\) and \(y=v-q\). The second coordinate after conjugation
is
\[
 v^2+(b-2q)v+a+q^2-bq+2q-u
 =v^2+ev+A-u,
\]
and the first is \(v\). The remaining statements follow because the
translation and its inverse are defined over \(\Z\).
\end{proof}

We henceforth use \(x_i\in\Z\) for normalized coordinates and treat
\(e=0,1\). Summing~\eqref{eq:normalized_word} around the cycle gives
\begin{align}
 \sum_i(x_i-1)^2&=n(1-A), && e=0, \label{eq:sum_even}\\
 \sum_i(x_i-\tfrac12)^2&=n(\tfrac14-A), && e=1.
 \label{eq:sum_odd}
\end{align}
For \(e=0\), there is no real periodic orbit when \(A>1\), and the
case \(A=1\) consists only of the fixed point \((1,1)\). For
\(e=1\), every summand in~\eqref{eq:sum_odd} is at least \(1/4\)
because \(x_i\) is integral. Consequently no rational periodic orbit
exists when \(A>0\). At \(A=0\), all coordinates are 0 or 1. A zero
coordinate forces both neighbors to be zero, and a coordinate 1 forces
both neighbors to be 1. There are exactly two fixed points,
\((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\).

Both branches can be centered into the same recurrence. Set
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
 e & u_i & c & \text{coordinate lattice}\\ \hline
 0 & x_i & -A & \Z\\
 1 & x_i+\tfrac12 & -A-\tfrac34 & \Z+\tfrac12
 \end{array}
 \label{eq:centered_data}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 u_{i-1}+u_{i+1}=u_i^2-c.
 \label{eq:centered_recurrence}
\end{equation}
The summation center \(x_i-1/2\) in~\eqref{eq:sum_odd} and the
recurrence center \(x_i+1/2\) in~\eqref{eq:centered_data} have different
purposes; their signs are not interchangeable.

\begin{lemma}[Maximum-coordinate bounds]
\label{lem:real_bounds}
For a real cyclic solution of~\eqref{eq:centered_recurrence}, let
\(R=\max_i|u_i|\). Whenever \(c\ge-1\),
\begin{equation}
 R\le1+\sqrt{1+c},\qquad |u_i^2-c|\le2R\quad\text{for every }i.
 \label{eq:real_bounds}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At a maximum coordinate, the recurrence gives
\(R^2-c\le2R\), hence \((R-1)^2\le1+c\) and the first bound.
At every index, the sum of the neighboring coordinates has absolute
value at most \(2R\), giving the second bound.
\end{proof}

We apply this lemma to the even branch with \(A\le0\), and to the odd
branch with \(A\le0\). The case \(A=0\) in the latter branch will
also appear in the finite certificate for uniform bookkeeping, although
it has already been settled directly.
