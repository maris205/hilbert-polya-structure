\section{Introduction and main results}\label{sec:introduction}

A finite-field orbit of a nonautonomous rational map carries two clocks:
the original update and the return of its coefficients. For the discrete
Painlev\'e I map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:torus-map-intro}
X=\frac{st}{sx-y},\qquad Y=\frac{sx}{y},\qquad T=st,
\end{equation}
the phase multiplier \(s\in\F_q^*\) has finite multiplicative order
\(r\). The natural question is whether the orbit length divided by
\(r\) admits the same upper bound as the number of points on an
elliptic curve. Here \(q\) denotes the field cardinality, whereas
\(s\) is the iterative parameter traditionally denoted by \(q\) in
the name \(q\)-Painlev\'e I.

Joshi and Roffelsen defined the resolved state space for
\eqref{eq:torus-map-intro}, constructed a matrix integral at every
root-of-unity parameter, and proposed this upper bound
\citep{JoshiRoffelsen2026}. The state space includes four accessible
exceptional affine lines. Deleting them would remove valid orbits,
including points whose line coordinate is zero. We retain exactly their
seven native branches, displayed in Table~\ref{tab:native} below.
Conjecture and theorem numbers attributed to that source in this
article refer to the explicitly identified arXiv v2 text.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform native-period bound]\label{thm:period}
Let \(k=\F_q\), choose \(s,t_0\in k^*\), and put
\(r=\ord_{k^*}(s)\). Apply the seven branches in
Table~\ref{tab:native} on all states
\begin{equation}\label{eq:state-domain-intro}
\begin{split}
\gamma&=(j,x,y,t,s),\qquad t\in t_0\langle s\rangle,\\
(j,x,y)&\in\bigl(\{0\}\times(k^*)^2\bigr)
\sqcup\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{4}\bigl(\{j\}\times\{0\}\times k\bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Every state is periodic. If \(\ell\) is its least period under one
native branch update, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-bound}
r\mid\ell,\qquad \frac{\ell}{r}\le q+1+2\sqrt q.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This proves the upper-bound part of the source's Conjecture~1.2.A.
The theorem is uniform in the finite field, the phase orbit, the order
\(r\), and the ordinary state. In particular, there is no exclusion
in characteristics two or three. The counted objects are distinct
states of the resolved system, not lengths of fixed-point schemes.

The geometric issue is stronger than the existence of an invariant.
A generic genus-one fibre does not control reducible or multiple
special fibres, and Hasse's theorem applies to smooth elliptic curves,
not to an arbitrary affine equation. We prove the following statement
on the actual compactification of the ordinary state space.

\begin{theorem}[All finite invariant fibres]\label{thm:fibres-intro}
Let \(S_{t,s}\) be the smooth rational surface obtained by the eight
blowups in Table~\ref{tab:blowups}, and let \(D\) be its reduced
eight-component boundary cycle. For every nonzero phase \(t\), the
Joshi--Roffelsen integral extends to a morphism
\[
f_t:S_{t,s}\longrightarrow\PP^1,
\qquad f_t^{-1}(\infty)=rD.
\]
Every finite geometric fibre is integral and reduced, has arithmetic
genus one, and lies entirely in \(U_{t,s}=S_{t,s}\setminus D\).
\end{theorem}

Arithmetic genus one in this statement does not assert smoothness.
When a finite fibre is singular, its normalization has genus zero and
there is exactly one geometric singular point. This gives the bound
\(q+2\) without classifying the singularity or assuming that an orbit
contains a smooth rational point.

\subsection{Prior inputs and the proof mechanism}

The original resolution, invariant, conjectured bound and local
leading term of the invariant belong to Joshi--Roffelsen
\citep[Definitions 2.1--2.2, Algorithm 1 and Theorem 3.1]{JoshiRoffelsen2026}.
The connection between roots of unity and Halphen pencils is also
established prior work. In particular, Carstea--Takenawa
\citep[Theorem 2.2 and Remark 2.3]{CarsteaTakenawa2012} give a criterion
using a complex period map. We do not specialize that analytic argument
to small characteristic. Table~\ref{tab:ownership} separates these
inputs from the all-finite-fibre argument used here.

There are three linked steps. First, the integral Picard lattice and
adjunction show that an integral curve disjoint from the boundary has
class \(dD\) for a positive integer \(d\). Second, every regular
nonconstant function on \(U\) has pole divisor \(mD\), and four
explicit face-root conditions force \(s^m=1\). Third, the source
integral has exact pole divisor \(rD\). Applying the divisibility
condition separately to the components of a finite fibre leaves room
for only one component with multiplicity one.

These are algebraic calculations on the specified surface, with
integer intersection numbers and local-ring multiplicities. They do
not use a proposed formula for singular values, a section of the
fibration, a translation law, or an identification with a spectral
curve. The underlying tools are classical; the conclusion is the
uniform all-finite-fibre statement needed for the original orbit bound.

Section~\ref{sec:states} fixes the states and proves bijectivity.
Section~\ref{sec:surface} constructs the compactification and computes
its boundary lattice. Sections~\ref{sec:poles} and~\ref{sec:integral}
establish the minimal pole order and the exact divisor of the source
integral. Section~\ref{sec:fibres} proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:fibres-intro}, and Section~\ref{sec:periods} converts
the fibre point counts into Theorem~\ref{thm:period}.
