\begin{abstract}
For the normalized planar circular restricted three-body problem with
$0<\mu\leq1/2$, we derive all five equilibria and their complete linear
spectral atlas.  Each collinear point is uniquely located in its collision-
free interval and is saddle--times--center.  At the equilateral points the
characteristic polynomial is
$\lambda^4+\lambda^2+27\mu(1-\mu)/4$.
Writing $\mu_R=(1-\sqrt{23/27})/2$, the linear flow is bounded elliptic below
$\mu_R$ and has an unstable Hamiltonian quartet above it.  At equality each
of $\pm i/\sqrt2$ has algebraic multiplicity two and geometric multiplicity
one: the linearization is defective, has linearly growing solutions, and is
\emph{not} stable.
\ifnum\CRevisionRound>0
We keep collisions, the zero-mass limit, and nonlinear stability outside the
claim and audit both signs of the triangular mixed Hessian entry.
\fi
\ifnum\CRevisionRound>1
Independent raw-Jacobian, symbolic, replay, and hostile-mutation lanes close
38 finite regression cells without replacing the all-parameter proof.
\fi
\end{abstract}

\section{Frozen normalization and claim level}
The primaries of masses $1-\mu$ and $\mu$ occupy $(-\mu,0)$ and
$(1-\mu,0)$ in the synodic frame.  For
\[
 r_1^2=(x+\mu)^2+y^2,\qquad
 r_2^2=(x-1+\mu)^2+y^2,
\]
set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:potential}
 \Om(x,y)=\frac{x^2+y^2}{2}+\frac{1-\mu}{r_1}+\frac{\mu}{r_2}.
\end{equation}
The equations, including our Coriolis convention, are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eom}
 \ddot x-2\dot y=\Om_x,\qquad
 \ddot y+2\dot x=\Om_y.
\end{equation}
Both collision points are removed.  ``Linearly stable'' below means that all
solutions of the constant-coefficient variational equation are bounded,
including at mass ratios where the two linear frequencies are resonant.  It
does not assert nonlinear stability, control resonance bifurcations, or invoke
KAM theory.

The equilateral configuration goes back to Lagrange~\cite{Lagrange1772}.
Gascheau's 1843 thesis is the first historical owner of the Newtonian
triangular stability criterion~\cite{Gascheau1843}; Routh's 1874 paper gives a
subsequent treatment and inverse-power-law generalization~\cite{Routh1874}.
A modern Hamiltonian treatment is given by Meyer, Hall, and
Offin~\cite{MHO2009}.  Neither the five points nor the threshold is claimed as
literature originality; all formulas needed for our convention are rederived
here.

\begin{theorem}[Equilibrium and linear-type atlas]\label{thm:main}
For every $0<\mu\leq1/2$, the collision-free plane has exactly five
equilibria.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item There is exactly one collinear equilibrium in each interval
 $(-\infty,-\mu)$, $(-\mu,1-\mu)$, and $(1-\mu,\infty)$.  Every one is of
 saddle--times--center type and is linearly unstable.
 \item The two remaining equilibria are
 \[
 L_{4,5}=\left(\frac12-\mu,\ \mathord\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right).
 \]
 Their characteristic polynomial is
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:trianglepoly}
 p_\mu(\lambda)=\lambda^4+\lambda^2+\frac{27}{4}\mu(1-\mu).
 \end{equation}
 With $\mu_R=(1-\sqrt{23/27})/2$, their linearized flow is bounded for
 $0<\mu<\mu_R$, defective with linear growth (hence not stable) for
 $\mu=\mu_R$, and exponentially unstable for $\mu_R<\mu\leq1/2$.
\end{enumerate}
At equality, each eigenvalue $\pm i/\sqrt2$ has algebraic multiplicity two
and geometric multiplicity one.
\end{theorem}

\section{Why there are exactly five points}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S}
 S(x,y)=\frac{1-\mu}{r_1^3}+\frac{\mu}{r_2^3}.
\end{equation}
Direct differentiation gives $\Om_y=y(1-S)$.  Thus an equilibrium either has
$y=0$ or satisfies $S=1$.

On the axis, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f}
 f_\mu(x)=x-(1-\mu)\frac{x+\mu}{|x+\mu|^3}
              -\mu\frac{x-1+\mu}{|x-1+\mu|^3}.
\end{equation}
Away from collisions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monotone}
 f_\mu'(x)=1+2\left(\frac{1-\mu}{|x+\mu|^3}
                    +\frac{\mu}{|x-1+\mu|^3}\right)>0.
\end{equation}
On $(-\infty,-\mu)$ its limiting signs are negative and positive; on
$(-\mu,1-\mu)$ they are negative and positive; and on
$(1-\mu,\infty)$ they are negative and positive.  The intermediate value
theorem and~\eqref{eq:monotone} give precisely three roots.

If $y\ne0$, then $S=1$.  Writing $u=r_1^{-3}$ and $w=r_2^{-3}$, a direct
rearrangement yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:offaxis}
 \Om_x-x(1-S)=\mu(1-\mu)(w-u).
\end{equation}
Because $0<\mu<1$, equilibrium implies $r_1=r_2$, hence
$x=1/2-\mu$.  Now $S=1$ becomes $r_1=r_2=1$, so $y^2=3/4$.  These are
exactly $L_4$ and $L_5$, proving the count.

\section{The raw rotating-frame determinant}
At any equilibrium let
$a=\Om_{xx}$, $c=\Om_{yy}$, and $d=\Om_{xy}$.  In coordinates
$(\xi,\eta,u,w)=(\delta x,\delta y,\delta\dot x,\delta\dot y)$ the raw
variational matrix is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
 M=\begin{pmatrix}
 0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&1\\a&d&0&2\\d&c&-2&0
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Expanding, without appealing to a stability label,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalpoly}
 \det(\lambda I-M)=
 \lambda^4+(4-a-c)\lambda^2+ac-d^2.
\end{equation}

At a collinear root, $d=0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:colhess}
 a=1+2S,\qquad c=1-S,
\end{equation}
where now $S$ is~\eqref{eq:S} at $y=0$.  We verify $S>1$ on all three
branches.  Between the primaries, $r_1+r_2=1$, so $r_1,r_2<1$ and
$S>(1-\mu)+\mu=1$.  To the right, write $d=x-1+\mu>0$.  At
$d=\mu^{1/3}$, equation~\eqref{eq:f} becomes
\[
 f_\mu=(1-\mu)\{1-(1+d)^{-2}\}>0.
\]
Since $f_\mu$ is increasing in $x$ and tends to $-\infty$ at the right side
of the collision, its root has $d<\mu^{1/3}$; hence
$S>\mu/d^3>1$.  To the left, put $d=-x-\mu>0$.  At
$d=(1-\mu)^{1/3}$ one obtains
\[
 f_\mu=-\mu\{1-(1+d)^{-2}\}<0.
\]
Here increasing $d$ decreases $x$, so the root has
$d<(1-\mu)^{1/3}$ and $S>(1-\mu)/d^3>1$.
Substitution in~\eqref{eq:generalpoly} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:colpoly}
 \lambda^4+(2-S)\lambda^2+(1+2S)(1-S).
\end{equation}
Its constant term is negative.  The two roots in $z=\lambda^2$ have opposite
signs, so there is one real pair and one imaginary pair.  This proves the
collinear assertion.

At either triangular point direct differentiation of~\eqref{eq:potential}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trihess}
 a=\frac34,\qquad c=\frac94,\qquad
 d=\mathord\pm\frac{3\sqrt3}{4}(1-2\mu).
\end{equation}
Thus $a+c=3$ and $ac-d^2=27\mu(1-\mu)/4$, proving
\eqref{eq:trianglepoly} for both $L_4$ and $L_5$.

\section{Open chambers and the critical Jordan structure}
Set $z=\lambda^2$.  The two roots of~\eqref{eq:trianglepoly} are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zroots}
 z_\pm=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-27\mu(1-\mu)}}{2}.
\end{equation}
On $(0,1/2]$, the expression $27\mu(1-\mu)$ is strictly increasing until
the included endpoint and equals one precisely at $\mu_R$.  Below $\mu_R$,
both $z_\pm$ are distinct and negative, giving four distinct imaginary
eigenvalues and a bounded diagonalizable linear flow.  Above it, the $z$
roots are nonreal conjugates; their square roots form a quartet
$\{\pm\alpha\pm i\beta\}$ with $\alpha,\beta>0$, giving exponential growth.

At equality,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:criticalpoly}
 p_{\mu_R}(\lambda)=(\lambda^2+\tfrac12)^2.
\end{equation}
Algebraic multiplicity is not enough to decide boundedness.  For an
eigenvalue $\lambda$, the position components solve
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pencil}
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \lambda^2-a&-2\lambda-d\\2\lambda-d&\lambda^2-c
 \end{pmatrix}\binom{\xi}{\eta}=0.
\end{equation}
At $\lambda=\pm i/\sqrt2$ the determinant vanishes.  The matrix cannot be
zero: its two off-diagonal entries would imply simultaneously
$2\lambda=-d$ and $2\lambda=d$, hence $\lambda=0$.  Therefore it has rank
one and a one-dimensional kernel.  Velocities are then uniquely
$(u,w)=\lambda(\xi,\eta)$, so each eigenspace of $M$ has dimension one.
Each double eigenvalue consequently has a nontrivial size-two Jordan block.
The exponential contains $t e^{\pm it/\sqrt2}$ terms and is unbounded.  This
proves the critical statement and, in particular, excludes linear stability.

\section{Boundary ledger and interpretation}
The lower mass boundary $\mu=0$ is excluded for a geometric reason, not
merely to avoid division by a parameter.  Then
$\Om=(x^2+y^2)/2+(x^2+y^2)^{-1/2}$ and
$\nabla\Om=(1-r^{-3})(x,y)$, so the entire unit circle is a continuum of
equilibria.  The five-isolated-point theorem cannot extend to that face.

The endpoint $\mu=1/2$ is included.  The two triangular points persist, but
$27\mu(1-\mu)>1$, so they lie in the quartet chamber.  At all positive mass
ratios the primary positions remain singular and are not silently counted as
solutions of $\nabla\Om=0$.

For $0<\mu<\mu_R$, ``bounded elliptic'' is exactly a statement about the
constant linear system $\dot z=Mz$ and remains true at resonant mass ratios in
that open chamber.  The theorem does not resolve the nonlinear consequences
of resonance, construct Lyapunov periodic families, or prove nonlinear/KAM
stability.  At $\mu_R$, even the linear boundedness statement fails because
of the Jordan factor.  Calling equality ``spectrally stable'' without this
qualification would conceal real linear growth and is not done here.

\section{Two sign checks for the triangular pair}
Reflection $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$ takes $L_4$ to $L_5$.  It fixes the diagonal
Hessian entries and reverses $d$ in~\eqref{eq:trihess}.  Formula
\eqref{eq:generalpoly} depends on $d^2$, so both points share the same
eigenvalues.  This is more than a symmetry slogan: the executable checker
constructs both raw matrices for every tested $\mu$, and the symbolic lane
differentiates~\eqref{eq:potential} at both signs independently.

flow only.  Spatial motion, eccentric primaries, collision regularization,
periodic-orbit families, nonlinear stability, and transport are outside
