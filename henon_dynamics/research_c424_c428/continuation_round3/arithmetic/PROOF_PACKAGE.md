## 1. Generic fixed schemes and simultaneous formal labels

Use `K=Q(c)` and `H_c(x,y)=(y,y^2+c-x)`. For `n>=1`, with indices modulo
`n`, consider the cyclic equations

\[
 f_i=X_i^2+c-X_{i-1}-X_{i+1}=0\quad(0\leq i<n). \tag{1}
\]

For `n=1` the two neighbors both mean `X_0`; for `n=2` they both mean the
other variable. Thus (1) also treats these small periods without dropping
the repeated neighbor. The tuple of coordinates identifies this scheme
with `Fix(H_c^n)`; for `n=1` the point is `(X_0,X_0)`, and for `n>=2`
the point is `(X_0,X_1)`. Conversely the recurrence recovers the whole tuple.

Choose any monomial order refining total degree. The leading monomials
of the `f_i` are the pairwise coprime `X_i^2`. The coprime-leading-term
criterion gives a Gröbner basis. For completeness, the S-polynomial for
`i != j` is obtained from
`X_j^2 f_i - X_i^2 f_j`; replacing each square by its degree-one tail
cancels the two tail products. Hence it reduces to zero. The standard
monomials are exactly the squarefree monomials, so the quotient algebra
has dimension `2^n` over `K`.

Embed `K` in `Q((s))` by

\[
 c=-s^{-2},\qquad X_i=s^{-1}U_i.
\]

Equations (1) become

\[
 U_i^2-1-s(U_{i-1}+U_{i+1})=0. \tag{2}
\]

For each sign word `epsilon=(epsilon_0,...,epsilon_{n-1})` in
`{+1,-1}^n`, there is a unique solution of (2) with

\[
 U_i(s)\in\mathbb Q[[s]],\qquad U_i(0)=\epsilon_i. \tag{3}
\]

Indeed, write `U_i=epsilon_i+sum_{k>=1}u_{i,k}s^k`. At coefficient `s^k`,
the only new unknown in the square is `2 epsilon_i u_{i,k}`. Every other
term uses coefficients of lower order. This recursively determines all
coefficients in `Q`, and proves existence and uniqueness coefficient by
coefficient. Equivalently, the formal Jacobian at `s=0` is the invertible
diagonal matrix with entries `2 epsilon_i`.

The `2^n` sign choices give distinct tuples, since their leading terms
are distinct. They are algebraic over `K`: evaluation factors through
the finite algebra above. They exhaust its dimension. Consequently the
generic fixed scheme is reduced and has exactly `2^n` ordinary geometric
points. This conclusion is over `Q(c)`, not at every specialization.

Shifting the indices of a solution shifts its sign word. By uniqueness,
the point has least period exactly the least shift period of its word.
Therefore

\[
 |P_n|=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)2^d. \tag{4}
\]

The point reversor `R(x,y)=(y,x)` acts on a tuple by
`X_i -> X_{1-i}` and on its word by the same reflection. The chosen
reflection convention matters only for labelling the two even-period
reflection classes, not for the generated dihedral group.

Counting words or reconstructing (4) is not a new claimed arithmetic
increment; P60/P62 and the classical fixed-point-count framework are
deducted in the source comparison.

## 2. Regularity and inertia at infinity at every finite level

For `N>=1`, let `K_N` be the field generated by all primitive periodic
coordinates of periods at most `N`. The embeddings constructed above
can be made simultaneously in the same field `Q((s))`: each tuple is
fixed by its finite word, and repeating a word gives the same solution
by uniqueness. Thus

\[
 K\subset K_N\subset\mathbb Q((s)). \tag{5}
\]

The extension `K_N/K` is finite and Galois, since the finite sets of
periodic points are stable under the absolute Galois group and all of
their coordinates are included.

**Constant-field claim.** The algebraic closure of `Q` in `Q((s))` is
`Q`. To verify it directly, a nonzero Laurent series algebraic over `Q`
has valuation zero: in a polynomial equation with constant nonzero
coefficients and nonzero constant term, a nonzero valuation would make
the lowest-valuation term unique. Its constant coefficient `a_0` is
rational. If the series differed from `a_0`, their difference would be
a nonzero element algebraic over `Q` of positive valuation, contradicting
the same argument. Hence the series is exactly `a_0`.

It follows from (5) that the constant field of `K_N` is `Q`. In
characteristic zero this is a regular function-field extension; base
extension to `Qbar` preserves its degree. Accordingly the geometric and
arithmetic Galois groups have the same permutation action:

\[
 \operatorname{Gal}(K_N\overline{\mathbb Q}/\overline{\mathbb Q}(c))
 \simeq \operatorname{Gal}(K_N/\mathbb Q(c)). \tag{6}
\]

This is not a statement about the Galois groups of rational specializations.

The completion of the base at `c=infinity` is
`Q((s^2))`, since `-1/c=s^2`. The completion of the place of `K_N`
selected by (5) lies between `Q((s^2))` and `Q((s))`. It is not the
smaller field: every periodic coordinate constructed in Section 1 has
leading term `epsilon_i s^{-1}`, hence odd `s`-valuation. Because the
ambient extension has degree two, the completed field is exactly

\[
 (K_N)_\infty=\mathbb Q((s)),\qquad e=2,\quad f=1. \tag{7}
\]

The decomposition group and inertia group at this place both have order
two. Its nontrivial element is induced by `s -> -s`. Applied to
`X_i(s)=s^{-1}U_i(s)`, it has leading term `-epsilon_i s^{-1}`;
uniqueness in Section 1 shows that it takes the entire word `epsilon`
to `-epsilon`. This is a **single simultaneous involution** on every
period layer. It commutes with shifting and reflection. Since every
other place above infinity is Galois-conjugate, all those residue fields
are also `Q`, and there are `[K_N:K]/2` such places.

Thus the complete infinity data are known at all finite joint levels.
The unknown degree `[K_N:K]` and finite-parameter ramification are not
determined by this observation. In particular, one inertia generator
cannot establish the full global group.

## 3. The necessary symmetry upper bound

Every `K`-automorphism of a splitting field commutes with `H_c` and `R`,
because both maps have coefficients in `K`. On `P_n`, the image therefore
lies in

\[
 \operatorname{Cent}_{\operatorname{Sym}(P_n)}
       (\langle H_c,R\rangle). \tag{8}
\]

The periodic-point action of the displayed generators is the usual
dihedral action on the primitive binary words of length `n`, by Section 1.
Equation (8) is an elementary equivariance upper bound. It neither implies
transitivity between permitted blocks nor independence of their fields.
The next section shows that even equality in this upper bound fails.

## 4. Exact period four: complete radical fields

Put `a^2=-c`; `a` is transcendental over `Q`. A period-dividing-four
coordinate tuple `(u,v,w,z)` satisfies

\[
 u+w=v^2+c=z^2+c,\qquad v+z=u^2+c=w^2+c.
\]

Consequently

\[
 (v-z)(v+z)=0,\qquad (u-w)(u+w)=0. \tag{9}
\]

For exact period four, both pairs cannot be equal, since that would give
period at most two. If one opposite pair is equal, rotate the tuple so
that `u=w`; the other pair is then opposite. If both are opposite, use
the second case below. Thus the following cases exhaust exact period four.

### A. One equal opposite pair

For each `t` with `t^2=-c`, put `b_t^2=t(t+2)`. The tuple is

\[
 (t,b_t,t,-b_t). \tag{10}
\]

Its four cyclic rotations are distinct over the generic field. Changing
the sign of `b_t` just changes the phase by two. The two possible values
`t=a,-a` give two different four-cycles: an orbit can identify its unique
equal opposite value `t`. There are eight points in this block.

### B. Both opposite pairs are opposite

Equations (9) give `u^2=v^2=-c`. There is one four-cycle, represented by

\[
 (a,a,-a,-a). \tag{11}
\]

Its cyclic rotations contain all choices and are distinct. This block
has four points and splitting field

\[
 L_B=K(a). \tag{12}
\]

No generic exceptional coincidences were omitted: `a`, `a+2`, and `a-2`
are nonzero in `Q(a)`. The twelve points agree with the primitive count
`2^4-2^2=12` but were exhausted directly by (9).

Choose radicals

\[
 b^2=a(a+2),\qquad d^2=a(a-2).
\]

The field of block A is exactly

\[
 L_A=K(a,b,d). \tag{13}
\]

In `Q(a)`, the square classes of `a(a+2)` and `a(a-2)` are independent:
valuation at `a=-2` detects the first exponent modulo two and valuation
at `a=2` detects the second. Hence `[L_A:Q(a)]=4`; since
`[Q(a):Q(c)]=2`, we have `[L_A:K]=8`.

Changing the signs of `b` and `d` independently gives a subgroup `C2^2`.
The involution

\[
 a\longmapsto-a,\qquad b\longmapsto d,\qquad d\longmapsto b
\]

is also a `K`-automorphism and exchanges these two sign changes. These
eight automorphisms account for the full degree, proving

\[
 \operatorname{Gal}(L_A/K)\simeq C_2^2\rtimes C_2\simeq D_4,
 \qquad |D_4|=8. \tag{14}
\]

Since `L_B=K(a)` is already contained in `L_A`, the splitting field `L_4`
of **all** exact period-four points is `L_A`, not a further quadratic
extension. Thus `Gal(L_4/K)` has order eight.

## 5. Why the permitted centralizer has order sixteen

For a tuple (10), the map `H_c^{-1}R` fixes the corresponding point;
the stabilizing reflections on its four-cycle have odd exponent in
`H_c^j R`. For (11), `R` itself fixes `(a,a)`; the stabilizing reflections
have even exponent. These are the two nonconjugate reflection classes
in the order-eight dihedral group. A permutation commuting with both
generators must preserve the union A and the union B.

On a transitive four-point orbit with a reflection stabilizer, the group
of dihedral-equivariant permutations has order two: its value at one
point must be one of the two points fixed by that same reflection, and
either choice extends uniquely. Block A has two isomorphic such orbits,
which can also be exchanged. Its full equivariant permutation group is
therefore `C2 wr S2`, of order eight. Block B has one orbit of the other
type, giving a further independent `C2` in the abstract centralizer.
Thus the centralizer in (8), for `n=4`, is

\[
 (C_2\wr S_2)\times C_2,\qquad\text{of order }16. \tag{15}
\]

In the actual Galois image, the last `C2` is not independent. On block B,
the nontrivial action sends `a` to `-a`. This occurs exactly when the two
A-cycles labelled by their repeated value `a` and `-a` are exchanged.
In (15), the image is the graph of the quotient
`C2 wr S2 -> S2 = C2`, not the whole product. This proves the strict
index-two obstruction to the naive maximal-centralizer hypothesis.

This is a radical-field consequence of classical complete period-four
algebra, not an all-period monodromy theorem or a novelty-certified
low-period result. In particular, a scalar orbital polynomial can repeat
coordinate values: (11) has four distinct affine phase points although
its scalar coordinate polynomial is `(X^2-a^2)^2`. Treating that repeated
scalar polynomial as a nonreduced affine periodic scheme would be wrong.
