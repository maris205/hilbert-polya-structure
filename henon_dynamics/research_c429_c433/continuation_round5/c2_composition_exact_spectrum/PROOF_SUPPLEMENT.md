## 1. Claim and assumptions

Let $\operatorname{TA}_2(\mathbb Z)$ be the subgroup of polynomial automorphisms generated by all integral affine automorphisms and all integral triangular automorphisms. Integral affine linear parts belong to $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z)$; triangular diagonal coefficients are units $\pm1$. Let $\mathcal H$ be the semigroup of nonempty finite ordered words in

$$
H_{p,\varepsilon}(x,y)=(y,p(y)-\varepsilon x),
\qquad p\in\mathbb Z[t],\quad\deg p\ge2,\quad\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}.
$$

For any family $\mathcal F$ of maps of $\mathbb Z^2$, write

$$
\operatorname{Spec}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathcal F)
=\{n\ge1:\exists T\in\mathcal F,\ P\in\mathbb Z^2,
\operatorname{per}_T(P)=n\}.
$$

**Lemma.**

$$
\operatorname{Spec}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathcal H)
=\operatorname{Spec}_{\mathbb Z}\bigl(\operatorname{TA}_2(\mathbb Z)\bigr).
$$

More precisely, every finite cycle of an integral tame automorphism is preserved, point for point and with its same native permutation, by some word in $\mathcal H$.

The word length and degrees may depend on the given cycle. No fixed-degree, fixed-length or global conjugacy conclusion is asserted. Both sides use the full selected map as one tick.

## 2. Strategy and dependency map

1. Express integral tame generators as finite words in $H_{p,\varepsilon}$ with the degree restriction temporarily removed.
2. For a chosen finite native orbit, record the finite second-coordinate input support of each factor.
3. Raise the degree of each low-degree polynomial by adding an integral polynomial that vanishes on that factor's entire input support.
4. Prove factor-by-factor agreement on every point of the native orbit, hence equality of its least period.

The source-owned ingredients are integral affine generation by elementary matrices and elementary vanishing-polynomial interpolation. No interpolation theorem for arbitrary permutations of integral points is being invoked.

## 3. Proof

**Step 1: elementary generators.** Permit $p\in\mathbb Z[t]$ of arbitrary degree for this step, including the zero polynomial. Put

$$
S=H_{0,-1},\qquad J=H_{0,1},
$$

so $S(x,y)=(y,x)$ and $J(x,y)=(y,-x)$. Direct substitution gives

$$
S\circ H_{p,-1}(x,y)=(x+p(y),y)=:U_p(x,y),
$$

$$
H_{p,-1}\circ S(x,y)=(x,y+p(x))=:V_p(x,y).
$$

Also $S\circ J(x,y)=(-x,y)$ and $J\circ S(x,y)=(x,-y)$. Constant $p$ gives translations. Linear $p$ gives elementary integer shears. The Euclidean algorithm reduces every unimodular integer matrix to a signed diagonal matrix by integer row additions and swaps, so elementary shears, swaps and these sign changes generate $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z)$. Reversing the finite row-reduction word gives an expression for the original linear map. Therefore every integral affine automorphism is a finite word in the unrestricted $H_{p,\varepsilon}$.

An upper triangular polynomial automorphism is a composition of a shear $U_p$, diagonal sign changes and translations; a lower triangular one is obtained by conjugation by $S$. Thus every element of $\operatorname{TA}_2(\mathbb Z)$ is a finite word in unrestricted $H_{p,\varepsilon}$. If an expression has zero factors, replace it by the nonempty identity expression $S\circ S$.

Conversely, $H_{p,-1}=S\circ U_p$ is tame and $H_{p,1}=H_{p,-1}\circ((-x),y)$ is tame. Every restricted word in $\mathcal H$ is therefore integral tame. This proves one spectrum inclusion.

**Step 2: freeze one actual cycle and its intermediate supports.** Let $T\in\operatorname{TA}_2(\mathbb Z)$ and let

$$
C=\{P,T(P),\ldots,T^{n-1}(P)\}
$$

be a cycle of least period $n$. Choose an unrestricted expression

$$
T=G_{s-1}\circ\cdots\circ G_0,
\qquad G_i=H_{p_i,\varepsilon_i},\qquad s\ge1.
$$

Define the stage maps $A_0=\operatorname{id}$ and $A_i=G_{i-1}\circ\cdots\circ G_0$ for $i\ge1$. For each $0\le i<s$, let

$$
E_i=\{\pi_2(A_i(Q)):Q\in C\}\subset\mathbb Z,
$$

where $\pi_2(x,y)=y$. Each $E_i$ is finite and nonempty, because $C$ is finite and nonempty and all intermediate maps have integral coefficients.

**Step 3: degree padding.** If $\deg p_i\ge2$, put $\widetilde p_i=p_i$. For all other $p_i$, including zero, set

$$
k_i=\max(0,2-|E_i|),\qquad
\widetilde p_i(t)=p_i(t)+t^{k_i}\prod_{e\in E_i}(t-e).
$$

The added polynomial is monic of degree $k_i+|E_i|\ge2$, and $p_i$ has degree at most one, so no leading cancellation occurs. Hence $\widetilde p_i\in\mathbb Z[t]$ has degree at least two. It agrees with $p_i$ at every $e\in E_i$, regardless of whether $0\in E_i$. Define

$$
\widetilde G_i=H_{\widetilde p_i,\varepsilon_i},\qquad
W=\widetilde G_{s-1}\circ\cdots\circ\widetilde G_0\in\mathcal H.
$$

**Step 4: equality of the native permutation.** For each $Q\in C$, induction on $i$ shows

$$
\widetilde G_{i-1}\circ\cdots\circ\widetilde G_0(Q)=A_i(Q).
$$

The base case $i=0$ is the identity. If the equality holds at stage $i$, the common state's second coordinate belongs to $E_i$, so $\widetilde p_i$ and $p_i$ take the same value there. Their signs also agree. Applying the two factor maps therefore gives equality at stage $i+1$. At $i=s$ this proves $W(Q)=T(Q)$ for every $Q\in C$.

Since $T$ permutes $C$ cyclically, repeated native applications of $W$ traverse the same $n$ pairwise distinct states in the same order. Thus $\operatorname{per}_W(P)=n$. The other spectrum inclusion follows. $\square$

## 5. The new exclusion and its derivative-parity character

**Theorem.** No word $F\in\mathcal H$ admits an integer point of native least period 16. More strongly, no such word admits a $\mathbb Z_2^2$ point of native least period 16. There is no restriction on word length, degrees, coefficient heights or determinant-sign pattern.

For this theorem one complete selected word remains one native tick. Taking $G=F^4$ below is only a proof device applied to a hypothetical native 16-cycle, not a redefinition of the requested period.

Let $V=\mathbb F_2^2$, and let $V^*=V\setminus\{0\}$, a three-element set. Every $A\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb F_2)$ permutes $V^*$. Write

$$
\sigma(A)\in\{1,-1\}
$$

for the ordinary sign of this permutation. It is a multiplicative homomorphism. The action identifies $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb F_2)$ with the symmetric group on $V^*$: it is faithful and both groups have six elements. Consequently $\sigma(A)=1$ means either $A=I$ or $A$ has order three. In the latter case

$$
A^2+A+I=0.
$$

For example, this identity follows because the three vectors $u,Au,A^2u$ are precisely the three distinct nonzero elements of $V$ for every $u\ne0$, and their sum is zero.

For an integral polynomial automorphism $T$ with integral polynomial inverse define

$$
\chi(T)=\prod_{q\in V}\sigma(D\overline T(q)),
$$

where reduction of the polynomial map and reduction of its formal derivative commute. Here $\overline T$ permutes all four points of $V$, and its derivative is invertible at every point. The chain rule and the permutation of the base points give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\chi(T\circ U)
&=\prod_{q\in V}
\sigma\bigl(D\overline T(\overline U(q))D\overline U(q)\bigr)\\
&=\left(\prod_{q\in V}\sigma(D\overline T(\overline U(q)))\right)
\left(\prod_{q\in V}\sigma(D\overline U(q))\right)\\
&=\chi(T)\chi(U).
\end{aligned}
$$

For every allowed Hénon factor, both determinant signs reduce to the same derivative shape:

$$
D\overline H_{p,\varepsilon}(x,y)
=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&\overline {p'}(y)\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For each fixed $y\in\mathbb F_2$ this matrix occurs at two points, one for each $x$. Its sign is therefore squared in the product defining $\chi$. Thus

$$
\chi(H_{p,\varepsilon})=1,
\qquad \chi(F)=1\quad\text{for every }F\in\mathcal H.
$$

This argument uses the actual derivative polynomial, not only the permutation of the four residue points. In particular, two polynomials with the same values modulo two are not assumed to have the same derivatives.

## 6. Local four-cycle obstruction with even derivative sign

For a vector $z\in\mathbb Z_2^2$, set

$$
\nu(z)=\min(v_2(z_1),v_2(z_2)),\qquad \nu(0)=+\infty.
$$

**Local lemma.** Let $g\in\mathbb Z_2[x,y]^2$ have a constant unit Jacobian determinant. Suppose $g$ has a cycle of least period four, all of whose points have the same image in $V$. If $z_0$ is one point of that cycle, then

$$
\sigma(Dg(z_0)\bmod2)=-1.
$$

**Proof: normalization.** Write the cycle as $z_0,z_1,z_2,z_3$. An integral polynomial map is congruence preserving, so

$$
\nu(z_{i+2}-z_{i+1})\ge\nu(z_{i+1}-z_i)
$$

for cyclic indices. Going once around the cycle forces all four finite valuations of successive differences to be equal. Call the common value $d\ge1$. Every $z_i-z_0$ is a sum of successive differences, so belongs to $2^d\mathbb Z_2^2$.

Put $a=2^{d-1}$ and define

$$
\widetilde g(X)=\frac{g(z_0+aX)-z_0}{a}.
$$

Its constant coefficient is $(z_1-z_0)/a\in2\mathbb Z_2^2$. Each coefficient of total degree $k\ge1$ in the numerator has a factor $a^k$, and division by $a$ leaves the integral factor $a^{k-1}$. Thus $\widetilde g$ has integral coefficients. Its Jacobian determinant is the same constant unit as that of $g$, and $D\widetilde g(0)=Dg(z_0)$. Its four cycle points lie in $2\mathbb Z_2^2$, its first point is zero, and its first image $v$ satisfies $\nu(v)=1$. The affine change is injective, so the least period is still four.

We may therefore replace $g$ by this normalized map, suppress the tilde, and write

$$
g(0)=v,\qquad \nu(v)=1,\qquad A=Dg(0),\qquad \overline A=A\bmod2.
$$

Assume for contradiction that $\sigma(\overline A)=1$.

**Case 1: $\overline A$ has order three.** All four cycle points are even. Taylor expansion at zero therefore gives

$$
g(z)\equiv v+Az\pmod4
$$

at every cycle point. Iterating this congruence four times and using $g^4(0)=0$ yields

$$
0\equiv(I+A+A^2+A^3)v\pmod4.
$$

Let $u=(v/2)\bmod2\ne0$. Dividing by two gives

$$
0=(I+\overline A+\overline A^2+\overline A^3)u=u,
$$

since $\overline A^3=I$ and $\overline A^2+\overline A=I$. This is impossible.

**Case 2: $\overline A=I$.** Expand through total degree two:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_1(x,y)&=v_1+ax+by+cx^2+dxy+ey^2+R_1(x,y),\\
g_2(x,y)&=v_2+fx+hy+ix^2+jxy+ky^2+R_2(x,y),
\end{aligned}
$$

where all coefficients belong to $\mathbb Z_2$ and every monomial of $R_1,R_2$ has total degree at least three. Here $a,h$ are odd and $b,f$ are even. The coefficient of $x$ in the Jacobian determinant is

$$
aj+2ch-2bi-df\equiv j\pmod2,
$$

and the coefficient of $y$ is

$$
2ak+dh-bj-2ef\equiv d\pmod2.
$$

The determinant is constant, so both coefficients vanish. Hence both mixed quadratic coefficients $d,j$ are even.

For every $z\in2\mathbb Z_2^2$ it now follows that

$$
Dg(z)\equiv A\pmod4.
$$

Indeed, derivatives of pure quadratic terms contain a factor two times an even coordinate; derivatives of mixed quadratic terms contain an even coefficient times an even coordinate; and derivatives of terms of degree at least three have total degree at least two in even coordinates. Each contribution is divisible by four.

Set $h_0=g^2$ and $w=h_0(0)$. The chain rule gives

$$
Dh_0(0)=Dg(v)A\equiv A^2\equiv I\pmod4,
$$

because $A=I+2B$ for an integral matrix $B$. Also

$$
w=g(v)\equiv(I+A)v\equiv0\pmod4.
$$

If $w=0$, zero already has period at most two, contradicting the assumed least period four. Thus $w\ne0$. Put $e_0=\nu(w)\ge2$, and write $Dh_0(0)=I+4C$ with $C$ integral. Taylor expansion of the integral polynomial map $h_0$ at zero gives

$$
0=h_0(w)=2w+4Cw+R(w),
\qquad R(w)\in2^{2e_0}\mathbb Z_2^2.
$$

Since $2e_0\ge e_0+2$, the two terms after $2w$ lie in $2^{e_0+2}\mathbb Z_2^2$. But some coordinate of $2w$ has valuation exactly $e_0+1$, so the right side cannot vanish. This is the required contradiction. Both possible even-sign cases are excluded. $\square$

## 7. Proof of the uniform native 16-exclusion

Suppose $F\in\mathcal H$ and $P\in\mathbb Z_2^2$ has least period 16. Let $m$ be the period of its reduction under the permutation $\overline F$ of $V$. Then $m\mid16$ and $m\le4$, so

$$
m\in\{1,2,4\}.
$$

Set $G=F^4$. The four points

$$
P,\quad F^4(P),\quad F^8(P),\quad F^{12}(P)
$$

are distinct and form a native four-cycle for $G$. They all reduce to the same point of $V$, because $m\mid4$. Moreover,

$$
\det DG=(\det DF)^4=1,
$$

since each original factor has constant determinant $\varepsilon_i\in\{1,-1\}$.

It remains to compute the derivative sign required by the local lemma. Let $q=P\bmod2$, let $q_j=\overline F^j(q)$, and write

$$
s_j=\sigma(D\overline F(q_j))\in\{1,-1\}.
$$

The chain rule gives

$$
\sigma(DG(P)\bmod2)=s_0s_1s_2s_3.
$$

- If $m=1$, this product is $s_0^4=1$.
- If $m=2$, it is $(s_0s_1)^2=1$.
- If $m=4$, the four $q_j$ exhaust $V$, so it is $\chi(F)=1$ by Section 5.

Thus the sign is even in every possible residue case. Section 6 says that a local four-cycle with constant unit Jacobian must instead have odd derivative sign. This contradiction proves the theorem. $\square$

No local-cycle upper theorem is needed for this proof. For source comparison only, Pezda's 2002 Lemma 4.2 was actually reread: it excludes an eight-cycle for a polynomial map of $\mathbb Z_2^2$ when all its points are congruent modulo two. That result concerns general polynomial maps; it is neither a premise of the simplified argument above nor claimed as our result. The upper spectrum $\mathcal P$ remains Pezda-owned.
