\section{The Wang class at every original completion}\label{sec:places}

We give the full arithmetic check, including the dyadic and infinite
places. The exceptional class itself is classical; the precise field,
exponent, and class appear in the remark following Proposition~2.1
of the accessible preprint of \cite{wang2015grunwald}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:wang}
For $K=\Q(\sqrt7)$ one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wang}
  16\in K_v^{\times8}\quad\text{for every }v\in\Omega_K,
  \qquad 16\notin K^{\times8}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First let $p$ be an odd rational prime. At least one of
$2,-2,-1$ is a square modulo $p$.
To see this, write $\chi$ for the quadratic character of
$\mathbb F_p^\times$. If neither $2$ nor $-1$ is a square,
then $\chi(-2)=\chi(-1)\chi(2)=1$.
These three elements are nonzero modulo $p$, so each square
root found there lifts to $\Q_p$ by Hensel's lemma:
the derivative of $X^2-b$ is nonzero at a nonzero root modulo
the odd prime $p$.
If $r^2=2$ or $r^2=-2$, then $r^8=16$.
If $i^2=-1$, then
\[
  (1+i)^2=2i,\qquad (1+i)^4=-4,\qquad (1+i)^8=16.
\]
Thus $\Q_p$ contains an eighth root of $16$ for every odd $p$.
Each completion $K_v$ above $p$ contains $\Q_p$, proving the
required statement at all these places, including those above
the ramified prime seven.

Next consider the rational prime two. The element $7$ is not
a square in $\Q_2$: an odd square is congruent to $1$ modulo
$8$. Hence
\[
  K\otimes_\Q\Q_2=\Q_2(\sqrt7)
\]
is a field, and $K$ has exactly one place above two.
On the other hand $-7$ is a square in $\Q_2$. The strong Hensel
criterion for $f(X)=X^2+7$ at $X=1$ applies because
\[
  v_2(f(1))=3>2v_2(f'(1))=2,
\]
where $v_2(2)=1$.
Choose $b\in\Q_2$ with $b^2=-7$. In the original dyadic
completion, $i=\sqrt7/b$ satisfies $i^2=-1$.
Consequently $1+i$ is an eighth root of $16$ there.
Equivalently, that completion is $\Q_2(i)$.

The field $K$ is real quadratic. Both infinite completions
are $\R$, and each contains $t=\sqrt2$ with $t^8=16$.
There are no complex places.

Finally, any real solution of $t^8=16$ is $\sqrt2$ or $-\sqrt2$.
Neither lies in $\Q(\sqrt7)$.
Indeed, if $(b+c\sqrt7)^2=2$ with $b,c\in\Q$, comparison
of the coefficient of $\sqrt7$ forces $bc=0$.
The two alternatives would require a rational square to equal
$2$ or $2/7$, respectively. Both numbers have odd $2$-adic
valuation, which is impossible for a rational square.
This proves \eqref{eq:wang}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:counterexample}]
Take $a=4$ in \eqref{eq:family}; the resulting $F_a$ is the
word \eqref{eq:counterexample}, and $a^2=16$.
At each original place $v$, Proposition~\ref{prop:wang}
supplies $t_v\in K_v^\times$ with $t_v^8=16$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:local-construction} gives
\[
  R_v(x,y)=(t_v^{-1}y,t_vx),\qquad
  R_v^2=\id,\qquad R_vFR_v^{-1}=F^{-1}
\]
over that same $K_v$.
If any polynomial reversor existed over $K$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:family} would force an eighth root of $16$
in $K$, contrary to Proposition~\ref{prop:wang}.
This excludes all global polynomial reversors, not only the
local affine formulas.
\end{proof}
