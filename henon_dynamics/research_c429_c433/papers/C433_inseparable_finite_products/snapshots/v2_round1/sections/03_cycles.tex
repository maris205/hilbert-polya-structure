\section{Simple returns, annihilators, and visibility}\label{sec:cycles}

By the chain rule, for every $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simple}
 (f^{\circ n})'=0,\qquad F_n'=-1.
\end{equation}
Thus $F_n$ has exactly $d^n$ distinct roots. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:badsets}
 \Bcal_n=\{x\in k:F_n(x)=0,\ H_n(x)\ne0\},\qquad
 \Bcal=\bigcup_{n\ge1}\Bcal_n.
\end{equation}
Each root of $F_n$ has ordinary least period dividing $n$.
Cyclically permuting the numerator factors, and separately the
denominator factors, shows that $H_n$ is constant along its cycle.
Hence $\Bcal_n$ is a union of full native cycles and is permuted
by $f$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ideal}
For every $S\in k[x]$ and $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:annihilator}
 F_n\mid SH_n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 S(x)=0\text{ for every }x\in\Bcal_n.
\end{equation}
Moreover, $I_g\ne(0)$ if and only if $\Bcal$ is finite, and these
conditions are equivalent to $\CP$. When $\Bcal$ is finite,
$I_g$ is generated by $\prod_{x\in\Bcal}(X-x)$, with empty
product equal to one.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:annihilator} follows from the simple roots in
\eqref{eq:simple}. A nonzero polynomial cannot vanish on an infinite
set of distinct points. If $\Bcal$ is finite, its monic vanishing
polynomial divides every polynomial vanishing on $\Bcal$, giving
the assertion about the ideal.

Only finitely many periodic cycles meet $V(AB)$: different cycles
are disjoint and each such cycle contains a point of this finite
set. The full union of those cycles is finite, though its size is
not bounded here by the number of support points. On an admissible
cycle $O$ of least period $r\mid n$, for $x\in O$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:repeatproduct}
 H_n(x)=\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}B(f^{\circ i}(x))\right)
                (W_O^{n/r}-1),
\end{equation}
and the prefactor is nonzero. If $\CP$ holds, every cycle outside
its finite exceptional collection and the support cycles has
$H_n=0$ at every return. Thus $\Bcal$ is finite. Conversely,
each admissible cycle with $W_O\ne1$ contributes all its points
to $\Bcal_r$ at its native period. If $\Bcal$ is finite, only
finitely many such cycles exist. Adding the finite support cycles
gives exactly $\CP$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What derivative filtering does, and does not, see}

For clarity, temporarily let $F$ be any nonzero polynomial over
$k$, and let $x_0$ be a root of multiplicity $e$. Write
$F=(X-x_0)^eV$ with $V(x_0)\ne0$. For arbitrary polynomials
$S,H$, local divisibility is characterized by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:filter}
 F\mid SF'H\text{ at }x_0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e\,S(x_0)H(x_0)=0\text{ in }k.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $p\nmid e$, the derivative has exact order $e-1$,
so one additional zero of $SH$ is necessary and sufficient. If
$p\mid e$, differentiation gives $F'=(X-x_0)^eV'$, and local
divisibility is automatic. This proves~\eqref{eq:filter} without
equating it with the pointwise value $F'(x_0)H(x_0)$.

For return polynomials, put $e_n(x)=\ord_x F_n$. Product visibility
means $H_n(x)\ne0$ at a return root. Derivative-filtered visibility
means $e_n(x)H_n(x)\ne0$, with the integer $e_n(x)$ interpreted
in $k$. Native Jacobian visibility alone means $p\nmid e_r(x)$
at the least period $r$, independently of the weight. These are
different notions in general. In the present derivative-zero
class, $e_n(x)=1$ at every return by~\eqref{eq:simple}, so
derivative filtering discards no product-visible return.

\begin{example}[A one-return warning outside the hypotheses]
In characteristic three, take $f=x^2$. Then
$F_2=x(x-1)^3$ and $F_2'=(x-1)^3$. Choose a constant
$c\in k^\times$ with $c^2\ne1$ and let $g=c$, so
$H_2=c^2-1\ne0$. With $S=x$ one has
$F_2\mid SF_2'H_2$ but $F_2\nmid SH_2$.
The fixed point $1$ has native multiplicity one in $F_1=x(x-1)$,
yet return multiplicity three in $F_2$. This proves only the
stated one-return blindness. It is not an all-return rational-weight
counterexample and is not used in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
\end{example}
