\section{One invariant nonlinear source}
A finite-rank invariant manifold need not be a finite Fourier approximation.
Here it contains infinitely many modes and an exact nonlinear evolution.
The cubic Szeg\H{o} equation and its Hankel structure originate in
G\'erard--Grellier \cite{gerard2010}; Xu \cite{xu2014} established the
rank-one threshold for its linear perturbation. Our contribution within
this repository is a complete convention-preserving proof and reproducibility
package. In particular, we retain the rank hypothesis, the turning point, and
the distinction between an auxiliary determinant and physical evolution.
We do not claim literature priority for the classical threshold.

Use normalized circle measure and let $\Pi$ retain nonnegative Fourier modes.
For every real $\alpha$ fix
\begin{equation}
 \ii u_t=\Pi(|u|^2u)+\alpha(u\mid1),\qquad
 u(z)=b+\frac{cz}{1-pz},\quad c\ne0,\quad |p|<1.
 \label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
The phase space in \eqref{eq:pde} is $\LL(1)$; constants will be treated
separately. The physical time is $t$, without rescaling. Set
\begin{equation}
 d=1-|p|^2,\quad \rho=|c|,\quad B=|b|^2,\quad
 Q=B+\frac{\rho^2}{d},\quad M=\frac{\rho^2}{d^2}>0.
 \label{eq:invariants}
\end{equation}
Energy and its defect are
\begin{equation}
 E_\alpha=\frac14\|u\|_4^4+\frac\alpha2|b|^2,\qquad
 \delta=E_\alpha-\frac{Q^2}{4}-\frac{\alpha Q}{2}.
 \label{eq:defect}
\end{equation}
We always take the positive square root of $M$. On a trajectory,
$\rho=\sqrt M\,d$ and $B=Q-Md$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact all-mode reduction and global existence]
Every initial datum in $\LL(1)$ has a unique solution for all real time.
Its coefficients satisfy
\begin{align}
 \ii\dot b&=(B+2\rho^2/d+\alpha)b+\rho^2c\bar p/d^2,\notag\\
 \ii\dot c&=(2B+\rho^2/d^2)c+2b\rho^2p/d,\label{eq:ode}\\
 \ii\dot p&=c\bar b+\rho^2p/d.\notag
\end{align}
The quantities $Q,M,E_\alpha$ are conserved. These statements hold at
$b=0$ and $p=0$ and involve no Fourier cutoff.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The geometric Fourier coefficients are $u_0=b$ and $u_n=cp^{n-1}$ for
$n\ge1$. Their absolute convergence on strict subdisks justifies convolution
in $\Pi(|u|^2u)$. Compare the coefficients of
$1,z/(1-pz),z^2/(1-pz)^2$; the derivative of the last coordinate is
$c\dot p$, not merely $\dot p$. The result is \eqref{eq:ode}.
A direct quartic sum gives
\begin{equation}
 4E_\alpha=B^2+\frac{4B\rho^2}{d}
  +\frac{\rho^4(1+|p|^2)}{d^3}
  +\frac{4\rho^2\Re(bp\bar c)}{d^2}+2\alpha B.
 \label{eq:quartic}
\end{equation}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:invariants} and \eqref{eq:quartic} along
\eqref{eq:ode} cancels every term, giving the three conservation laws.

To justify all-time continuation, put $X=bp\bar c$. Direct differentiation
gives
\begin{equation}
 \dot d=2\Im X,\qquad
 \dot\rho=2\sqrt M\,\Im X,\qquad
 |\dot\rho/\rho|\le2\sqrt{QM}.
 \label{eq:logbound}
\end{equation}
Hence $\rho(t)\ge\rho(0)\e^{-2\sqrt{QM}|t|}$ on every finite time interval.
The conserved quantities bound $|b|\le\sqrt Q$, $\rho\le\sqrt M$, and
$d=\rho/\sqrt M$ away from zero on that interval.
The real six-dimensional vector field is locally Lipschitz on $d>0$,
so its continuation theorem excludes finite-time exit.
The resulting geometric series solves \eqref{eq:pde} in every $H^s$.
This argument proves the named invariant-manifold theorem, not global
well-posedness for arbitrary Hardy data.
\end{proof}

\section{Compactness and the exact exceptional faces}
The defect is useful because it vanishes precisely where a compactness
estimate could fail.
\begin{lemma}[Factored defect and a quantitative bound]
For every trajectory in $\LL(1)$,
\begin{equation}
 \delta=\frac{Md}{2}\left(\left|b+\frac{c\bar p}{d}\right|^2-\alpha\right).
 \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
If $\delta\ne0$, then for every real $t$,
\begin{equation}
 d(t)\ge
 \frac{2|\delta|}{M\big((\sqrt Q+\sqrt M)^2+|\alpha|\big)}>0.
 \label{eq:compact}
\end{equation}
The orbit is relatively compact in $\LL(1)$ and bounded in every $H^s$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Subtract the two terms in \eqref{eq:defect} from \eqref{eq:quartic};
the remaining quadratic expression is \eqref{eq:factor}.
Since $|b+c\bar p/d|\le\sqrt Q+\sqrt M$,
taking absolute values proves \eqref{eq:compact}.
Thus $|p|$ lies in a strict subdisk and
$|c|=\sqrt M\,d$ stays positive, while $b,c$ remain bounded.
These inequalities give compact closure within the coefficient manifold.
Every Sobolev norm is then bounded by a convergent geometric series.
\end{proof}

For $\alpha<0$, \eqref{eq:factor} has strictly positive defect; thus all
rank-one orbits are relatively compact. On a compact set of initial data,
the positive continuous defect has a positive minimum, making the bound
uniform for that set. This does not assert uniformity across data approaching
a positive-alpha zero-defect hypersurface.

For $\alpha=0$, nonzero defect is already settled. Zero defect forces
$b=-c\bar p/d$. Writing $A=c/d$ yields
\begin{equation}
 Q=M=|A|^2,\qquad
 u(z)=A\,\frac{z-\bar p}{1-pz},\qquad
 u(t,z)=\e^{-\ii Qt}u(0,z).
 \label{eq:inner}
\end{equation}
The quotient in \eqref{eq:inner} has modulus one on the circle. Substitution
in \eqref{eq:ode} gives $\dot p=0$, $\dot b=-\ii Qb$ and
$\dot c=-\ii Qc$, proving the formula even at $p=0$.
Therefore every zero-alpha rank-one orbit is also relatively compact.

\paragraph{Rank zero cannot inherit the threshold.}
If $c=0$, the parameter $p$ is redundant and the complete solution is
\begin{equation}
 u(t,z)=b(0)\e^{-\ii(|b(0)|^2+\alpha)t}.
 \label{eq:constant}
\end{equation}
Every constant satisfies $\delta=0$ but has constant Sobolev norm.
The zero function is stationary. A nonzero constant is stationary exactly
when $|b|^2+\alpha=0$; otherwise its least temporal period is
$2\pi/||b|^2+\alpha|$.
These facts explain why the rank-one hypothesis cannot be dropped from a
positive-alpha growth criterion.

\section{Turning-point-safe cascade}
Assume $\alpha>0$ and $\delta=0$. Define the discriminant
\begin{equation}
 \kappa^2=4QM-(\alpha-Q-M)^2.
 \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The next result strengthens the usable form of the classical threshold:
it provides the complete radial trajectory with a valid turning-point proof.

\begin{theorem}[Complete positive-alpha dichotomy]
For initial data in $\LL(1)$ and $\alpha>0$, the following are equivalent:
the trajectory is not relatively compact in $\LL(1)$; and $\delta=0$.
On that level $\kappa>0$, and, with
\begin{equation}
 d_*=\frac{\kappa^2}{4\alpha M},\qquad
 t_*=\frac2\kappa\artanh\left(\frac{\dot d(0)}{\kappa d(0)}\right),
 \label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}
the exact radial orbit for every real $t$ is
\begin{equation}
 d(t)=d_*\sech^2\left(\frac{\kappa(t-t_*)}{2}\right),\qquad
 \rho(t)=\sqrt M\,d(t).
 \label{eq:sech}
\end{equation}
Here $0<d_*\le1$, and the formula includes $p=0$ at the turn.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:factor} gives
$2\Re X=d(\alpha-Q-M)+2Md^2$.
Together with $|X|^2=(Q-Md)Md^2(1-d)$ and \eqref{eq:logbound}, it implies
\begin{equation}
 \dot d^{\,2}=d^2(\kappa^2-4\alpha Md).
 \label{eq:squared}
\end{equation}
Since $d>0$, the right side forces
$\kappa^2\ge4\alpha Md>0$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \kappa^2-4\alpha M=-(Q-M-\alpha)^2\le0,
 \label{eq:dstar}
\end{equation}
so $d_*\le1$.

A squared velocity identity alone permits spurious sticking at a turn.
To remove that ambiguity, differentiate the complex ODE itself.
Put $T=\alpha-Q-M$. Then
\begin{align}
 \dot X&=\ii(FX+G),\qquad F=-T-4Md,\notag\\
 G&=-M^2d^2(1-d)-(Q-Md)Md^2
       +2(Q-Md)Md(1-d).
 \label{eq:xderivative}
\end{align}
All coefficients $F,G$ are real. Since $\dot d=2\Im X$,
substitute $\Re X=dT/2+Md^2$ in
$\ddot d=2(F\Re X+G)$ to obtain the polynomial equation
\begin{equation}
 \ddot d=\kappa^2d-6\alpha Md^2.
 \label{eq:second}
\end{equation}
This derivation never divides by $\dot d$ or by $p$.
At $d=d_*$ it gives $\ddot d=-\kappa^2d_*/2<0$,
excluding a stationary turning point.

By \eqref{eq:squared}, $v_0=\dot d(0)/(\kappa d(0))$ lies strictly between
$-1$ and $1$, so \eqref{eq:shift} is finite. The function
\eqref{eq:sech} solves \eqref{eq:second}.
At time zero its logarithmic derivative is
$\kappa\tanh(\kappa t_*/2)=\kappa v_0$.
Also $\sech^2(\kappa t_*/2)=1-v_0^2=d(0)/d_*$,
so both initial data agree.
Uniqueness for the polynomial second-order initial-value problem proves
\eqref{eq:sech} for every real $t$, including the turn.

The radial variable tends to zero at both infinite-time ends, so this orbit
does not have compact closure within $\LL(1)$.
Every nonzero-defect orbit is compact by \eqref{eq:compact}.
This proves both directions of the dichotomy.
\end{proof}

The all-time clause matters. No finite-time singularity is created:
$d(t)$ remains positive for every finite $t$. The expression
$\kappa^2/(4\alpha M)$ can equal one, but that is the harmless event $p=0$,
not the forbidden pole boundary $|p|=1$.

The source threshold appears in Xu \cite[Theorem 3.1]{xu2014}.
We do not rely on a globally positive lower bound for
$|\dot d/d|$: that derivative is zero at $t=t_*$.
Equations \eqref{eq:xderivative}--\eqref{eq:second} and uniqueness provide
the complete argument instead.

\section{All-mode Sobolev growth and momentum escape}
Define the exact norm convention
\begin{equation}
 \|u\|_{H^s}^2=\sum_{n\ge0}(1+n)^{2s}|u_n|^2.
 \label{eq:sobolev}
\end{equation}
The radial theorem has an all-frequency consequence, not merely a growing
finite-dimensional diagnostic.

\begin{theorem}[Exact growth exponent]
On the positive-alpha cascade level, for every $s>1/2$,
\begin{align}
 \|u(t)\|_{H^s}^2&\sim M\Gamma(2s+1)d(t)^{1-2s},
       && t\to\pm\infty,\label{eq:asymptotic}\\
 \lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{\log\|u(t)\|_{H^s}}{|t|}
       &=(s-\tfrac12)\kappa.\label{eq:rate}
\end{align}
At the critical exponent,
$\|u(t)\|_{H^{1/2}}^2=Q+M$ exactly.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $d\downarrow0$, the geometric sum satisfies
\begin{equation}
 d^{2s+1}\sum_{n\ge1}(n+1)^{2s}(1-d)^{n-1}
 \longrightarrow\int_0^\infty x^{2s}\e^{-x}\dd x=\Gamma(2s+1).
 \label{eq:riemann}
\end{equation}
To justify this Riemann-sum limit, use $x=dn$.
On a bounded interval separated from zero, the summand converges uniformly
to $x^{2s}\e^{-x}$. A multiple of $x^{2s}$ controls the interval near zero,
and an exponential majorant controls the tail.
These bounds allow the two omitted intervals to be made arbitrarily small,
uniformly for sufficiently small $d$.

Since $|c|^2=Md^2$, multiplying \eqref{eq:riemann} gives
\eqref{eq:asymptotic}; the bounded constant coefficient does not contribute
to the diverging leading term. Equation \eqref{eq:sech} yields
\begin{equation}
 d(t)\sim\frac{\kappa^2}{\alpha M}
             \e^{-\kappa|t-t_*|},
 \label{eq:radialtail}
\end{equation}
and then \eqref{eq:rate} follows. At $s=1/2$, direct geometric summation
in \eqref{eq:sobolev} gives $Q+M$, proving the critical assertion.
\end{proof}

The conserved momentum does not remain in a fixed frequency window.
Indeed,
\begin{equation}
 \|u(t)-b(t)\|_2^2=Md(t)\longrightarrow0,\qquad
 \sum_{n\ge1}n|u_n(t)|^2=M.
 \label{eq:escape}
\end{equation}
For each fixed $N$, the sum over $1\le n\le N$ tends to zero because
$|c(t)|^2\to0$. Thus the source preserves momentum while its location moves
beyond every fixed Fourier cutoff. There is no finite-time norm singularity
or loss of a conserved quantity.

For the explicit data $u_0(z)=\sqrt\alpha+z$, one has
$Q=1+\alpha$, $M=1$, $\kappa=2\sqrt\alpha$, $t_*=0$.
Hence $d(t)=\sech^2(\sqrt\alpha\,t)$ and the rate is
$(2s-1)\sqrt\alpha$, agreeing with the classical example
\cite[Theorem 3.2]{xu2014}.

\section{The same determinant sees opposite dynamics}
The relevant Hankel operator is antilinear. Fix the inner product linear
in its first variable and define
$H_uf=\Pi(u\bar f)$, $K_u=S^*H_u$.
The matrix of $K_u$ is $(u_{j+k+1})=(cp^{j+k})$.
With $v=(1,p,p^2,\ldots)$, direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
 K_uf=c\langle v,f\rangle v,\qquad
 K_u^2f=\frac{|c|^2}{d}\langle f,v\rangle v.
 \label{eq:hankel}
\end{equation}
Since $\|v\|^2=1/d$, $K_u^2$ is complex-linear, positive, rank one,
and its sole nonzero eigenvalue is $M$.

\begin{proposition}[Auxiliary isospectral blindness]
For all complex $w$ and integers $r\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
 \det(I-wK_u^2)=1-wM,\qquad \tr(K_u^{2r})=M^r.
 \label{eq:det}
\end{equation}
At each fixed $\alpha>0$, this determinant is shared by a bounded pure-mode
trajectory and a two-sided cascading trajectory.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first assertions follow from the explicit rank-one linear operator in
\eqref{eq:hankel} and conservation of $M$.
For $u_0=z$, the source solution is $u(t)=\e^{-\ii t}z$, with $M=1$.
For $u_0=\sqrt\alpha+z$, the preceding theorem gives a cascade, also with
$M=1$. Both therefore have determinant $1-w$.
Even the recurrence distinction is absent from that determinant.
Allowing $\alpha$ to vary further keeps $1-w$ fixed while the growth exponent
$2\sqrt\alpha$ varies continuously.
\end{proof}

Equation \eqref{eq:det} is a native auxiliary invariant.
It is not a determinant of the nonlinear time evolution, and it is not
a primitive-orbit product. We do not assign a complex Fredholm determinant
directly to the antilinear $K_u$.
A zero-free entire factor cannot change the fact that
$1-wM$ has only one zero; this polynomial is not a full target divisor.

The source has a concrete reversal on Hardy functions:
\begin{equation}
 (\CC u)(z)=\overline{u(\bar z)}.
 \label{eq:reversal}
\end{equation}
It conjugates Fourier coefficients and preserves the Hardy space.
The real convolution coefficients in \eqref{eq:pde} show that
$\CC u(-t)$ solves the same alpha equation.
Pointwise conjugation alone would reverse Fourier support and is not the
stated Hardy-space reversal. This nonlinear Hamiltonian symmetry is not a
construction of a linear quantum Hamiltonian.
