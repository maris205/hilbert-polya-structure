\begin{abstract}
At one positive nonsquare binary-form discriminant we freeze an exact
primitive reduced phase space and prove that the Gauss shift is its
permutation.  Every orbit has a primitive continued-fraction word whose
positive matrix product is a hyperbolic stabilizer; the same matrix gives an
exact cycle multiplier and its discriminant identity.
\ifnum\CRevisionRound>0
Conjugate reciprocity reverses the dynamics, while the complete cycle
inventory yields every fixed-point count, the finite Artin--Mazur zeta, and
the Koopman determinant.
\fi
\ifnum\CRevisionRound>1
Square, zero, negative, invalid-congruence, imprimitive, and norm-sign faces
are closed explicitly.  No class-group convention or target arithmetic is
silently imported.
\fi
Finite exact computations audit conventions only; the theorem is analytic.
\end{abstract}

\section{The convention and the finite phase space}
Fix a positive nonsquare integer \(\Delta\equiv0,1\pmod4\).  Let
\(\mathcal R_\Delta\) contain the numbers
\begin{equation}\label{eq:state}
 \alpha=\frac{P+\sqrt\Delta}{Q},\qquad Q=2A>0,
 \qquad A=\frac Q2,\quad B=-P,\quad C=\frac{P^2-\Delta}{2Q},
\end{equation}
for which \(A,B,C\in\Z\), \(\gcd(A,B,C)=1\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced}
 \alpha>1,\qquad -1<\bar\alpha=\frac{P-\sqrt\Delta}{Q}<0.
\end{equation}
The polynomial \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) has discriminant exactly \(\Delta\) and roots
\((P\pm\sqrt\Delta)/Q\).  Thus \eqref{eq:state} is a binary-form
discriminant convention, not a radicand convention that accidentally changes
the discriminant to \(4\Delta\).

Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gauss}
 G(\alpha)=\frac1{\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Complete reduced dynamics]\label{thm:main}
The set \(\mathcal R_\Delta\) is finite and \(G\) is a bijection.  Let
\(\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_{\ell-1}\) be a minimal cycle and
\(a_j=\lfloor\alpha_j\rfloor\).  Let \(A(a)\) denote the two-by-two matrix
with rows \((a,1)\) and \((1,0)\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix}
 M=A(a_0)A(a_1)\cdots A(a_{\ell-1}).
\end{equation}
Writing \(M_{11}=a,M_{12}=b,M_{21}=c,M_{22}=d\), this matrix fixes
\(\alpha_0\), has \(\det M=(-1)^\ell\), and is the primitive positive
inverse-branch stabilizer.  For some integer \(k>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:disc}
 (c,d-a,-b)=k(A,B,C),\qquad
 (\operatorname{tr}M)^2-4\det M=k^2\Delta.
\end{equation}
If \(\lambda=c\alpha_0+d\), then \(\lambda>1\) and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplier}
 \left|(G^\ell)'(\alpha_0)\right|=\lambda^2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:reduced},
\(|P|<\sqrt\Delta\) and \(0<Q<P+\sqrt\Delta<2\sqrt\Delta\), proving
finiteness.  Put \(a=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:update}
 P_1=aQ-P,\qquad Q_1=\frac{\Delta-P_1^2}{Q}.
\end{equation}
Rationalization gives
\(G(\alpha)=(P_1+\sqrt\Delta)/Q_1\).  Because
\(0<\alpha-a<1\), this root exceeds one; because
\(-a-1<\bar\alpha-a<-a\), its conjugate lies in \((-1,0)\).
The original integrality says \(2Q\mid P^2-\Delta\), while
\(P_1^2-P^2=a^2Q^2-2aPQ\) is divisible by \(2Q\); hence \(Q_1\) is positive
and even and the new form is integral.  The inverse branch
\(x\mapsto a+1/x\) is unimodular, so form content, and hence primitivity, is
preserved.

Conversely, for reduced \(\beta\), set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:predecessor}
 t=-1/\bar\beta,\qquad a=\lfloor t\rfloor,\qquad
 \alpha=a+1/\beta.
\end{equation}
Here \(t>1\) is irrational, so \(\alpha>1\) and
\(\bar\alpha=a-t\in(-1,0)\).  The inverse unimodular substitution preserves
the integral primitive form and \(G(\alpha)=\beta\).  The conjugate interval
determines \(a\) uniquely, proving bijectivity.

Writing \(H_a(x)=a+1/x\), one has
\(\alpha_j=H_{a_j}(\alpha_{j+1})\); therefore \eqref{eq:matrix} fixes
\(\alpha_0\).  A shorter digit word would, by uniqueness of an infinite
simple continued fraction, give a shorter state return.  Each factor has
determinant minus one.  The integral stabilizer of a real quadratic irrational
maps through \(N\mapsto c_N\alpha+d_N\), after choosing the positive
representative, to units of its quadratic order that are positive at the
\(\alpha\)-embedding.  We use the classical
reduction--automorph cross-section lemma in its exact return form: an expanding
positive projective stabilizer preserves the oriented geodesic from
\(\bar\alpha\) to \(\alpha\) and advances its reduced cross-section by an
integer \(r\geq1\) of complete Gauss digits.  Thus
\[
 [N]=[A(a_0)\cdots A(a_{r-1})],\qquad G^r(\alpha_0)=\alpha_0,
\]
and every complete return gives such a stabilizer.  This standard
continued-fraction correspondence is source-anchored to Uspensky and Buell
below.  Minimality of \(\ell\) makes every \(r=n\ell\), and periodicity makes
the corresponding product \(M^n\); a representative first written with mixed
signs therefore cannot yield a smaller positive return.  Hence \(M\)
generates the positive projective stabilizer.  Since
\(\det(M^n)=(-1)^{\ell n}\), the determinant-one stabilizer up to \(\pm I\)
is generated by \(M\) for even \(\ell\) and by \(M^2\) for odd \(\ell\).

The fixed-point equation of \(M\) is
\(cx^2+(d-a)x-b=0\).  Its coefficient vector is an integral multiple of the
primitive vector \((A,B,C)\), giving \eqref{eq:disc}.  Finally
\(M'(\alpha)=\det M/(c\alpha+d)^2\); since \(M\) is the local inverse of
\(G^\ell\), reciprocal differentiation gives \eqref{eq:multiplier}.  Positive
digit matrices give \(\lambda>1\).  This is the \emph{reduced-permutation
owner}.
\end{proof}

\section{Reversal and the complete determinant}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reversal}
 R(\alpha)=-\frac1{\bar\alpha},\qquad
 R(P,Q)=(P,(\Delta-P^2)/Q).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Reversing involution]\label{prop:reverse}
The map \(R\) preserves \(\mathcal R_\Delta\), satisfies \(R^2=1\), and
\(RGR=G^{-1}\).  It sends every cycle word to its reversal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The two inequalities in \eqref{eq:reduced} are exchanged by conjugate
reciprocity; rationalization gives the pair formula in \eqref{eq:reversal},
from which \(R^2=1\).  Since the digit matrices are symmetric, transposing
\(A(a_0)\cdots A(a_{\ell-1})\) reverses its word.  Equivalently,
\[
 [\overline{a_0,\ldots,a_{\ell-1}}]
 \longmapsto
 [\overline{a_{\ell-1},\ldots,a_0}].
\]
Reversal conjugates a left shift to a right shift, proving \(RGR=G^{-1}\).
\end{proof}

Let \(c_m\) count cycles of length \(m\), and let \(U_Gf=f\circ G\) on
\(\ell^2(\mathcal R_\Delta)\).  A length-\(m\) cycle contributes all its
states to \(\operatorname{Fix}(G^n)\) exactly when \(m\mid n\), hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed}
 \#\operatorname{Fix}(G^n)=\sum_{m\mid n}m c_m.
\end{equation}
Substitution into the finite Artin--Mazur definition, followed by the
determinant of a cyclic permutation matrix, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta}
 \zeta_{G,\Delta}(z)=\prod_m(1-z^m)^{-c_m},
 \qquad
 \det(I-zU_G)=\prod_m(1-z^m)^{c_m}=\zeta_{G,\Delta}(z)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{reversal-determinant owner}.  It is a finite source cycle
identity, not an Euler product over rational primes.

\section{Boundary atlas and Route-A interpretation}
The theorem has six sharp boundary rules.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If \(\Delta=s^2>0\), the roots are rational and the regular continued
fraction terminates.  There is no reduced-quadratic-irrational permutation.
\item At \(\Delta=0\) the polynomial has a repeated rational root.  For
\(\Delta<0\) it has no ordered real conjugates.
\item Integers \(2\) or \(3\pmod4\) are not discriminants of integral binary
quadratic forms.
\item The primitive filter is structural: \([2,-2,-2]\), of discriminant
20, divides to \([1,-1,-1]\), of discriminant 5.  Dropping content one would
duplicate a lower-discriminant state.
\item If \(\ell\) is odd, \(\det M=-1\); the determinant-one stabilizer is
generated by \(M^2\), not \(M\).  For even \(\ell\), it is generated by
\(M\), up to \(\pm I\).
\item No proper-class or narrow-class bijection is claimed.  Such a bridge
would require a separate orientation and nonmaximal-order convention audit.
\end{enumerate}
This is the \emph{boundary-firewall owner}.
