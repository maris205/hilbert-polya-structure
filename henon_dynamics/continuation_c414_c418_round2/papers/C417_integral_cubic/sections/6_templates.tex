\section{Exhaustion of the seven templates}
\label{sec:templates}

A least-period-$n$ orbit has $n$ distinct ordered coordinate pairs.
This elementary observation restricts the possible words before
interpolation is applied.

\subsection{One and two coordinate values}

A one-symbol word is fixed, and its condition is $f(u)=2u$.
With two symbols there are only four ordered pairs, so there is
no least-period-six cycle. Period two has the word $(u,v)$,
where $u\ne v$; choose $u<v$ to avoid duplicate rotations.
The equations $f(u)=2v$ and $f(v)=2u$ say that the monic cubic
$f(t)+2t-2(u+v)$ vanishes at $u$ and $v$. Dividing by
$(t-u)(t-v)$ leaves a monic linear factor. Comparing its quadratic
coefficient with $b$ forces that factor to be $t+u+v+b$.
This proves the second row of Table~\ref{tab:templates}.

A two-symbol three-cycle has a uniquely repeated symbol, so its
word can be written $(u,u,v)$ with $u\ne v$.
The recurrence gives $f(u)=u+v$ and $f(v)=2u$.
Consequently $f(t)+t-(2u+v)$ vanishes at both $u,v$ and has the
same forced third factor, proving the third row.

A two-symbol four-cycle must have two occurrences of each letter.
Indeed a word $(u,u,u,v)$ would force both $f(u)=2u$ and
$f(u)=u+v$, and the analogous assertion holds when the other
symbol is repeated three times. With two occurrences of each,
the alternating word has period two. The remaining word is
$(u,u,v,v)$ up to rotation, and reversal is already a rotation
of this word. Its equations are $f(u)=f(v)=u+v$. Factoring
$f(t)-(u+v)$ proves the fifth row.

\subsection{Three coordinate values and an integer interpolation lemma}

If a three-cycle uses distinct symbols $u<v<w$, the neighbors of
each are the other two. With $S=u+v+w$, the monic polynomial
$f(t)+t-S$ vanishes at all three symbols. It is therefore exactly
$(t-u)(t-v)(t-w)$. Both words $(u,v,w)$ and $(u,w,v)$ satisfy
the recurrence, and no rotation identifies them. This proves
the fourth row, including $b=-S$.

\begin{lemma}[Integral reciprocal sum]
\label{lem:reciprocal}
If $p,q$ are distinct nonzero integers and $1/p+1/q$ is an
integer, then $p+q=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For opposite signs the absolute value of the sum is strictly less
than one. If it is an integer it must be zero.
For the same sign, change both signs if necessary and arrange
$0<p<q$. When $p=1$, the sum is strictly between one and two.
When $p\ge2$, it is strictly between zero and one.
Neither alternative is integral.
\end{proof}

Consider a three-symbol four-cycle. The repeated symbol cannot occur
adjacently: in $(h,h,u,v)$, the two equations for $h$ would give
$h+v=h+u$, contrary to $u\ne v$. The word is therefore
$(h,u,h,v)$, with three distinct integers, and its conditions are
\[
 f(h)=u+v,\qquad f(u)=f(v)=2h.
\]
Let $Q(t)$ be the quadratic interpolant at $h,u,v$.
Since the difference between the monic cubic and this interpolant is
\[
 f(t)-Q(t)=(t-h)(t-u)(t-v),
\]
the quadratic coefficient of $Q$ is an integer. Subtracting the
constant $2h$ in its interpolation data shows that this coefficient is
\[
 \frac{u+v-2h}{(h-u)(h-v)}
 =\frac{1}{u-h}+\frac{1}{v-h}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:reciprocal} gives $u+v=2h$. Thus the two endpoints
are $h-k,h+k$ with $k\ge1$, and $Q(t)=2h$. The resulting monic
identity is exactly the centered four-cycle row in the table.
Swapping the two endpoints only rotates the displayed word.

\subsection{The three-symbol six-cycle}

First suppose that a three-symbol six-cycle has no equal adjacent
letters. Its six distinct ordered pairs must be all six off-diagonal
pairs on the alphabet. Hence each symbol occurs twice, with the other
two symbols each occurring once as predecessor and once as successor.
At a symbol $u$, equality of the two neighbor sums has two possible
pairings of predecessors and successors. Pairing each predecessor
with itself would require $2v=2w$ for the other distinct symbols,
which is impossible over $\Z$. Both occurrences must therefore
have the unordered neighbor pair $\{v,w\}$. At every step, after
two distinct symbols the next symbol is the third one. The word
repeats the same order of three symbols and has period three,
a contradiction.

There must be adjacent equal letters $p,p$. The equations at the
two occurrences force the predecessor and successor of this pair
to be the same symbol, say $h$. They cannot be $p$: a triple
$p,p,p$ gives $f(p)=2p$ and forces its neighbors successively to
equal $p$, making the entire cycle constant. Rotate the word to
$(h,p,p,h,*,*)$ with $h\ne p$.
The third symbol $q$ must occur in one or both of the remaining
positions. The possibilities involving $q$ are
\[
 (p,q),\ (q,p),\ (h,q),\ (q,h),\ (q,q).
\]
In the first two, comparing the additional occurrence of $p$ with
an occurrence in the double forces $q=p$. In the next two, the
equations at the additional $h$ force $q=h$. These contradict
the distinctness of the third symbol. The last two positions
are therefore $(q,q)$, giving the word
\[
 (p,p,h,q,q,h).
\]
Its recurrence equations are
\[
 f(p)=p+h,\qquad f(q)=q+h,\qquad f(h)=p+q.
\]
Apply interpolation to the monic cubic $f(t)-(t+h)$. It has
values zero at $p,q$ and value $p+q-2h$ at $h$.
As in the four-cycle argument, the quadratic coefficient of its
quadratic interpolant is an integer and equals
\[
 \frac{p+q-2h}{(h-p)(h-q)}
 =\frac1{p-h}+\frac1{q-h}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:reciprocal} forces $p+q=2h$.
Writing the endpoints as $h-k,h+k$ now gives
\[
 f(t)=t+h+(t-h)((t-h)^2-k^2),
\]
and the displayed period-six word in Table~\ref{tab:templates}.
Swapping endpoints rotates this word by three places.

\subsection{Converses and exact least periods}

The previously proved period and symbol restrictions exhaust all
cases considered above. Conversely, substituting each of the seven
conditions into \eqref{eq:recurrence} verifies its word.
The ranges make the ordered pairs distinct. The two-cycle and the
three-distinct-symbol three-cycle have distinct successive first
coordinates. The word $(u,u,v)$ uses $(u,u),(u,v),(v,u)$,
which are distinct for $u\ne v$. The two-symbol four-cycle uses
$(u,u),(u,v),(v,v),(v,u)$; and the centered four-cycle uses
$(h-k,h),(h,h+k),(h+k,h),(h,h-k)$.
For the six-cycle, subtracting $h$ from both coordinates gives
the pairs
\[
 (-k,-k),\ (-k,0),\ (0,k),\
 (k,k),\ (k,0),\ (0,-k),
\]
which are distinct for $k\ge1$. Their asserted lengths are therefore
their least periods. Rows with the same period but different numbers
of symbols cannot describe the same orbit.

All five least periods occur. The polynomial $t^3$ has a fixed
point and the centered six-cycle with $h=0,k=1$.
The polynomial $t^3-3t$ has the two-cycle $(-1,1)$;
$t^3-2t$ has the three-cycle $(-1,0,1)$;
and $t^3-t$ has the four-cycle $(-1,0,1,0)$.
This completes the template and period parts of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
