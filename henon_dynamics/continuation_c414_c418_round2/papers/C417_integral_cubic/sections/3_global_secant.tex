\section{Integrality and the global secant}
\label{sec:secant}

The normalization in this section uses the coordinate set of all
periodic points of one map. Choosing the extrema separately for
individual cycles would not control their total number.

\begin{lemma}[Integral points and a common finite box]
\label{lem:integrality}
Every point in $\cP_f$ is integral, and
\[
 \cP_f\subseteq ([-R,R]\cap\Z)^2,
 \qquad R=|a|+|b|+|c|+2.
\]
In particular, $\cP_f$ is finite.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Take any periodic coordinate sequence $(x_i)$ satisfying
\eqref{eq:recurrence}. For a prime $p$, suppose
$T=\max_i|x_i|_p>1$. At an index attaining $T$, the leading term
$x_i^3$ has $p$-adic absolute value $T^3$. The lower terms have
absolute values at most $T^2$, $T$ and $1$, since the coefficients
are integral. The ultrametric inequality with a unique largest term
therefore gives $|f(x_i)|_p=T^3$. But
$|x_{i-1}+x_{i+1}|_p\le T$, a contradiction. This holds at every
prime, so all rational coordinates are integers.

At the real place let $M=\max_i|x_i|$. From the recurrence at an
index attaining $M$,
\[
 M^3\le |b|M^2+(|c|+2)M+|a|.
\]
If $M\ge1$, the right side is at most $RM^2$, so $M\le R$.
If $M<1$, the same bound holds since $R\ge2$. The bound is common
to every orbit of this polynomial. Thus the entire periodic set lies
in the stated finite lattice square.
\end{proof}

If $\cP_f$ is empty, the bounds in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} hold
without further work. If the union $E$ of all its coordinate values
has one element $u$, the periodic set is just the diagonal fixed point
$(u,u)$. Assume from now on that $E$ has at least two elements, and set
\begin{equation}
 m=\min E,\qquad D=\max E-m\ge1,\qquad
 g(t)=f(t+m)-2m.
 \label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
Writing $T_m(x,y)=(x+m,y+m)$, we have
$H_g=T_{-m}H_fT_m$. The translated global coordinate set
$E'=E-m$ lies in $\{0,\ldots,D\}$ and contains both endpoints.
The polynomial $g$ remains monic and integral. This is an internal
conjugacy, not a restriction on the original quadratic coefficient.

\begin{lemma}[Integral secant root]
\label{lem:secant}
There are integers $A,q,r$ such that
\begin{align}
 g(t)&=A+qt+t(t-D)(t-r),\label{eq:secant}\\
 -2\le q\le2,\qquad 0&\le A\le2D,\qquad
 0\le A+qD\le2D.\label{eq:endpoint-conditions}
\end{align}
Every $t\in E'$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 |t(t-D)(t-r)|\le2D.
 \label{eq:secant-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
When $t$ occurs in a cycle, $g(t)$ is the sum of its two neighbors,
both in $[0,D]$. Hence $0\le g(t)\le2D$ for every $t\in E'$.
Set $A=g(0)$ and $q=(g(D)-g(0))/D$. Since $g$ has integer
coefficients, $D$ divides $g(D)-g(0)$; the endpoint bounds give
\eqref{eq:endpoint-conditions}. The monic cubic $g(t)-A-qt$
vanishes at $0$ and $D$. If its quadratic coefficient is $B$, its
third root is $r=-B-D\in\Z$, proving \eqref{eq:secant}.

The line $A+qt$ interpolates the endpoint values $g(0)$ and $g(D)$.
It belongs to $[0,2D]$ throughout $[0,D]$. For an actual coordinate
$t\in E'$, both $g(t)$ and this line value are in that interval.
Their difference is the cubic product in \eqref{eq:secant}, giving
\eqref{eq:secant-bound}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The affine-root case]
\label{lem:affine}
If $E'\subseteq\{0,D,r\}$, there are at most nine periodic points.
Every least period is in $\{1,2,3,4,6\}$, and each cycle uses at
most three symbols. If $E'\subseteq\{0,D\}$, the point bound is
four, irrespective of $r$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At a root of the cubic remainder the recurrence becomes
\begin{equation}
 x_{i+1}=A+qx_i-x_{i-1}.
 \label{eq:affine-recurrence}
\end{equation}
At most three coordinate values give at most nine ordered pairs.
For $q\ne2$, subtract the stationary value $A/(2-q)$; the resulting
homogeneous pair recurrence has matrix
\[
 L_q=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&q\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Multiplication gives $L_{-1}^3=I$, $L_0^4=I$ and $L_1^6=I$.
The least periods divide the indicated orders. For $q=-2$ the
homogeneous coordinate solutions are
$(\alpha+\beta i)(-1)^i$; a periodic solution must have
$\beta=0$, so its period divides two. For $q=2$, summing
\eqref{eq:affine-recurrence} around a cycle gives $nA=0$ and hence
$A=0$. Consecutive differences are then constant; summing them over
one period makes that constant zero. The sequence is fixed.
The union of all these possible divisors is $\{1,2,3,4,6\}$.
With only two symbols there are at most four ordered pairs, and the
same recurrence applies.
\end{proof}

The last assertion includes $D=1$. The one-symbol and empty cases
already cover $D=0$ and absence of extrema. These cases are not sent
to a computation that assumes an interior symbol.
