           All rational cycles of monic integral conservative cubic
               Hénon maps and the sharp eleven-point bound
                                            Anonymous Authors


                                                    Abstract
           We classify every rational periodic orbit of Hf (x, y) = (y, f (y) − x) for every monic cubic
       f (t) = t3 + bt2 + ct + a with a, b, c ∈ Z. The difficulty is to control the coexistence of different
       cycles uniformly in all three coefficients, rather than to bound the length of a single cycle.
       Rational periodic points are integral, and the extrema of the entire periodic coordinate set give
       an integral third root of a cubic secant remainder. This reduces all sufficiently large diameters to
       ninety graphs over Z[D]; an exact finite complement contains 9,373 explicitly bounded parameter
       tuples. We give the full graph procedure and its result tables, with no period or original-coefficient
       cutoff. Every cycle uses at most three distinct coordinate values and has least period 1, 2, 3, 4,
       or 6. Seven exact word and polynomial templates describe all cycles. There are at most eleven
       rational periodic points in total, with equality precisely when f (t) = (t − h)3 − 5(t − h) + 2h
       for an integer h. The coordinate set of all coexisting cycles can nevertheless contain more than
       three values. The theorem uses ordinary iteration and counts points without identifying the two
       orientations of a cycle.


1     The full cubic classification
The rational periodic points of an individual polynomial automorphism can be finite even when
a direct search depends strongly on its coefficients. For the full monic integral conservative cubic
family, that dependence can be removed: every cycle has one of seven explicit forms, and the largest
possible union of cycles has eleven points. The sharp bound concerns all cycles of the same map,
not merely a bound on their individual lengths.
    Throughout the paper, fix

                 f (t) = t3 + bt2 + ct + a,        a, b, c ∈ Z,      Hf (x, y) = (y, f (y) − x).                (1)

Its Jacobian determinant is +1 and its inverse is (x, y) 7→ (f (x) − y, x). The domain is all of Q2 . Let
Pf = Per(Hf , Q2 ) be the union of its periodic orbits. All periods below are least periods for ordinary
iteration by Hf . An orbit with coordinate word (v0 , . . . , vn−1 ) consists of the points (vi , vi+1 ), with
indices taken modulo n. The word is identified under cyclic rotation, never under reversal. Its
recurrence is
                                         vi−1 + vi+1 = f (vi ).                                            (2)
Theorem 1.1 (All coefficients, all rational periodic points). For every polynomial in (1), the
following statements hold.
    1. Every rational periodic point is integral. Each cycle uses at most three distinct coordinate
       values, and all its possibilities are the seven rows of Table 1.

    2. Every least period belongs to {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}; each of these five periods occurs for a member of
       the family.

     The proof has three parts. First, integrality and a real escape bound make the whole periodic
set finite. Its two global coordinate extrema then give an integral secant slope and an integral third
root of the cubic remainder. Second, the secant inequality forces either an affine recurrence on three
roots or one of finitely many endpoint graphs. For large diameters these are exact graphs over Z[D],
not graphs sampled at one large integer. The remaining diameters have proved finite parameter
ranges. Third, short-word interpolation gives all templates, and the unique maximizing graph gives
(3).
     The finite arithmetic tables are part of the proof. Their complete enumeration is specified in
Appendix A; every input range is justified before it is used. They involve no floating-point decision,
guessed period cutoff, or bounded scan through the original coefficients. The manuscript separates
the analytic reduction from the exact finite computation.


2     Arithmetic context and the scope of the increment
General restrictions on integral cycles precede this classification. Pezda studies polynomial mappings
of Z2 and gives, in Theorem 2.1, the cycle-length list

                                        {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 16, 18, 24};

see Pezda [5]. That is a restriction on cycle length, not the sharp number of periodic points in
the particular cubic family (1). We do not claim the existence of a uniform integral period bound

as new. Nor do we use the displayed list as a substitute for the cubic graph argument: the latter
proves the smaller period set and the coexistence bound directly.
    Canonical heights provide a wider arithmetic setting for polynomial automorphisms. Ingram
develops height and specialization results for Hénon maps and explicitly discusses earlier boundedness
work of Pezda [3]. Our use of local maxima is a direct elementary argument in a restricted monic
integral family, not a new general height theorem. The published bibliographic record is cited; the text
used for the comparison is the introduction and opening local-height material of arXiv:1111.3609v1.
In particular, the quadratic conjecture for (y, x + y 2 + b) discussed there has determinant −1. It is
not the family studied here. No assertion about that conjecture follows from Theorem 1.1.
    The arithmetic questions collected in Section 11 of de Hénon [2] distinguish general rational-
coefficient uniform boundedness, interpolation constructions and special determinants. Those
questions are broader than the present all-integral cubic classification. Elementary interpolation
can produce selected short orbits; it does not on its own exclude additional orbits elsewhere. The
main issue here is precisely that exclusion and the resulting coexistence theorem. We therefore
deduct word interpolation as a method, while proving its exact seven-template consequence under
the global restrictions established below.
    Rational coefficients cannot be silently substituted for integral coefficients. The rational-
coefficient, integer-valued polynomials of Kim et al. [4] have many periodic points as their odd
degrees grow. Their discrete-sine cubic is

3    Integrality and the global secant
The normalization in this section uses the coordinate set of all periodic points of one map. Choosing
the extrema separately for individual cycles would not control their total number.

Lemma 3.1 (Integral points and a common finite box). Every point in Pf is integral, and

                            Pf ⊆ ([−R, R] ∩ Z)2 ,       R = |a| + |b| + |c| + 2.

In particular, Pf is finite.

Proof. Take any periodic coordinate sequence (xi ) satisfying (2). For a prime p, suppose T =
maxi |xi |p > 1. At an index attaining T , the leading term x3i has p-adic absolute value T 3 . The lower
terms have absolute values at most T 2 , T and 1, since the coefficients are integral. The ultrametric
inequality with a unique largest term therefore gives |f (xi )|p = T 3 . But |xi−1 + xi+1 |p ≤ T , a
contradiction. This holds at every prime, so all rational coordinates are integers.
   At the real place let M = maxi |xi |. From the recurrence at an index attaining M ,

                                    M 3 ≤ |b|M 2 + (|c| + 2)M + |a|.

If M ≥ 1, the right side is at most RM 2 , so M ≤ R. If M < 1, the same bound holds since R ≥ 2.
The bound is common to every orbit of this polynomial. Thus the entire periodic set lies in the
stated finite lattice square.

   If Pf is empty, the bounds in Theorem 1.1 hold without further work. If the union E of all its
coordinate values has one element u, the periodic set is just the diagonal fixed point (u, u). Assume
from now on that E has at least two elements, and set

Lemma 3.2 (Integral secant root). There are integers A, q, r such that

                                        g(t) = A + qt + t(t − D)(t − r),                              (6)
                          −2 ≤ q ≤ 2,     0 ≤ A ≤ 2D,         0 ≤ A + qD ≤ 2D.                        (7)

Every t ∈ E ′ satisfies
                                        |t(t − D)(t − r)| ≤ 2D.                                       (8)

Proof. When t occurs in a cycle, g(t) is the sum of its two neighbors, both in [0, D]. Hence
0 ≤ g(t) ≤ 2D for every t ∈ E ′ . Set A = g(0) and q = (g(D) − g(0))/D. Since g has integer
coefficients, D divides g(D) − g(0); the endpoint bounds give (7). The monic cubic g(t) − A − qt
vanishes at 0 and D. If its quadratic coefficient is B, its third root is r = −B − D ∈ Z, proving (6).
    The line A + qt interpolates the endpoint values g(0) and g(D). It belongs to [0, 2D] throughout
[0, D]. For an actual coordinate t ∈ E ′ , both g(t) and this line value are in that interval. Their
difference is the cubic product in (6), giving (8).

6    Exhaustion of the seven templates
A least-period-n orbit has n distinct ordered coordinate pairs. This elementary observation restricts
the possible words before interpolation is applied.




                                                  9
6.1   One and two coordinate values
A one-symbol word is fixed, and its condition is f (u) = 2u. With two symbols there are only four
ordered pairs, so there is no least-period-six cycle. Period two has the word (u, v), where u ̸= v;
choose u < v to avoid duplicate rotations. The equations f (u) = 2v and f (v) = 2u say that the
monic cubic f (t) + 2t − 2(u + v) vanishes at u and v. Dividing by (t − u)(t − v) leaves a monic
linear factor. Comparing its quadratic coefficient with b forces that factor to be t + u + v + b. This
proves the second row of Table 1.
    A two-symbol three-cycle has a uniquely repeated symbol, so its word can be written (u, u, v)
with u ≠ v. The recurrence gives f (u) = u + v and f (v) = 2u. Consequently f (t) + t − (2u + v)
vanishes at both u, v and has the same forced third factor, proving the third row.
    A two-symbol four-cycle must have two occurrences of each letter. Indeed a word (u, u, u, v)
would force both f (u) = 2u and f (u) = u + v, and the analogous assertion holds when the other
symbol is repeated three times. With two occurrences of each, the alternating word has period two.
The remaining word is (u, u, v, v) up to rotation, and reversal is already a rotation of this word. Its
equations are f (u) = f (v) = u + v. Factoring f (t) − (u + v) proves the fifth row.

6.2   Three coordinate values and an integer interpolation lemma
If a three-cycle uses distinct symbols u < v < w, the neighbors of each are the other two. With
S = u + v + w, the monic polynomial f (t) + t − S vanishes at all three symbols. It is therefore
exactly (t − u)(t − v)(t − w). Both words (u, v, w) and (u, w, v) satisfy the recurrence, and no
rotation identifies them. This proves the fourth row, including b = −S.

Lemma 6.1 (Integral reciprocal sum). If p, q are distinct nonzero integers and 1/p + 1/q is an
integer, then p + q = 0.

Proof. For opposite signs the absolute value of the sum is strictly less than one. If it is an integer
it must be zero. For the same sign, change both signs if necessary and arrange 0 < p < q. When
p = 1, the sum is strictly between one and two. When p ≥ 2, it is strictly between zero and one.
Neither alternative is integral.

    Consider a three-symbol four-cycle. The repeated symbol cannot occur adjacently: in (h, h, u, v),
the two equations for h would give h + v = h + u, contrary to u ̸= v. The word is therefore (h, u, h, v),
with three distinct integers, and its conditions are

                                f (h) = u + v,      f (u) = f (v) = 2h.

Let Q(t) be the quadratic interpolant at h, u, v. Since the difference between the monic cubic and
this interpolant is
                               f (t) − Q(t) = (t − h)(t − u)(t − v),
the quadratic coefficient of Q is an integer. Subtracting the constant 2h in its interpolation data

6.3    The three-symbol six-cycle
First suppose that a three-symbol six-cycle has no equal adjacent letters. Its six distinct ordered
pairs must be all six off-diagonal pairs on the alphabet. Hence each symbol occurs twice, with the
other two symbols each occurring once as predecessor and once as successor. At a symbol u, equality
of the two neighbor sums has two possible pairings of predecessors and successors. Pairing each
predecessor with itself would require 2v = 2w for the other distinct symbols, which is impossible
over Z. Both occurrences must therefore have the unordered neighbor pair {v, w}. At every step,
after two distinct symbols the next symbol is the third one. The word repeats the same order of
three symbols and has period three, a contradiction.
    There must be adjacent equal letters p, p. The equations at the two occurrences force the
predecessor and successor of this pair to be the same symbol, say h. They cannot be p: a triple
p, p, p gives f (p) = 2p and forces its neighbors successively to equal p, making the entire cycle
constant. Rotate the word to (h, p, p, h, ∗, ∗) with h ̸= p. The third symbol q must occur in one or
both of the remaining positions. The possibilities involving q are

                                   (p, q), (q, p), (h, q), (q, h), (q, q).

In the first two, comparing the additional occurrence of p with an occurrence in the double forces
q = p. In the next two, the equations at the additional h force q = h. These contradict the
distinctness of the third symbol. The last two positions are therefore (q, q), giving the word

                                              (p, p, h, q, q, h).

Its recurrence equations are

                          f (p) = p + h,       f (q) = q + h,       f (h) = p + q.

Apply interpolation to the monic cubic f (t) − (t + h). It has values zero at p, q and value p + q − 2h
at h. As in the four-cycle argument, the quadratic coefficient of its quadratic interpolant is an
integer and equals
                                   p + q − 2h          1        1
                                                  =         +       .
                                 (h − p)(h − q)      p−h q−h
Lemma 6.1 forces p + q = 2h. Writing the endpoints as h − k, h + k now gives

                                  f (t) = t + h + (t − h)((t − h)2 − k 2 ),

and the displayed period-six word in Table 1. Swapping endpoints rotates this word by three places.

8    Reproducibility and limitations
Theorem 1.1 closes the full monic integral conservative cubic question in ordinary rational dynamics.
The integer third secant root controls every coexisting cycle in one common normalized graph. The
finite complement then singles out one normalized eleven-point graph, and short-word interpolation
translates the graph restrictions into exact coefficient identities.
    The finite certificate is an explicit integer computation after a proved reduction, not a proof-
assistant formalization. The retained producer evaluates the ninety symbolic graphs and the 9,373
bounded small-diameter tuples using path traversal. Before manuscript drafting, a separate internal
implementation reconstructed the small computation on the full square {0, . . . , D}2 , without the
secant-symbol filter, and isolated the periodic vertices by iterating a partial injection with repeated
squaring. Its large-graph arithmetic represented affine polynomials by their values at zero and
one; equality of these two values is exact because the expressions were already proved affine. That
independent reconstruction agreed with all table entries and the unique maximizing tuple. The
present typeset specification is complete without either implementation; Appendix A states the
calculation and its correctness argument. This internal verification is not described as human peer
review.
    Neither the threshold sixteen nor the number of finite cases is claimed to be minimal. Those
choices give a short no-cancellation proof and an explicitly bounded complement. A purely hand-
classified replacement for the small graph table could change the form of the proof, but is not
assumed here. The source comparisons remain bounded by the accessed primary records and the
named repository predecessor.
    The restrictions on coefficients and determinant are essential to the stated theorem. No extension
to all rational-coefficient cubics, other degrees, number fields or positive characteristic is proved.
Finally, the finite ordinary zeta is a source-dynamical invariant. It does not establish rational-prime
primitive ownership, target Euler factors, root numbers, automorphy, a target zero or divisor
correspondence, or a Hilbert–Pólya operator.

A     Complete finite-graph specification
This appendix gives the full finite arithmetic specification for Propositions 4.3 and 5.1. The
pseudocode uses exact integers, finite sets, dictionaries and lexicographic order. Its half-open
range(a,b) contains a, a + 1, . . . , b − 1; product is Cartesian product. There is no imposed
orbit-length bound. A word is canonicalized by rotation only.

A.1    Cycle extraction and its correctness
For an ordered finite symbol set S and values V [y], the graph has the edge (x, y) 7→ (y, V [y] − x)
when the last coordinate belongs to S. The symbols and values are either integers or pairs of integer
coefficients. Subtraction is ordinary subtraction in the first case and componentwise subtraction in
the second.

from itertools import product

def minus(u, v):
    if isinstance(u, tuple):
        return (u[0] - v[0], u[1] - v[1])
    return u - v

def cycles(S, V):
    S = tuple(sorted(S))
    allowed = set(S)
    seen = set()
    answer = []
    for start in product(S, repeat=2):
        if start in seen:
            continue
        point = start
        path = []
        position = {}
        while point not in seen:
            if point in position:
                orbit = path[position[point]:]
                word = tuple(x for (x, y) in orbit)
                word = min(word[i:] + word[:i]
                           for i in range(len(word)))
                answer.append(word)
                break
            position[point] = len(path)
            path.append(point)
            x, y = point
            z = minus(V[y], x)
            if z not in allowed:
                break
            point = (y, z)
        seen.update(path)
    return sorted(answer, key=lambda w: (len(w), w))

Lemma A.1 (Exact extraction). The procedure cycles terminates and returns every directed cycle
of the partial graph once, and no other words. Each returned word has length equal to its least
ordinary period.
Proof. The graph has finitely many vertices. Within one traversal, either an image leaves the graph,
the path reaches an already completed traversal, or a vertex repeats on the current path; one of

                                                 14
these events occurs after finitely many steps. A repeat on the current path closes exactly its segment
from the first occurrence to the repeat. Its vertices are distinct before closure, so it is one whole
cycle with its least period.
    Consider any cycle and the first traversal that reaches it. No vertex of that cycle is yet in a
completed traversal. Following the deterministic edges therefore closes and records it. A subsequent
path reaching that cycle stops at a completed vertex and cannot record it again. Distinct cycles
cannot intersect in a functional graph. Marking every traversed vertex completed thus loses no
unrecorded cycle and creates no duplicate. The outer loop visits every vertex, so every cycle
is eventually reached. Canonical rotation changes only its chosen starting coordinate, not its
orientation or its length.

A.2    Small-diameter enumeration
The following code computes the input counts, the endpoint-filtered point maxima, period sets,
per-cycle symbol maxima, and every global maximizer. The value eleven is not supplied as an input
or assertion.

def small_certificate():
    rows = []
    global_maximum = -1
    equality = []
    all_periods = set()
    for D in range(2, 16):
        cases = kept = 0
        maximum = 0
        max_symbols = 0
        periods = set()
        for r, A, q in product(range(-3, D + 4),
                                range(2 * D + 1),
                                range(-2, 3)):
            if not (0 <= A + q * D <= 2 * D):
                continue
            cases += 1
            S = [t for t in range(D + 1)
                  if abs(t * (t-D) * (t-r)) <= 2 * D]
            V = {t: A + q*t + t*(t-D)*(t-r) for t in S}
            C = cycles(S, V)
            used = {t for w in C for t in w}
            if not (0 in used and D in used):
                continue
            kept += 1
            count = sum(len(w) for w in C)
            maximum = max(maximum, count)
            for w in C:
                periods.add(len(w))
                all_periods.add(len(w))
                max_symbols = max(max_symbols, len(set(w)))
            if count > global_maximum:
                global_maximum = count
                equality = []
            if count == global_maximum:
                equality.append((D, r, A, q, C))
        rows.append((D, cases, kept, maximum,
                      sorted(periods), max_symbols))
    return rows, global_maximum, equality, sorted(all_periods)

A.3    Symbolic large-diameter enumeration
Represent kD + s by (k, s). The symbol D is (1, 0) and an integer symbol t ∈ Tr is (0, t). Formula
(12) defines its image coefficients. The extra statistic error measures the maximum absolute
constant discrepancy among all edge tests. This statistic checks the arithmetic bound, but the
independent proof of uniformity remains Lemma 4.2.

def symbolic_certificate():
    rows = []
    endpoint_patterns = []
    for r in (-1, 0, 1, 2, 3):
        T = [t for t in range(4)
             if t == 0 or abs(t * (t-r)) <= 2]
        S = [(0, t) for t in T] + [(1, 0)]
        Sigma = {(u[0] + v[0], u[1] + v[1])
                 for u, v in product(S, repeat=2)}
        cases = kept = maximum = max_symbols = error = 0
        periods = set()
        for (k, s), q in product(sorted(Sigma), range(-2, 3)):
            if (k + q, s) not in Sigma:
                continue
            cases += 1
            V = {(0, t): (k - t*(t-r),
                           s + q*t + t*t*(t-r)) for t in T}
            V[(1, 0)] = (k + q, s)
            for y, u, v in product(S, repeat=3):
                error = max(error, abs(V[y][1]-u[1]-v[1]))
            C = cycles(S, V)
            count = sum(len(w) for w in C)
            maximum = max(maximum, count)
            for w in C:
                periods.add(len(w))
                max_symbols = max(max_symbols, len(set(w)))
            used = {t for w in C for t in w}
            if (0, 0) in used and (1, 0) in used:
                kept += 1
                endpoint_patterns.append((r, (k, s), q, C))
        rows.append((r, T, cases, kept, maximum,
                     sorted(periods), max_symbols, error))
    return rows, endpoint_patterns
