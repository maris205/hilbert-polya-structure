Canonical-height distributions for constant-coefficient Hénon maps


                                            September 7, 2026


                                                  Abstract
          We determine the forward-plus-backward canonical-height distribution of every polynomial
      point for every constant-coefficient Hénon map H(x, y) = (y, f (y) − ax) over Fq , with deg f =
      d ≥ 2 and a ̸= 0. The distribution depends only on q and d, including in inseparable cases.
      A unique degree valley in each nonconstant orbit gives two disjoint classes of representatives
      with exact multiplicities. Summing their integer time shifts yields an explicit height Dirichlet
      series, absolutely convergent exactly for ℜs > 1 and meromorphic for ℜs > 0. We determine
      every pole after combining the sectors: all are simple except for a specified lattice of double
      poles on ℜs = 1. The surviving pole lattices accumulate at every point of the imaginary
      axis, which is a natural boundary even for meromorphic continuation. Direct counting gives
                                          ⌊B⌋+1
      #{P : b h(P ) ≤ B} = d−1
                             d+1 (q − 1)q       B + Oq,d (q B ) for arbitrary real B. The fractional-part
      oscillation is retained in the leading term. The domain is Fq [t]2 and the series counts heights,
      not periodic orbits.


1     Introduction
For a fixed nonperiodic orbit of a Hénon map, forward and backward canonical heights have opposite
geometric scaling. Counting all polynomial points requires an additional step: the different orbits
must be parametrized without overlap, with the correct arithmetic multiplicity. For constant
coefficients over a finite field, this step has an exact answer. Each nonconstant orbit has a unique
degree valley of one of two types. Their multiplicities do not depend on the coefficients of the map.
    We use this classification to determine the full two-sided canonical-height distribution on Fq [t]2 .
Its analytic structure is not captured by a single leading pole. The sectors coming from neighboring
time shifts meet at the same candidate poles, and their residues must be combined before any
natural-boundary conclusion is justified. We compute the combined residue explicitly and prove
that it never vanishes at the proposed simple poles.
    The resulting contribution has three parts.

    1. An exhaustive, disjoint edge/turn parametrization gives exact height multiplicities for every
       prime power, every degree at least two and every allowed constant coefficient.

    2. The associated series has absolute-convergence abscissa one, an explicit complete simple/double
       pole divisor in ℜs > 0, and a meromorphic natural boundary at ℜs = 0.

    3. A direct count gives the real-height asymptotic with its nonconstant fractional-part oscillation.
       Replacing the floor in the exponential main term by a real exponent would change the result.

Classical height inputs. Kawaguchi constructs local Green functions and global canonical
heights for regular polynomial automorphisms [3]. The height limits, functional equations and
two-sided orbit scaling are classical inputs. The global results there are formulated over number
fields, and the bounded-height orbit count in Corollary D concerns a given orbit and naive height.
It does not give the distribution over all polynomial points studied here. Our required specialization
is proved directly by degrees.
    Ingram develops canonical heights and non-Archimedean escape for Hénon maps [2]. The
introduction and Section 2 of arXiv:1111.3609v1, in particular Lemma 2.1, supply the nearby
framework used for comparison. The direct degree argument below handles arbitrary nonzero
leading coefficient without a separability assumption. We do not claim a new canonical-height
construction or a general Northcott theorem. The bibliography uses the verified journal issue
metadata, while the text comparison remains to the accessed arXiv version.

Height-zeta counting. Hsia’s published development of dynamical height zeta functions [1] is
a predecessor of the one-variable counting program. We use its bibliographic attribution, not an
unaccessed theorem: the original full article was not available for the present comparison. Takehira’s
account attributes the definition, via Hsia, to Silverman’s unpublished 1994 seminar talk; that talk
was also not accessed. Neither is a proof dependency here.
    Takehira gives explicit one-variable function-field height-zeta formulas and treats the effect of
several vertical-line poles on height counting [4]. Our comparison is to the introduction, Section 2.2,
Condition 3.1, Theorem 3.4 and the opening of Section 4 in arXiv:2404.00955v1. Its finite local
height-discrepancy partition does not apply to the present whole two-dimensional domain: for an
edge with both coordinate degrees m, the two-sided height is 2m and the naive height is m. The
discrepancy is therefore unbounded even with constant coefficients. The theorem below retains the
oscillation explicitly, without a single-pole Tauberian assumption. These source comparisons are
bounded by the stated text access and do not assert worldwide priority.
    Section 2 fixes the domain and states the distribution and analytic conclusions. Sections 3 and 4
prove the degree-valley census and sum the sectors. Section 5 combines their residues and proves
the boundary; Section 6 counts points directly.

3     Unique degree valleys and their multiplicities
Write the entire orbit as H j P = (xj , xj+1 ) and set bj = max(0, deg xj ). Then

                                   xj+1 + axj−1 = f (xj ),           j ∈ Z.                      (3.1)

If bj ≥ 1 and bj ≥ bj+1 , the right side has degree dbj > bj+1 , so bj−1 = dbj . Repetition forces
geometric growth in the backward direction. If bj ≤ bj+1 and bj+1 ≥ 1, the same comparison gives
bj+2 = dbj+1 and geometric forward growth. Two consecutive zeros would put the point in F2q ,
which is invariant under both maps in (2.1); the entire orbit would consist of constant points.
    Every nonconstant orbit therefore escapes in both directions. Indeed, if one direction has not yet
been covered by a nondecreasing adjacent pair in that orientation, failure to find such a pair would
give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers. A positive plateau instead
forces escape, and a zero plateau implies constant coordinates. The global minimum of (bj ) is
attained. Both-sided escape also excludes periodicity, so all integer iterates on a nonconstant orbit
are distinct.

Proposition 3.1 (Disjoint orbit representatives). Every nonconstant orbit contains exactly one
representative of one of the following two types, and never representatives of both types.

    1. An edge: (x, y) with m = deg x ≥ 1, n = deg y ≥ 1, m < dn and n < dm.

    2. A turn: (x, y) with M = deg x ≥ 1, d max(0, deg y) ≤ M and deg(f (y) − ax) = M .

Proof. At a strict minimum bj = n, let the neighboring degrees be b, c > n. For n ≥ 1 and b =     ̸ c,
the recurrence forces max(b, c) = dn. The pair formed from xj and its smaller neighbor has positive
degrees satisfying both strict edge inequalities. If b = c = M , cancellation in the recurrence forces
M ≥ dn for n ≥ 1. For n = 0 the same inequality holds and the equal-neighbor case is forced, since
unequal positive neighbors could not have a constant sum. The ordered pair (xj−1 , xj ) is then a
turn. If a minimum occurs at two consecutive equal degrees, those degrees are positive and the
adjacent pair is an edge. Three consecutive equal positive degrees contradict the escape comparison.
These cases exhaust the minima and establish existence.
    For uniqueness, an edge of degrees (m, n) has exterior degrees dm on the left and dn on the
right, and all farther degrees grow by d. No other adjacent pair satisfies the edge inequalities, and
no turn occurs. A turn has the pattern

                      . . . , d2 M, dM, M, n, M, dM, d2 M, . . . ,      0 ≤ n ≤ M/d.             (3.2)

The falling-to-rising orientation selects a unique ordered pair. The reversed adjacent pair is not
another turn, and the pattern has no edge. This proves disjointness and uniqueness.

7    Scope and reproducibility
The exact distribution follows from an all-orbit degree-valley census, not from the canonical-height
definition alone. Its coefficient uniformity includes arbitrary nonzero constant leading coefficients,
all lower coefficients, every nonzero determinant and inseparable polynomials. The natural boundary
follows only after the neighboring sectors’ residues have been combined and shown nonzero. The
point-count asymptotic retains the lattice dependence on a real height bound.
     The accompanying source package includes the exact diagnostic check_height_valleys.py
and its frozen execution receipt. It compared literal polynomial iteration with the independently
specified valley weights in eleven prime-field cases, for 77,974 polynomial pairs. Heights were exact
rational numbers, with escape certified by strict degree domination; all tested distributions agreed.
The test varies coefficients, degree and determinant but does not establish the prime-power or
infinite-parameter theorem. Those quantifiers, the combined residue and the analytic continuation
are proved in the article. The script was not rerun merely to produce this manuscript.
     Our domain restriction is substantive. The result does not count all rational-function points,
treat nonconstant coefficients, or assert a height distribution over an arbitrary constant field. In
particular, it does not classify the nonconstant-parameter periodic loci of a different Hénon family.
Source version and access limits are retained in the accompanying citation audit; an independent
internal review is not human journal peer review or a worldwide novelty certificate.
     Finally, ZH is a height Dirichlet series. Its pole divisor and mean counting exponent are not a
target zero-counting correspondence. No target Euler factors, root numbers, automorphy, arithmetic
divisor matching or Hilbert–Pólya realization follow from these source results.
