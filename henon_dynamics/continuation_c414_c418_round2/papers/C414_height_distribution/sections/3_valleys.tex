\section{Unique degree valleys and their multiplicities}\label{sec:valleys}
Write the entire orbit as $H^jP=(x_j,x_{j+1})$ and set
$b_j=\max(0,\deg x_j)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrence}
 x_{j+1}+a x_{j-1}=f(x_j),\qquad j\in\Z.
\end{equation}
If $b_j\ge1$ and $b_j\ge b_{j+1}$, the right side has degree
$db_j>b_{j+1}$, so $b_{j-1}=db_j$. Repetition forces geometric
growth in the backward direction. If $b_j\le b_{j+1}$ and
$b_{j+1}\ge1$, the same comparison gives $b_{j+2}=db_{j+1}$
and geometric forward growth. Two consecutive zeros would put the
point in $\F_q^2$, which is invariant under both maps in
\eqref{eq:map}; the entire orbit would consist of constant points.

Every nonconstant orbit therefore escapes in both directions.
Indeed, if one direction has not yet been covered by a nondecreasing
adjacent pair in that orientation, failure to find such a pair would
give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers.
A positive plateau instead forces escape, and a zero plateau implies
constant coordinates. The global minimum of $(b_j)$ is attained.
Both-sided escape also excludes periodicity, so all integer iterates
on a nonconstant orbit are distinct.

\begin{proposition}[Disjoint orbit representatives]\label{prop:valleys}
Every nonconstant orbit contains exactly one representative of one
of the following two types, and never representatives of both types.
\begin{enumerate}
\item An \emph{edge}: $(x,y)$ with $m=\deg x\ge1$,
$n=\deg y\ge1$, $m<dn$ and $n<dm$.
\item A \emph{turn}: $(x,y)$ with $M=\deg x\ge1$,
$d\max(0,\deg y)\le M$ and $\deg(f(y)-a x)=M$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At a strict minimum $b_j=n$, let the neighboring degrees be $b,c>n$.
For $n\ge1$ and $b\ne c$, the recurrence forces
$\max(b,c)=dn$. The pair formed from $x_j$ and its smaller
neighbor has positive degrees satisfying both strict edge
inequalities. If $b=c=M$, cancellation in the recurrence forces
$M\ge dn$ for $n\ge1$. For $n=0$ the same inequality holds
and the equal-neighbor case is forced, since unequal positive
neighbors could not have a constant sum. The ordered pair
$(x_{j-1},x_j)$ is then a turn. If a minimum occurs at two
consecutive equal degrees, those degrees are positive and the
adjacent pair is an edge. Three consecutive equal positive
degrees contradict the escape comparison. These cases exhaust
the minima and establish existence.

For uniqueness, an edge of degrees $(m,n)$ has exterior degrees
$dm$ on the left and $dn$ on the right, and all farther degrees
grow by $d$. No other adjacent pair satisfies the edge inequalities,
and no turn occurs. A turn has the pattern
\begin{equation}\label{eq:turn-pattern}
 \ldots,d^2M,dM,M,n,M,dM,d^2M,\ldots,
 \qquad 0\le n\le M/d.
\end{equation}
The falling-to-rising orientation selects a unique ordered pair.
The reversed adjacent pair is not another turn, and the pattern
has no edge. This proves disjointness and uniqueness.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Heights at all shifts]\label{lem:heights}
For an edge representative of degrees $(m,n)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edge-height}
 (\hh^-(P),\hh^+(P))=(m,n),\qquad
 \hh(H^kP)=d^{-k}m+d^kn.
\end{equation}
For a turn representative of degree $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:turn-height}
 (\hh^-(P),\hh^+(P))=(M,M/d),\qquad
 \hh(H^kP)=M(d^{-k}+d^{k-1}).
\end{equation}
Constants have height zero. For every polynomial point,
$h(P)\le\hh(P)$. An edge uniquely minimizes its total height
on its orbit; a turn has exactly two adjacent minima, at $k=0,1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The geometric degree patterns prove both limits directly.
For a turn, forward geometric growth begins one step later,
accounting for the factor $1/d$. Shifting either limit gives
the displayed formulas for every $k\in\Z$. In those patterns,
the largest coordinate degree away from the center is one of
the two positive height summands. At the center it is at most
their sum, proving $h\le\hh$. This also gives finiteness of
bounded-height sets, contained in the degree box
$h(P)\le\lfloor B\rfloor$.

For an edge, $1/d<n/m<d$. The first height difference in
each direction from $k=0$ is strictly positive, and subsequent
differences increase. For a turn the formula is invariant
under $k\mapsto1-k$ and increases away from $k=0,1$.
\end{proof}

There are $(q-1)q^m$ polynomials of degree $m\ge1$ and $q$
constants. Every pair in the open edge cone is admissible, so
its exact multiplicity is $(q-1)^2q^{m+n}$. To count turns
of degree $M$, first impose $d\deg y<M$. The second turn
condition is automatic and the number of pairs is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strict-turn-count}
 (q-1)q^M q^{\lfloor(M-1)/d\rfloor+1}.
\end{equation}
If $M=dr$ and $\deg y=r\ge1$, exactly one nonzero leading
coefficient of $x$ would cancel that of $f(y)$. For each $y$
there are therefore $(q-2)q^M$ choices of $x$, giving
$(q-1)(q-2)q^{(d+1)r}$ boundary pairs. At $q=2$ this
boundary portion is zero; the strict portion is still present.
Adding the two disjoint portions proves \eqref{eq:turn-weights}.

Only degrees and nonvanishing of the specified coefficients entered
these counts. They hold over every prime power, including when
$f$ is inseparable. Summing all shifts and the $q^2$ constant
points proves Theorem~\ref{thm:distribution}.
