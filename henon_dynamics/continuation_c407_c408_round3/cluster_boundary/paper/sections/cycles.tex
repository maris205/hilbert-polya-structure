\section{Cycle gradient colengths}\label{sec:cycles}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cycles}
For every $m\geq1$, the gradient ideal of the cycle potential $V_m$ has
finite colength $\mu(V_m)=c_m(k)$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The Hessian and period-four reduction}

The Hessian of the quadratic part $\sum_jz_jz_{j+1}$ acts by
\[
 (H_m z)_j=z_{j-1}+z_{j+1}.
\]
This also gives $H_1=[2]$ and the off-diagonal entries $2$ in $H_2$;
these are the loop and double-edge conventions of \cref{sec:main}.
The cyclic Fourier vectors have eigenvalues
$2\cos(2\pi j/m)$, $0\leq j<m$, so $H_m$ is invertible if and only
if $4\nmid m$. In these cases the full-gradient implicit-function theorem
gives colength one.

Let $m=4r$. The kernel is the two-dimensional space of repeated patterns
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclekernel}
 z=(a,b,-a,-b,a,b,-a,-b,\ldots).
\end{equation}
The two displayed kernel basis vectors are orthogonal, each with squared
length $2r$, so the Hessian is invertible on their orthogonal complement.
Let $p_m(a,b)$ be the potential after eliminating that complement.
Shift by four preserves the potential, the kernel point, and the chosen
complement. Uniqueness of formal elimination forces its solution to be
period four. On the period-four subspace $V_m=rV_4$, and the complementary
equations restrict to $r$ times the four-site equations. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:periodfour}
 p_m(a,b)=r p_4(a,b).
\end{equation}
Multiplication by the nonzero scalar $r$ does not change a gradient ideal
over $\C$.

Two reflections of the four-cycle induce $(a,b)\mapsto(-a,b)$ and
$(a,b)\mapsto(a,-b)$ on the kernel. They preserve the orthogonal
complement, so uniqueness again makes the effective potential invariant.
A one-step shift exchanges $a,b$ up to signs. We obtain the exact formal
description
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W}
 p_4(a,b)=W(A,B),\qquad A=a^2,\quad B=b^2,\quad W(A,B)=W(B,A).
\end{equation}
These are graph symmetries; no global sign symmetry of the odd-degree
unary potential is assumed.

We shall compute the mixed quartic coefficient and the first pure-axis
coefficient:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alphabeta}
 [a^2b^2]p_4=\alpha,\qquad
 p_4(a,0)=\beta a^{2k}+O(a^{2k+2}),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficientvalues}
 (\alpha,\beta)=
 \begin{cases}
  \bigl(1,(k^2-2)/4\bigr),&k\geq5\text{ odd},\\
  (-8,-5),&k=3.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In particular both coefficients are nonzero, and there is no earlier
pure-axis term.

\subsection{The mixed quartic coefficient}

On the kernel $(a,b,-a,-b)$, the four adjacent products alternate between
$ab$ and $-ab$. Their pair potential is
\[
 2\Li(ab)+2\Li(-ab)=\Li(a^2b^2),
\]
whose mixed quartic coefficient is one. For $k\geq5$, the unary potential
has degree at least five. Complementary corrections start in degree at
least three (the pair nonlinearity is cubic in the gradient), and so their
effect on the potential starts in degree at least six by
\cref{lem:eliminate}. They cannot change the quartic coefficient.

For $k=3$, the leading complementary gradient is the degree-two vector
\[
 g=3(a^2,b^2,a^2,b^2).
\]
It lies in the period-two complement of the kernel. On a complementary
vector $(c,d,c,d)$, the Hessian acts as $(2d,2c,2d,2c)$. The leading
elimination correction is $-H_4^{-1}g$, and its quartic change of the
potential is obtained by substituting into the quadratic and linear terms:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quarticcorrection}
 -\tfrac12 g^TH_4^{-1}g=-9a^2b^2.
\end{equation}
Indeed $H_4^{-1}g=\tfrac32(b^2,a^2,b^2,a^2)$.
The restricted cubic unary term is zero by the opposite kernel pairs,
and all other terms of this expansion have degree above four.
Adding the pair coefficient yields $\alpha=1-9=-8$.

\subsection{The pure-axis coefficient}

On the axis $b=0$, the first complementary correction is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycleaxis}
 u_0=(0,c,0,c),\qquad c=-\frac{k}{2}a^{k-1}.
\end{equation}
The leading unary gradient is $k a^{k-1}$ at the two $a$-sites,
and the Hessian of $u_0$ contributes $2c$ there, so these terms cancel.
This vector is orthogonal to both kernel basis vectors. As in the path
calculation, the higher pair gradients have orders at least $k+1$ and
$2k-1$, while the corrected unary gradients have order $(k-1)^2$.
The inequalities \eqref{eq:residualinequalities} apply. Hence the
complementary residual has order at least $k+1$, and replacing $u_0$ by
the exact solution changes the potential only in degree at least $2k+2$.

At $(a,c,-a,c)$ the four pair terms sum to
\[
 2\Li(ac)+2\Li(-ac)=\Li(a^2c^2),
\]
with degree-$2k$ coefficient $k^2/4$. The opposite $a$-sites contribute
$-\tfrac12\Li(a^{2k})$, with coefficient $-1/2$.
The two $c$-sites have leading total unary term $2c^k$. This is of
degree above $2k$ for $k\geq5$, and equals $-(27/4)a^6$ for $k=3$.
Thus
\[
 \beta=\frac{k^2}4-\frac12\quad(k\geq5),\qquad
 \beta=\frac94-\frac12-\frac{27}4=-5\quad(k=3).
\]
Each paired expression has first degree $2k$, and the corrected unary
degree is at least $2k$. Together with the error estimate and separate
evenness, this proves \eqref{eq:alphabeta} including its pure-axis order.

\subsection{Four finite intersections}

In terms of $W$ in \eqref{eq:W}, the coefficients give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Waxes}
 \begin{aligned}
 W(A,0)&=\beta A^k+O(A^{k+1}),&
 W(0,B)&=\beta B^k+O(B^{k+1}),\\
 W(A,B)&=\alpha AB+\text{terms of total degree at least three}.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The absence of degree-two pure terms uses $k\geq3$.
The Jacobian of $(W_A,W_B)$ at zero is
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\alpha\\\alpha&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
which is invertible. Formal inversion implies
\[
 (W_A,W_B)=(A,B)\quad\text{in }\C[[A,B]].
\]
After substitution $A=a^2$, $B=b^2$, the ideal generated by these two
derivatives is therefore $(a^2,b^2)$ and has colength four.

The two effective gradient components are $2aW_A$ and $2bW_B$.
For two series in $\C[[a,b]]$ with finite common colength, write
$I(f,g)=\length\C[[a,b]]/(f,g)$. The four choices of factors have
finite intersections: the coordinate intersection has length one, the
two axis intersections have length $2k-2$ by \eqref{eq:Waxes}, and the
derivative intersection has length four as just shown. Applying
\cref{lem:additivity} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclelength}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mu(p_4)
 &=I(a,b)+I(a,W_B)+I(W_A,b)+I(W_A,W_B)\\
 &=1+(2k-2)+(2k-2)+4=4k+1.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\Cref{lem:eliminate} identifies this with $\mu(V_4)$, and
\eqref{eq:periodfour} gives the same value for every $m=4r$.
This proves \cref{prop:cycles} without a Newton nondegeneracy assumption.

\begin{proof}[Completion of the proof of \cref{thm:length}]
\Cref{prop:paths,prop:cycles} prove that every complete factor choice in
\eqref{eq:reductionlength} has finite colength and give its exact value.
\Cref{lem:additivity} therefore applies and gives
\eqref{eq:main_length}. The common local presentation and the phase
isomorphism are \cref{prop:potential}. Finally multiply the common
length by the support counts in \cref{lem:finite_support}.
\end{proof}
