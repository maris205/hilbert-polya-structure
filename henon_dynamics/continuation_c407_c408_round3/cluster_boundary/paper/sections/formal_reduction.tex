\section{Formal reduction and intersection additivity}\label{sec:formal}

All series in this section are formal series over $\C$. Write
\[
 \Li(s)=\sum_{q\geq1}\frac{s^q}{q^2}.
\]
We use this defining series only on elements of a maximal ideal; no
analytic continuation or global dilogarithm identity is required.

\begin{proposition}[Common local presentation]\label{prop:potential}
At any point \eqref{eq:alt_point}, the completed local algebra is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:localmodel}
 \C[[z_0,\ldots,z_{m-1}]]
 \big/(z_j\partial_j V_m:0\leq j<m),
 \qquad
 V_m(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Li(z_jz_{j+1})
       -\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Li(-z_j^k).
\end{equation}
The formula uses cyclic indices and counts every summand literally.
In particular its local embedding dimension is $m$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Set $z_j=x_{2j}$ and $b_j=x_{2j+1}$. The odd-index equations are
$b_j^k=z_jz_{j+1}-1$. Their derivatives $kb_j^{k-1}$ are units at the
chosen point, so the formal implicit-function theorem eliminates them as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bsolve}
 b_j=t_j(1-z_jz_{j+1})^{1/k}.
\end{equation}
Here the fractional power is its unique expansion with constant term one.
The remaining equations are $b_{j-1}b_j=1+z_j^k$. By
$t_{j-1}t_j=1$, their unit ratios have constant term one and logarithms
\[
 \frac1k\log(1-z_{j-1}z_j)
 +\frac1k\log(1-z_jz_{j+1})-\log(1+z_j^k).
\]
For a unit $U$ with constant term one, $\log U=(U-1)h(U-1)$ with
$h(0)=1$. Thus taking this logarithm replaces each equation by a unit
multiple, not merely an equation with the same zero set. After multiplication
by $k$, the remaining ideal has generators
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lj}
 L_j=\log(1-z_{j-1}z_j)+\log(1-z_jz_{j+1})
       -k\log(1+z_j^k).
\end{equation}
Direct differentiation of the defining series gives
$L_j=-z_j\partial_j V_m$. If $m=1$, differentiating the loop
$\Li(z_0^2)$ contributes twice; if $m=2$, the two copies of
$\Li(z_0z_1)$ each contribute. These are exactly the duplicated neighbor
terms in \eqref{eq:Lj}.

The presentation is independent of every $t_j$. A one-step cyclic shift
identifies the other phase. Since the original local algebra is Artin by
\cref{lem:finite_support}, completion does not change it or its length.
Finally $V_m$ has no constant or linear term, so every
$z_j\partial_jV_m$ belongs to the square of the maximal ideal. There are
therefore no linear relations among the $m$ generators of its cotangent
space, which proves the embedding-dimension assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The ambient-scheme distinction]\label{rem:ambient}
Any closed subscheme of a smooth surface has local embedding dimension
at most two: its cotangent space is a quotient of the surface's cotangent
space. For $m\geq3$, \cref{prop:potential} consequently rules out an
identification of the local algebra here with the fixed-point local scheme
of an endomorphism of a smooth cluster surface. The smoothness statement
for the relevant rank-two cluster surface is an existing result
\cite[Theorem~5.2.3 in arXiv v2]{BFMS}; it is not a conclusion about
\eqref{eq:algebra}. The support's cluster interpretation does not identify
these nonreduced structures.
\end{remark}

The next lemma supplies the particular intersection-additivity fact that
we need, with no reducedness hypothesis.

\begin{lemma}[Splitting product generators]\label{lem:additivity}
Let $R=\C[[z_1,\ldots,z_d]]$, and let $a_i,b_i$ lie in its maximal ideal.
Suppose every ideal
generated by choosing one of $a_i,b_i$ for each $i$ has finite colength.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additivity}
 \length R/(a_1b_1,\ldots,a_db_d)
 =\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}^d}
  \length R/(c_1^{\varepsilon_1},\ldots,c_d^{\varepsilon_d}),
 \quad c_i^0=a_i,\quad c_i^1=b_i.
\end{equation}
The symbols $c_i^0,c_i^1$ in this formula denote the two choices, not powers.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every intermediate ideal with some product generators and some chosen
factors also has finite colength. Indeed, a prime containing such an ideal
contains at least one factor from each remaining product, and hence contains
one of the assumed finite-colength ideals. It must therefore be the
maximal ideal.

Fix $d-1$ intermediate generators and let $Q$ be their quotient ring.
Adjoining the last product, or either of its factors $a,b$, gives a system
of parameters in the regular local ring $R$. A regular local ring is
Cohen--Macaulay, and any system of parameters is a regular sequence.
Thus $Q$ is a one-dimensional Cohen--Macaulay ring, and $a,b$ are parameters
and nonzerodivisors in $Q$. There is an exact sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exactlength}
 0\longrightarrow Q/(a)
 \xrightarrow{\ \times b\ } Q/(ab)
 \longrightarrow Q/(b)\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Injectivity follows by cancelling the nonzerodivisor $b$; the other
exactness statements follow from the quotient maps. Lengths in
\eqref{eq:exactlength} add. Splitting the product generators successively
proves \eqref{eq:additivity}. None of these rings is required to be reduced.
\end{proof}

For a formal potential $P$ with an isolated critical point at the origin,
write
\[
 \mu(P)=\dim_\C\C[[z]]/(\partial_1P,\ldots,\partial_dP)
\]
for its gradient colength. On a path of $L$ sites the restricted potential is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pathpotential}
 P_L(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\Li(z_jz_{j+1})
             -\sum_{j=1}^L\Li(-z_j^k).
\end{equation}
If $S\subsetneq C_m$, setting all unselected coordinates to zero splits
$V_m|_S$ as the sum of the path potentials on $\Runs(S)$.
Choosing $\partial_jV_m$ for $j\in S$ and $z_j$ for $j\notin S$
in \cref{lem:additivity} gives precisely this restricted gradient ideal.
The disjoint components involve disjoint sets of variables, so their finite
local algebras tensor over $\C$ and their lengths multiply. It follows,
provided the gradient colengths are finite, that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reductionlength}
 \ell_m(k)=\mu(V_m)
       +\sum_{S\subsetneq C_m}\prod_{R\in\Runs(S)}\mu(P_{|R|}).
\end{equation}
Finiteness and the exact values of these gradient colengths are established
in \cref{sec:paths,sec:cycles}, so this application will not assume what
it needs to prove.

We record also the elimination estimate used in both calculations. The
order of a vector-valued series is the least total degree of any nonzero
component; $O(a^s)$ means that every term has total degree at least $s$.

\begin{lemma}[Eliminating a nonsingular Hessian block]\label{lem:eliminate}
Let $P(a,u)$ be a formal series with $dP(0)=0$ whose quadratic part is
$\tfrac12u^THu$, with $H$ invertible. There is a unique series $u=u(a)$
vanishing at the origin that solves $\partial_uP=0$, and the full gradient
algebra is isomorphic to the gradient algebra of $p(a)=P(a,u(a))$.
If an approximation $u_0(a)$ vanishes at the origin and satisfies
$\partial_uP(a,u_0(a))=O(a^s)$ for $s\geq2$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyerror}
 u(a)-u_0(a)=O(a^s),\qquad
 P(a,u(a))-P(a,u_0(a))=O(a^{2s}).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The derivative of $\partial_uP$ with respect to $u$ at the origin is $H$.
Formal implicit elimination gives the unique $u(a)$, and quotienting by
the complementary equations is substitution of that solution. The chain
rule gives $\partial_ap=(\partial_aP)(a,u(a))$, since the complementary
gradient is zero there; hence the remaining ideals agree. Subtract the
complementary equations at $u_0$ and $u$ and compare their lowest degrees.
The invertible linear coefficient $H$ shows that their solution difference
has order at least $s$. Taylor expansion of $P$ at $u_0$ now has a linear
term of order at least $s+s$ and quadratic and higher difference terms of
order at least $2s$. This proves \eqref{eq:energyerror}.
\end{proof}
