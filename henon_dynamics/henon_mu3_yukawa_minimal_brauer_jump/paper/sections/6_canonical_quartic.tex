\section{The canonical determinant quartic}
\label{sec:canonical-quartic}

We next turn the twelve-line carrier into one canonical quartic.  Two rank
bounds have different origins.  Geometry produces a section and hence an
upper bound on the restriction rank; exact arithmetic supplies a nonzero
pivot and hence the lower bound.  Keeping both halves visible prevents a
finite-field minor from being mistaken for an existence proof.

\subsection{A proved quotient gauge}

The space \(H^0(\PP^3_{\FD},\mathcal O(4))\) has dimension \(35\).  Since
\(F\) is a nonzero cubic, multiplication by \(F\) embeds the four-dimensional
space of linear forms:
\begin{equation}
 F\cdot H^0(\PP^3_{\FD},\mathcal O(1))
 \subset H^0(\PP^3_{\FD},\mathcal O(4)).
\label{eq:F-linear-subspace}
\end{equation}
The quotient therefore has dimension \(31\).

Put
\[
 c=[u_0^3]F=75081586157.
\]
Using the source basis \((Fu_0,Fu_1,Fu_2,Fu_3)\) and projecting to the
coefficients of
\[
 u_0^4,\quad u_0^3u_1,\quad u_0^3u_2,\quad u_0^3u_3,
\]
gives the triangular block
\begin{equation}
 B_F=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 75081586157&0&0&0\\
 -28576620789&75081586157&0&0\\
 -122000922135&0&75081586157&0\\
 -5364921951&0&0&75081586157
 \end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:gauge-block}
\end{equation}
Its determinant is
\begin{equation}
 \det B_F=c^4
 =31778526453059635681033276764499400992765201\ne0.
\label{eq:gauge-determinant}
\end{equation}
Consequently the four coefficient functionals restrict isomorphically to
the subspace in \cref{eq:F-linear-subspace}.  Every class modulo \(F\) times
a linear form has a unique representative in which those four coefficients
vanish.  This proves the gauge; deleting four monomials is not a convention
made after inspecting the answer.

Let
\[
 \cM=(m_0,m_1,\ldots,m_{30})
\]
be the remaining degree-four monomials in the locked descending
lexicographic order, with
\begin{equation}
 m_0=u_0^2u_1^2.
\label{eq:normalization-monomial}
\end{equation}
The complete order appears in \cref{app:quartic-specification}.

\subsection{Restriction matrix}

For each of the twelve carrier lines, restriction of
\[
 Q=\sum_{j=0}^{30}q_jm_j
\]
is a binary quartic and supplies five scalar equations.  We use the monic
carrier in the \(d\)-variable with the scaling
\(\alpha_i=c_gd_i\) and subtop normalization of
\cref{eq:alpha-scaling,eq:carrier-subtop}.  Reducing the five coefficients
against that degree-\(12\) carrier gives the exact matrix
\begin{equation}
 M_D\in\operatorname{Mat}_{60\times31}(\FD),\qquad M_Dq=0.
\label{eq:restriction-matrix}
\end{equation}
Rows are ordered first by binary degree \(0,\ldots,4\) and then by carrier
degree \(0,\ldots,11\).  The column order is \(\cM\).

\begin{proposition}[Geometric upper bound]
\label{prop:rank-upper}
The matrix \(M_D\) satisfies \(\rank_{\FD}M_D\le30\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the marking fixed in \cref{eq:marked-double-six}, the divisor classes of
the two sixers are
\[
 [\cE]=e_\Sigma,\qquad [\cG]=12h-5e_\Sigma.
\]
Hence
\begin{equation}
 \cE+\cG\sim12h-4e_\Sigma=4H_Y.
\label{eq:double-six-class}
\end{equation}
This geometric equivalence does not by itself provide an \(\FD\)-rational
section, so we descend it explicitly.

Put \(k=\FD\), and choose a \(k\)-rational hyperplane section
\(H_0=\divv_Y(\ell)\).  Both the unordered divisor \(\cE+\cG\) and \(4H_0\)
are \(k\)-rational.  Over \(\bar k\), choose
\(r\in\bar k(Y)^\times\) such that
\[
 \divv(r)=\cE+\cG-4H_0.
\]
For \(\sigma\in G_k\), the rational function
\(c_\sigma=\sigma(r)/r\) has zero divisor.  Since \(Y_{\bar k}\) is
projective and integral, \(c_\sigma\in\bar k^\times\).  These scalars form a
multiplicative cocycle.  Hilbert theorem 90 gives \(a\in\bar k^\times\) with
\[
 c_\sigma=\frac{\sigma(a)}{a}.
\]
It follows that \(r_0=r/a\) lies in \(k(Y)^\times\).  The section
\[
 s_D=r_0\ell^4\in H^0(Y_k,\mathcal O_Y(4))
\]
is defined over \(k\) and vanishes along \(\cE+\cG\).

The hypersurface restriction sequence
\begin{equation}
 0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{\PP^3_k}(1)
 \xrightarrow{\;\cdot F\;}\mathcal O_{\PP^3_k}(4)
 \longrightarrow\mathcal O_{Y_k}(4)\longrightarrow0
\label{eq:restriction-sequence}
\end{equation}
is surjective on global sections because
\(H^1(\PP^3_k,\mathcal O(1))=0\).  Thus \(s_D\) lifts to a nonzero ambient
quartic class over \(k\) that restricts to zero on the twelve lines.  After
applying the unique gauge, this class is a nonzero vector in \(\ker M_D\).
Therefore \(\rank M_D\le30\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Exact lower bound]
\label{prop:rank-lower}
The matrix \(M_D\) has rank at least \(30\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Delete the \(m_0\)-column and select the zero-based row set
\begin{equation}
 \cR=\{0,\ldots,10,12,\ldots,20,24,\ldots,29,36,37,38,48\}.
\label{eq:pivot-rows}
\end{equation}
The resulting matrix \(N_D\) is \(30\times30\).  Exact arithmetic at the
denominator-good prime \(7\) computes the norm of its determinant as
\[
 \Norm_{\FD/\Q}(\det N_D)\equiv3\pmod7.
\]
Thus \(\det N_D\ne0\) in \(\FD\).  The replay verifies the same nonvanishing
for all thirty-six conjugate configurations, and so
\(\rank M_D\ge30\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Determinant-defined quartic]
\label{thm:canonical-quartic}
For every double-six \(D\),
\[
 \rank_{\FD}M_D=30,\qquad \dim_{\FD}\ker M_D=1.
\]
There is a unique kernel vector
\[
 q_D=(1,q_1,\ldots,q_{30}),
\]
and hence a unique normalized gauge representative
\begin{equation}
 Q_D=\sum_{j=0}^{30}q_jm_j
\label{eq:QD-definition}
\end{equation}
whose restrictions to all twelve lines vanish.  Every \(q_j\) is defined by
Cramer's rule from the locked matrix \(N_D\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{prop:rank-upper,prop:rank-lower} give rank \(30\), hence a
one-dimensional kernel.  If a kernel vector had \(q_0=0\), its entries in
columns \(1,\ldots,30\) would lie in the kernel of the invertible selected
minor \(N_D\), so the vector would be zero.  Thus \(q_0\ne0\) on the kernel
line and can be uniquely normalized to one.  Solving the selected equations
by Cramer's rule gives \cref{eq:QD-definition}; the independent replay then
checks all \(60\) equations, not only the pivot rows, at all \(36\)
conjugates.
\end{proof}

the twelve carrier lines.  We have not yet promoted those restrictions to a
divisor equality; that is a separate geometric argument in
