\begin{abstract}
The orbit-resolved reflection Euler product of the full H\'enon horseshoe
admits a two-variable scalar-channel continuation.  We classify the complete
complex singular geometry on every positive weight fiber.  The $m$th channel
has coefficient
\[
 c_m=\frac1m\prod_{\substack{p\mid m\\p\ {\rm odd}}}(1-p)\ne0
\]
and poles
\[
 \alpha_{m,k}(q)=(1+q^{2m})^{-1/(2m)}e^{\pi i k/m},
 \qquad 0\le k<2m.
\]
For every $q>0$, the channel radii increase strictly to
$L(q)=\min(1,q^{-1})$.  Each listed point is an exponential essential
singularity of the Euler product, while the angular mesh becomes dense.
It follows that $|z|=L(q)$ is a natural boundary for the exact
unrenormalized punctured continuation.  For $q>1$ the limiting circle lies
strictly inside the unit disk.  The conclusion is deliberately
object-specific: an all-channel counterterm changes the function, and no
weighted Lind source, transfer operator, arithmetic trace, or Route-B claim
is obtained.
\end{abstract}

\section{From the first boundary to the full complex divisor}

The area-preserving H\'enon map on the frozen horseshoe is conjugate to the
full two-shift in the regime used throughout this programme
\cite{DevaneyNitecki1979}.  For the cross-axis observable
\(\chi=\mathbf 1\{s_{-1}=s_1\}\), the earlier orbit-resolved calculation
gives the odd reflection polynomial
\[
 F_{2r+1}(q)=2q(1+q^2)^r
\]
and the primitive dilation--M\"obius inversion
\[
 E_n(q)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)F_{n/d}(q^d).
\]
The associated product is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product}
 \Zorb(z,q)=
 \prod_{\substack{n\ge1\\n\ {\rm odd}}}
 \prod_{\omega\in A_n}
 (1-z^nq^{S_n\chi(\omega)})^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Its first positive convergence boundary is
\((1+q^2)^{-1/2}\), where it is essentially singular.

The point of the present paper is that the first boundary is only the first
member of a rigid complex divisor.  The weighted channel regrouping reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:channels}
 \log\Zorb(z,q)=\sum_{m\ge1}c_m\Psi_m(z,q),
 \qquad
 \Psi_m(z,q)=
 \frac{2(qz)^m}{1-(1+q^{2m})z^{2m}},
\end{equation}
initially in the disk of the Euler product and then on the complement of its
locally finite polar set.  Here
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cm}
 c_m=\frac1m\sum_{\substack{d\mid m\\d\ {\rm odd}}}d\mu(d)
 =\frac1m\prod_{\substack{p\mid m\\p\ {\rm odd}}}(1-p).
\end{equation}
In particular no channel disappears.

\section{Strict separation of channel radii}

For $m\ge1$ and $q>0$, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho}
 \rho_m(q)=(1+q^{2m})^{-1/(2m)}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Moving-radius ladder]\label{thm:radii}
For every fixed $q>0$,
\[
 \rho_1(q)<\rho_2(q)<\rho_3(q)<\cdots
\]
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit}
 \lim_{m\to\infty}\rho_m(q)
 =L(q):=\min(1,q^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Moreover
\(\rho_m(q)=q^{-1}\rho_m(q^{-1})\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $p>0$, let
\[
 N_p(q)=\|(1,q)\|_p=(1+q^p)^{1/p}.
\]
The finite-dimensional $\ell^p$ norm of a vector with two nonzero
coordinates is strictly decreasing in $p$.  Since
\(\rho_m(q)=N_{2m}(q)^{-1}\), strict increase follows.  The standard
$p\to\infty$ limit is \(N_p(q)\to\max(1,q)\), which proves
\eqref{eq:limit}.  Factoring $q^{2m}$ from \eqref{eq:rho} gives the final
identity.
\end{proof}

Strictness is important: singularities belonging to different channel
indices never collide on a positive-weight fiber.  This rules out
cross-channel cancellation before any local expansion is computed.

\section{Every complex channel root is essential}

The denominator in \eqref{eq:channels} has exactly the roots
\begin{equation}\label{eq:roots}
 \alpha_{m,k}(q)=\rho_m(q)e^{\pi i k/m},
 \qquad 0\le k<2m.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Exact local coefficient]\label{prop:local}
Fix $q>0$, $m\ge1$, and $0\le k<2m$.  With
\(v=1-z/\alpha_{m,k}(q)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal}
 \log\Zorb(z,q)=
 \frac{c_m(-1)^kq^m}{m\sqrt{1+q^{2m}}}
 \frac1v+G_{m,k,q}(z),
\end{equation}
where $G_{m,k,q}$ is holomorphic near $\alpha_{m,k}(q)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The root identity gives
\[
 \alpha_{m,k}(q)^m=
 \frac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt{1+q^{2m}}}.
\]
Writing $z=\alpha_{m,k}(q)(1-v)$, we obtain
\[
 1-(1+q^{2m})z^{2m}=2mv+O(v^2)
\]
and
\[
 2(qz)^m=
 \frac{2(-1)^kq^m}{\sqrt{1+q^{2m}}}+O(v).
\]
Their quotient is the principal term in \eqref{eq:principal}.  By
\cref{thm:radii}, every other channel has its poles on a different circle
and is therefore holomorphic locally.  Normal convergence of the remaining
tail supplies $G_{m,k,q}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Full essential divisor]\label{cor:essential}
Every point in \eqref{eq:roots} is an exponential essential singularity of
the continuation of \(\Zorb(\,\cdot\,,q)\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The coefficient in \eqref{eq:principal} is nonzero by \eqref{eq:cm}.
Exponentiating a nonzero simple pole gives an essential singularity.
\end{proof}

\section{The limiting circle is a natural boundary}

For fixed $m$, the arguments in \eqref{eq:roots} form the uniform grid
\[
 0,\frac{\pi}{m},\ldots,\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{m}.
\]
Its maximum gap is $\pi/m$.

\begin{theorem}[Natural-boundary circle]\label{thm:natural}
For every $q>0$, no point of the circle
\[
 |z|=L(q)=\min(1,q^{-1})
\]
has a neighborhood to which the explicit unrenormalized continuation
\(\Zorb(\,\cdot\,,q)\) extends meromorphically.  Thus this circle is a
natural boundary for that punctured continuation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(z_0=L(q)e^{i\theta}\).  Choose a grid angle
\(\pi k_m/m\) at distance at most \(\pi/(2m)\) from $\theta$.  By
\cref{thm:radii},
\[
 \alpha_{m,k_m}(q)
 =\rho_m(q)e^{\pi i k_m/m}\longrightarrow z_0.
\]
Every term of this sequence is an essential singularity by
\cref{cor:essential}, and the radii are distinct.  If a meromorphic
continuation existed on a neighborhood of $z_0$, that neighborhood would
contain infinitely many of these nonmeromorphic isolated singularities
accumulating at its interior point $z_0$.  This contradicts the local
structure of a meromorphic function \cite{Conway1978}.
\end{proof}

The theorem has three regimes.  When $0<q<1$, the natural boundary is the
unit circle.  At $q=1$, it is the unweighted boundary already approached by
the positive P72 ladder.  When $q>1$, it moves inward to $|z|=q^{-1}$.
The last behavior is not a numerical phase transition; it is the exact
$\ell^\infty$ limit in \cref{thm:radii}.

\section{Renormalization and operator firewalls}

The natural-boundary terminology in \cref{thm:natural} refers to analytic
continuation of the fixed function \eqref{eq:product} through the exact
channel continuation \eqref{eq:channels}.  It does not say that the
prescribed divisor cannot be subtracted.  An all-channel exponential
counterterm can cancel it precisely because the counterterm contains the
same complete ledger.  The resulting function is a different object.

Likewise, the theorem does not identify a weighted analogue of the
full reverse-shift Lind zeta of \citet{KimLeePark2003}.  No source formula
currently supplies such a comparison for $q\ne1$.  Nor is the channel
continuation itself a transfer determinant: a parameter-dependent diagonal
operator can be engineered after the fact, while the direct sum of
source-native orbit blocks has a separate compactness gate.  Those two
notions are compared in the next paper.

In particular, the channel index $m$ has no intrinsic rational-prime
meaning, and no prime-power amplitude, explicit formula, self-adjoint
operator, zeta-zero correspondence, or Route-B result follows.

\section{Executable certificate}

The main certificate reconstructs forty channels on each of the fibers
$q=1/2,1,2$, using high-precision decimal radii to preserve strictness near
the limiting circle.  It checks every complex root numerically, records the
principal coefficient and angular gap, locks nine P70/P72/P75 dependencies, and
rejects 24 claim mutations.  A separate program reconstructs 192 rows on
the fibers $q=0.4,1,2.5$ without importing the main module.  Eleven unit tests
pass in normal and optimized modes.  These finite checks certify the
implementation; \cref{thm:radii,thm:natural} prove the all-channel claims.
