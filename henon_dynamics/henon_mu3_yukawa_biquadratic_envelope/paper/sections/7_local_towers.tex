\section{Both retained \texorpdfstring{$3$}{3}-adic towers}
\label{sec:local}

The global calculation leaves two possible decomposition groups at $3$.
Rather than select one, we compute the complete relative tower in each case
and isolate the conclusion common to both.

\subsection{Double cosets and row conventions}

Fix a prime of $K$ above $3$, with decomposition group $D$, inertia group
$I$, and lower ramification filtration.  For $F=K^H$, primes of $F$ above
$3$ are indexed by double cosets
\begin{equation}
 D\backslash G/H.
 \label{eq:double-cosets}
\end{equation}
For a double-coset representative, intersections of its conjugate of $H$ with $D$, $I$, and
the lower filtration determine the local degree, ramification index, residue
degree, and different exponent.  We record an absolute factor by
$(n,e,f,d)$, where $n=ef$ is its local degree and $d$ is the exponent of its
different.  Over a fixed factor of $M$, a factor in one of
$\Fplus,\Fzero,\Fthree,L$ is recorded by $(g,e,f,d)$, where $g$ is the
number of relative factors over that fixed base prime and $e,f,d$ are their
common ramification index, residue degree, and different exponent.  A
multiplicity after a row counts base primes of $M$ with the same complete
relative pattern.

For every base prime and every relative field $E$, the rows satisfy
\begin{equation}
 g e f=[E:M].
 \label{eq:local-degree-check}
\end{equation}
Summing local different contributions recovers the exponent of $3$ in
\eqref{eq:relative-norms}.  These degree and different checks are performed
row by row, not only after taking global totals.

The difference between $22$ and $11$ base primes reflects the residue-degree
two contribution in the second decomposition group: the collected base rows
for \Tom{206} have residue degree $2$, whereas those for \Tom{140} have
residue degree $1$.  This changes the number of factors but not the relative
different exponents.

\begin{proposition}[Branch-independent relative ramification]
\label{prop:branch-independent}
For each of the two possibilities in \eqref{eq:d3-options}, every relative
extension $\Fplus/M,\Fzero/M,\Fthree/M,L/M$ is unramified outside primes
above $3$.  Above $3$, every ramified relative factor has $e=2$ and $d=1$.
Consequently all relative ramification is tame, and the relative
discriminant norms are those in \eqref{eq:relative-norms} in both branches.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Unramifiedness away from $3$ follows already from the relative discriminant
exponent differences in \cref{sec:global}.  At $3$, enumerate
\eqref{eq:double-cosets} separately for the exact embedded filtrations of
\Tom{140} and \Tom{206}.  The resulting collected rows are precisely
\cref{tab:tom140-relative,tab:tom206-relative}.  In each relative entry,
$g e f$ equals $2$ for each quadratic field and $4$ for $L$, as required by
\eqref{eq:local-degree-check}.  Multiplicity-weighted factor
counts give the middle column of \cref{tab:branch-comparison}; summing the
relative different contributions gives $(8,16,8,32)$ in each branch.

Inspection of all relative entries shows that every ramified entry is either
$(1,2,1,1)$ or $(2,2,1,1)$.  Since the residue characteristic is $3$ and
$2$ is prime to $3$, such ramification is tame; for a tame extension the
different exponent is $e-1=1$, agreeing with the table.  No other ramified
row occurs.
\end{proof}

The proposition is one-way.  We do not infer that the actual decomposition
group is $140$ or $206$, and a tuple $(n,e,f,d)$ does not classify an
individual high-degree local field up to isomorphism.  In particular, the
tables do not prove a converse local classification.  Their role is to show
that both retained global possibilities imply the same exact relative
ramification statement.
