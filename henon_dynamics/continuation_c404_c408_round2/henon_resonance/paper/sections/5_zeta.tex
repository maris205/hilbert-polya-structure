\section{The zeta product and all positive powers}\label{sec:zeta}

This section proves Corollary~\ref{cor:zeta} directly from the count.
No algebraic-group structure or abstract zeta dichotomy is required for
the proof. Set
\[
 A=\frac{m-1}{q-1},\qquad B=\frac{q-m}{q-1},\qquad
 \mathcal Z(u)=Z_S(u/q^2).
\]
Both $A$ and $B$ are positive and $A+B=1$. Formula \eqref{eq:count}
becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized}
 \frac{N_n}{q^{2n}}=A+Bq^{-p^{v_p(n)}}.
\end{equation}
Its right side lies between $A$ and $A+B/q=m/q$, so
$N_n^{1/n}\longrightarrow q^2$. In particular, the logarithmic series
defining $\mathcal Z$ converges absolutely on $|u|<1$.

\subsection{Divisibility decomposition and convergence}

For every positive integer $n$ the following sum is finite and telescopes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:telescoping}
 q^{-p^{v_p(n)}}
 =q^{-1}+\sum_{k\ge1}
   \bigl(q^{-p^k}-q^{-p^{k-1}}\bigr)\ind_{p^k\mid n}.
\end{equation}
For $|u|\le\rho<1$, the absolute sum needed to interchange $n$ and
$k$ is bounded by
\[
 \sum_{k\ge1}\bigl(q^{-p^{k-1}}-q^{-p^k}\bigr)
     \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\rho^n}{n}
 =q^{-1}\bigl[-\log(1-\rho)\bigr]<\infty.
\]
Using
\[
 \sum_{p^k\mid n}\frac{u^n}{n}
 =\frac1{p^k}\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{u^{p^k j}}j
 =-\frac1{p^k}\log(1-u^{p^k}),
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-product}
 \log\mathcal Z(u)
 =-\frac mq\log(1-u)+\sum_{k\ge1}e_k\log(1-u^{p^k}),
\end{equation}
with the coefficients $e_k$ in \eqref{eq:product-intro}.
Every logarithm denotes its analytic branch on the unit disk with
value zero at zero. The positive coefficients $e_k$ are summable.
Also, on $|u|\le\rho<1$,
\[
 |\log(1-u^{p^k})|
 \le\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{\rho^{p^k j}}j
 \le-\log(1-\rho).
\]
Thus the logarithmic sum converges locally uniformly. Exponentiating
gives the locally uniformly convergent product
\eqref{eq:product-intro}; it is analytic and nonzero on $|u|<1$ and
equals the original germ there.

\subsection{Fractional radial orders}

Let $\xi$ be a primitive $p^a$-th root of unity, where $a\ge1$.
For $k<a$, $1-\xi^{p^k}\ne0$, and also $1-\xi\ne0$. The associated
finitely many logarithmic moduli stay bounded along $u=\rho\xi$ as
$\rho\uparrow1$. For $k\ge a$, one has $\xi^{p^k}=1$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:domination}
 0\le\frac{\log(1-\rho^{p^k})}{\log(1-\rho)}\le1,
 \qquad
 \lim_{\rho\uparrow1}
 \frac{\log(1-\rho^{p^k})}{\log(1-\rho)}=1.
\end{equation}
The inequality follows from $1-\rho\le1-\rho^{p^k}<1$;
the limit follows from
$1-\rho^{p^k}=(1-\rho)(1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{p^k-1})$.
Taking real parts in \eqref{eq:log-product}, dividing by
$\log(1-\rho)$, and applying dominated convergence to the summable
weights $e_k$ yields the exact radial order
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radial-order}
 \lim_{\rho\uparrow1}
 \frac{\log|\mathcal Z(\rho\xi)|}{\log(1-\rho)}
 =\sigma_a:=\sum_{k\ge a}e_k.
\end{equation}
It satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail-bound}
 0<\sigma_a\le
 \frac{B}{p^a}\sum_{k\ge a}
       \bigl(q^{-p^{k-1}}-q^{-p^k}\bigr)
 =\frac{Bq^{-p^{a-1}}}{p^a}<1,
 \qquad \sigma_a\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}

If a nonzero meromorphic function is defined in a neighborhood of
$\xi$, it has the form $(u-\xi)^j h(u)$ there, where $j\in\mathbb Z$
and $h$ is holomorphic and nonzero at $\xi$. Its radial logarithmic
order is $j$, since $|\rho\xi-\xi|=1-\rho$ and
$\log|h(\rho\xi)|$ stays bounded. The strictly fractional order
$\sigma_a$ consequently excludes a meromorphic extension of
$\mathcal Z$ to a neighborhood of $\xi$.

Now fix any integer $M\ge1$. By \eqref{eq:tail-bound}, for every
sufficiently large $a$ one has $0<M\sigma_a<1$. The same argument
excludes a meromorphic extension of $\mathcal Z^M$ at every primitive
$p^a$-th root of unity for all such $a$. These roots are dense on the
unit circle: among the grid numerators modulo $p^a$, excluding the
multiples of $p$ leaves gaps of at most two, so the largest angular
gap tends to zero with $a$. An extension across any nonempty arc would
give an extension at one of these forbidden points. Hence the unit
circle is a natural boundary for meromorphic continuation of
$\mathcal Z^M$.

Returning to $t=u/q^2$ proves the asserted circle for $Z_S^M$.
Finally, an algebraic function over $\C(u)$ has only finitely many
possible finite branch points or poles: away from the zeros of the
leading coefficient and discriminant of a square-free defining
polynomial, its local branches are holomorphic. It cannot have a
natural boundary along the unit circle. Thus $Z_S$ is not algebraic,
and in particular is not rational. This proves
Corollary~\ref{cor:zeta}.
