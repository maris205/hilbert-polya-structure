\section{Primitive products and the complete divisor}\label{sec:products}

For fixed $0<a<1$, the denominators in~\eqref{eq:tau-main} are uniformly
separated from zero as $n$ varies: $1-a^n\ge1-a$ and
$1-q^n\ge1-|q|$. Thus $\tau_n$ is bounded. At criticality it is bounded by
$1/2$, and above criticality it decays exponentially. The logarithmic series
in~\eqref{eq:definition} therefore converges absolutely on $|u|<1$.

For a finite-real primitive orbit $\gamma$ with least period $p_\gamma$, let
$\Lambda_\gamma=(T^{p_\gamma})'(x)>1$ at any point on it. Its $s$-fold
repetition has multiplier $\Lambda_\gamma^s$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:geometric-weight}
 \frac1{\Lambda_\gamma^s-1}=\sum_{j\ge1}\Lambda_\gamma^{-js}.
\end{equation}
The sum obtained after replacing $u$ by $|u|$ is finite. Grouping first by
primitive orbits and then by $j$ is therefore justified and gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:primitive}
 D_a(u)=\prod_{\gamma\text{ primitive}}\prod_{j\ge1}
 \left(1-\frac{u^{p_\gamma}}{\Lambda_\gamma^j}\right),\qquad |u|<1.
\end{equation}
This includes only finite-real primitive orbits. We do not assert global
convergence of the primitive product.

Expand the weights~\eqref{eq:tau-main} into geometric series and use
$-\sum_{n\ge1}v^n/n=\log(1-v)$ on the disk. Absolute convergence permits
interchanging the sums and proves~\eqref{eq:products-main}. Here
$\Li(u)=\sum_{n\ge1}u^n/n^2$ and the square-root branch is normalized to one
at zero. Each separate product with geometric bases $q^j$, $a^k$, or $a^{-k}$
converges locally uniformly on $\C$, since the bases are absolutely summable.
Away from the listed zeros its tail is nonzero by absolute convergence of the
tail logarithms. Hence the subcritical quotient is meromorphic on $\C$, while
the supercritical product is entire with simple zeros $u=a^k$, $k\ge1$.

\begin{proposition}[All subcritical cancellations]\label{prop:divisor}
Let $0<a<1$. The point $u=1$ is always a simple zero. If $q\ne0$, the
numerator has double zeros at $q^{-j}$, $j\ge1$; the denominator has simple
zeros at $a^{-k}$, $k\ge1$. A denominator zero not coinciding with a numerator
zero is a simple pole. A coincidence is a simple zero, and every remaining
$q^{-j}$ is a double zero. The coincidence equation is exactly $a^k=q^j$.

If $q=0$ there are no coincidences. If $q>0$ and
$\log a/\log q=M/N$ in lowest positive terms, coincidences are exactly
$k=N\ell$, $j=M\ell$, $\ell\ge1$; for an irrational ratio there are none.
If $q<0$ and $\log a/\log(q^2)=M/N$ in lowest positive terms, coincidences
are exactly $k=N\ell$, $j=2M\ell$; for an irrational ratio there are none.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
All zeros within each geometric sequence are distinct, and none equals one.
Thus the orders follow directly from~\eqref{eq:products-main}, with no hidden
zeros in the product tails. For $q>0$ the equation is equivalent to
$k\log a=j\log q$. For $q<0$ it first forces $j$ even and then reduces to
the same rational commensurability statement for $q^2$. This proves the full
classification.
\end{proof}

To finish the entire classification in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, first take
$q\ge0$. If $q=0$, no denominator point cancels. If $q>0$, then $q<a<1$, so
no $q^j$ equals $a$: the first denominator zero cannot cancel. If $q<0$,
entireness likewise requires cancellation of that first point, which means
$a=q^{2m}=(1-2a)^{2m}$ for some $m\ge1$. Conversely this equality makes
every denominator factor equal to the numerator factor with index $2mk$, so
all poles cancel. For each fixed $m$, the function $(1-2a)^{2m}-a$ is
continuous and strictly decreasing on $(0,1/2)$, with endpoint limits $1$ and
$-1/2$. It has exactly one root $a_m$. The roots are distinct, since two
distinct powers of a number in $(0,1)$ cannot agree, and $a_1=1/4$.

At such a root the surviving entire product is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resonant}
 D_{a_m}(u)=(1-u)\prod_{j\ge1}
 (1-q^ju)^{2-\boldsymbol{1}_{\{2m\mid j\}}}.
\end{equation}
An abstract self-adjoint trace-class operator realizes this product: take a
real diagonal operator with entry $1$ once and $q^j$ with the displayed
multiplicity. Its absolute trace sum is at most $1+2|q|/(1-|q|)$. Similarly,
the diagonal entries $a^{-k}$ realize the supercritical entire product. These
are constructions from the already known factors, not identifications with
a natural dynamical transfer operator.
